Add Text to Images: Complete Guide

Learn how to add text to images online: captions, titles, memes, quotes, and annotations with fonts, colours, outlines, and layered positioning.

Text turns a picture into a message. A caption explains what the photo shows, a title gives it a name, and a quote gives it a voice.

Adding text to an image is a simple skill with many uses: social posts, memes, presentations, product labels, and annotated screenshots all rely on it.

This guide explains how to add text well. It covers fonts, size, colour, contrast, outlines, positioning, and the mistakes that make text unreadable.

What Text on an Image Does

Text adds meaning that the picture alone cannot carry. A photo of a mountain is just a mountain, but the same photo with a caption becomes a memory, a joke, or an invitation.

Text also adds context. A date, a place, or a name written on the image tells the viewer what they are looking at without needing a separate explanation.

For businesses, text on an image is a label. A product photo with a price, a sale banner with a discount, or a poster with an event date all use text to sell.

The key is restraint. A few well-placed words strengthen an image, while a paragraph crammed onto a photo hides the very thing it was meant to describe.

Captions, Titles, and Watermarks Are Different Jobs

A caption explains the image. It sits near the subject, it is easy to read, and it is meant to be seen by everyone who views the photo.

A title names the image. It is usually larger and bolder than a caption, and it sets the tone for everything else on the page or the post.

A watermark protects the image. It identifies the owner, it is often semi-transparent, and it is meant to survive copying and reposting.

Do not confuse the three. A caption that looks like a watermark will be ignored, and a watermark that looks like a caption will not protect anything.

Fonts: Choosing a Typeface That Fits

The font sets the mood of the text before anyone reads the words. A bold sans-serif feels modern and direct, while a serif feels classic and formal.

Choose a font that matches the image. A playful meme needs a bold, chunky font, while a wedding photo needs something elegant and light.

Limit yourself to one or two fonts per image. Mixing many typefaces looks chaotic, while a single consistent font keeps the design clean and professional.

Check the font at the size you will use it. A decorative font that looks beautiful at large sizes can become unreadable when it is small.

Text Size: Small Photos vs Large Photos

Text size is set as a percentage of the image, from 2 percent to 22 percent. That means the same setting looks consistent on a small thumbnail and a huge camera photo.

A size that looks right on a large photo may be invisible on a small one if it is set in pixels. Percentage sizing avoids that problem entirely.

For a headline, use a larger size near the top of the range. For a caption or a credit line, use a smaller size that stays readable without dominating the image.

Check the text on the actual size you will share. A headline that looks bold at full size may be tiny in a social feed preview, and a caption sized for a thumbnail may dominate a large print.

Colour and Contrast: Making Text Readable

White text works on dark backgrounds and black text works on light ones, but most photos contain both. A single colour will always disappear somewhere in the image.

Contrast is what makes text readable, not colour alone. A bright colour on a bright background fails, while a dark colour on a dark background fails just as badly.

Place text over the plainest part of the photo. A caption over a clear sky or a blank wall is far easier to read than one over a busy crowd or a forest.

When no plain area exists, add a background panel. A semi-transparent dark box behind white text makes it readable anywhere, though it covers more of the image.

Outlines and Drop Shadows

An outline is a border drawn around each letter. A thin dark outline around white text keeps it readable on a bright sky, which is why memes use it.

A drop shadow lifts the text off the background. It adds a soft dark edge behind the letters, which helps them stand out without the hard look of an outline.

Use one or the other, not both at full strength. An outline plus a heavy shadow makes the text look cluttered, while a single effect keeps it clean.

For classic meme text, use white letters with a thick dark outline. The combination is readable on almost any background, which is why it became the standard.

Positioning and Layers

Text layers can be moved anywhere on the image. Drag a layer to place it, and the tool lets you select each layer separately when you have several.

Top and bottom are the classic positions for captions. A headline at the top and a punchline at the bottom is the meme format, and it works because the eye reads top to bottom.

Keep text away from the edges. A caption that touches the border looks cramped, and platforms often crop the edges again for different screen sizes.

When you have several layers, select the one you want to edit before changing it. Editing the wrong layer is a common mistake, and it is easy to avoid by checking which layer is active.

Rotating Text for Effect

Text can be tilted at custom angles, which adds energy to a design. A slightly diagonal headline feels dynamic, while straight text feels calm and formal.

Use rotation sparingly. A small tilt of a few degrees adds character, while a steep angle makes the text hard to read and looks like a mistake.

Diagonal text works well on posters and social graphics, where the design is meant to catch the eye. It works poorly on instructions and labels, where clarity matters most.

Check rotated text at the size it will be viewed. A tilt that looks fine at full size can make small text unreadable in a feed preview.

Multi-Line Captions

Pressing Enter inside the text content creates a new line. This lets you write a caption that spans two or three lines without creating a separate layer for each one.

Keep lines short. A caption that runs across the whole width of the image is hard to read, while short lines centred on the subject are easy to follow.

Break lines at natural pauses in the sentence. A line break in the middle of a phrase feels awkward, while a break between phrases feels deliberate.

For a meme, use one layer for the top caption and one for the bottom. Separate layers let you position each line exactly where it belongs.

Making Memes

A meme is a photo with a short, funny caption, usually in white text with a thick dark outline at the top and the bottom. The format is simple, and that simplicity is the point.

Keep the text short. A meme with a paragraph of text is not funny, because nobody reads a paragraph on a joke image. One line at the top and one at the bottom is enough.

Use a bold, chunky font. The classic meme look uses a heavy sans-serif that stays readable at small sizes and on busy backgrounds.

Place the top line first and the bottom line second. The eye reads the setup at the top, looks at the image, and then reads the punchline at the bottom.

Quotes, Titles, and Social Graphics

A quote on a photo is a popular social format. The text is usually large, centred, and set over a plain area of the image or a darkened background.

Keep quotes short. A single sentence works far better than a paragraph, because the image and the words share the space and neither should dominate.

For titles, use a larger size and a bolder font than for captions. The title should be the first thing the eye finds, and the image should support it, not compete with it.

Darken the image slightly behind the text when the photo is busy. A semi-transparent overlay makes white text readable without hiding the photo completely.

Annotations and Labels

Annotations explain parts of an image. A label with an arrow, a numbered step, or a short note turns a screenshot into instructions.

Keep annotations short and place them near the thing they describe. A label far from its subject confuses the viewer, while a label right next to it is obvious.

Use a consistent style for all annotations on one image. The same font, size, and colour for every label makes the image look professional and easy to follow.

For screenshots, annotate with the Add Text to Image tool after cropping. A tight crop plus a few clear labels is far more useful than a full screenshot with no guidance.

Formats and Export

The tool accepts common image formats, including JPG, PNG, WebP, and HEIC, with files up to 20 MB. You can export as JPG, PNG, or WebP depending on your needs.

Exporting as PNG or WebP preserves transparency in the base image. If your source is a transparent PNG sticker, the text export keeps the see-through areas intact.

For photos with text, JPG at a high quality setting works well. Text edges can soften slightly under JPG compression, so keep the quality high for small captions.

The quality slider appears for JPG and WebP exports and hides for PNG, because PNG is lossless and has no quality setting to adjust.

Privacy: Adding Text Without Uploading

All text editing happens in your browser. Your photos are never uploaded to a server, which matters when the images are personal, family photos, or work screenshots.

This is a real advantage over cloud editors. A family photo with a caption should not travel to a server you cannot see, just to add a few words.

The tool also strips EXIF GPS data on export. A captioned photo downloaded from the tool carries no record of where it was taken.

Keep the original photo without text. Once text is flattened into an export, it cannot be removed from that file, so the original is your only clean copy.

Common Mistakes When Adding Text

The most common mistake is text that is too small. A caption that looks fine at full size can be unreadable in a social feed, where the image is shown much smaller.

Another mistake is poor contrast. White text on a bright sky and black text on a dark background both vanish, so check the text on the actual photo, not on a blank canvas.

People also write too much. A paragraph of text on a photo hides the image and overwhelms the viewer, while a few well-chosen words do the job better.

Finally, do not place text where the platform will crop it. Social apps trim the edges of images for different screens, so keep important words well inside the frame.

Text on Product Photos

Product photos often carry short text: a price, a discount, or a feature. The text should be large, bold, and placed where it does not cover the product itself.

Put the price or the offer in a corner, not across the middle. A corner label leaves the product visible, while a central label hides the very thing you are selling.

Use a consistent style across your whole catalogue. The same font, size, and colour for every price label makes the shop look professional and trustworthy.

Keep the text short. A price and a single word, such as Sale or New, is enough. A paragraph on a product photo distracts the buyer from the product.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best font for text on a photo?

A bold sans-serif is the safest choice for most photos, since it stays readable at small sizes and on busy backgrounds. Serif fonts suit formal images such as wedding photos, while decorative fonts work only at large sizes. Limit yourself to one or two fonts per image.

How do I make white text readable on a bright photo?

Add a dark outline or a drop shadow around the letters, which keeps them visible on bright areas. Placing the text over the darkest part of the photo also helps. A semi-transparent dark panel behind the text works when no plain area exists.

Can I add more than one line of text to an image?

Yes. Press Enter inside the text content to create a new line in the same layer, or add a separate layer for each line. Separate layers let you position a top caption and a bottom caption independently, which is the classic meme layout.

What is the difference between adding text and watermarking?

Adding text creates a caption, title, or annotation that is meant to be read by everyone. Watermarking adds a semi-transparent ownership mark that is meant to survive copying. Use the text tool for messages and the watermark tool for protection.

How big should text be on an image for social media?

Large enough to read in a small feed preview, not just at full size. Headlines need a size near the top of the 2 to 22 percent range, while captions can be smaller. Test the result by viewing the image at the size it will appear in the feed.

Does adding text to a photo remove its location data?

Yes, exporting from the tool strips EXIF GPS tags from the file. The text is flattened into the image and the location record is removed in the same step. Keep the original file if you want to preserve that data.
