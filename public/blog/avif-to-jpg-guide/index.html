AVIF to JPG: Complete Guide

Convert AVIF images from modern websites to JPG. What AVIF is, why apps reject it, quality settings, and transparency handling.

AVIF is the newest image format on the web, and it is also the one most apps cannot open. You download a beautiful image from a modern website, and your photo viewer, your editor, or your upload form rejects it.

Converting AVIF to JPG is the fix. The result opens everywhere, from a ten-year-old laptop to a government portal, and the conversion takes seconds.

This guide explains what AVIF is, why it exists, and how to convert it cleanly.

What Is AVIF?

AVIF stands for AV1 Image File Format. It is based on the AV1 video codec, the same technology used in modern video streaming, adapted to store still images.

The format compresses photos extremely efficiently. An AVIF image is typically 30 to 50 percent smaller than a JPG at the same visual quality, which is why websites adopted it.

AVIF supports high dynamic range, wide colour gamut, transparency, and even animation. It is the most capable image format available today in purely technical terms.

The format is open and royalty-free, developed by the Alliance for Open Media. That openness helped it spread quickly across browsers and websites.

Why Do Modern Websites Serve AVIF?

Websites serve AVIF because it makes pages load faster. A photo that is half the size loads in half the time, which improves the visitor experience and the search ranking.

Modern browsers support AVIF natively. Chrome, Edge, Firefox, and Safari all display it, so most visitors see the images without any problem.

Content delivery networks convert images to AVIF automatically. A website may serve AVIF to modern browsers and JPG to older ones, without the site owner doing anything.

That automatic conversion is why you keep downloading AVIF files. The website chose the format, not you, and your computer must deal with the result.

Why Do Apps and Forms Reject AVIF?

AVIF is new, and many systems were built before it existed. Upload forms, printers, and older software list JPG and PNG as accepted formats, and AVIF is not on the list.

Desktop photo editors are catching up slowly. Photoshop supports AVIF now, but many older editors do not, and a file that opens in the browser may fail in the editor.

Operating systems are split. Recent versions of Windows, macOS, and Android handle AVIF, but older versions show a broken icon instead of the image.

Converting to JPG removes all of that uncertainty. JPG has worked everywhere for thirty years, and no system built in that time rejects it.

How Does AVIF Decoding Work in a Browser?

Modern browsers can decode AVIF natively, so the converter uses the browser's own decoder to read the file. No extra plugin or download is needed.

The decoded pixels are drawn onto a canvas and exported as a standard JPG, PNG, or WebP. The result is an ordinary image file that any program can open.

If your browser cannot decode AVIF, the conversion fails with a clear message. Updating the browser usually fixes it, since AVIF support arrived in recent versions.

The conversion happens entirely on your device. Your downloaded images never leave your computer, which matters for personal files and client work.

Quality Settings That Work

For most AVIF photos, 85 percent JPG quality is the sweet spot. The photo looks clean, the file is reasonable, and the loss from the original is invisible.

For images going to print, use 90 percent or higher. Print reveals flaws that screens hide, and the extra size is cheap for a single important image.

For images going to a form with a size limit, start at 85 and step down gradually. Check the size after each step, and stop as soon as the file fits.

AVIF photos are often very high quality, so they tolerate conversion well. The JPG at 85 percent usually looks identical to the AVIF at normal viewing sizes.

Why the JPG Is Bigger Than the AVIF

A converted JPG is almost always larger than the original AVIF, often by double. That surprises people, but it is normal and expected.

AVIF is a far more efficient format than JPG. Moving from a modern format to an older one costs space, because JPG cannot pack the same detail into fewer bytes.

The JPG is not worse than the AVIF. It holds the same picture, just stored less efficiently. The larger size is the price of universal compatibility.

If the larger size is a problem, lower the quality to 80 percent. That step often halves the file size with little visible change.

Transparency in AVIF Conversion

AVIF supports transparency, just like PNG and WebP. When an AVIF with transparent areas becomes a JPG, those areas must be filled with a solid matte colour.

White is the default and suits most documents and web pages. A logo converted onto white looks natural on a white page or in a Word document.

Pick black for dark designs, or enter a hex code to match a specific website background exactly. A matched background makes the logo look like it belongs there.

If you need to keep the transparency, convert to PNG instead. The Image Converter offers PNG output for AVIF files, preserving the alpha channel.

HDR and Wide-Gamut Photos

AVIF supports high dynamic range and wide colour gamut, which capture brighter highlights and richer colours than standard formats. Those features are part of why the format looks so good.

Converting to JPG flattens the HDR data into standard colours. The image still looks good, but the brightest highlights and the most saturated colours may look slightly less vivid.

The shift is subtle for most photos. Only images with extreme highlights or intense colours show a visible difference after conversion.

For colour-critical work, compare the JPG against the AVIF carefully. If the shift matters, keep the AVIF as the master and use the JPG only where compatibility demands it.

Batch Converting AVIF Downloads

You can convert several AVIF downloads in one batch. Each file converts independently, and the ZIP holds them all.

Files up to 25MB each are accepted, which covers high-resolution downloads and camera captures without any preparation.

Use the same quality and matte settings for the whole batch. Consistency matters when the images belong to the same project or document.

Watch for error badges as the batch runs. A file that your browser cannot decode shows a warning, and updating the browser usually fixes it.

Checking Quality Before Downloading

Click Compare on any card to see the AVIF and the JPG side by side. Drag the split handle across the image to inspect faces, text, and fine detail.

Check the colours first, since wide-gamut AVIF photos can shift slightly during conversion. A quick comparison shows whether the shift matters for that image.

If the result looks soft, raise the quality by five points and convert again. The original AVIF stays in the queue, so you never need to re-upload.

For a batch, check three or four representative files rather than every one. If those look good, the rest will too, since the settings are the same.

When to Keep AVIF Instead

Keep AVIF when the image lives on a modern website. The smaller files make pages load faster, and every modern browser displays them.

Keep AVIF as your master copy when you download from a website. It is the best-quality version you have, and you can convert it again whenever you need a different format.

Convert to JPG when the destination is a form, a printer, an email, or old software. Compatibility matters more than size there, and JPG opens everywhere.

Do not convert your whole AVIF collection to JPG just in case. That doubles your storage use for no benefit, since most images will never leave the modern web.

Common Problems and Fixes

If the conversion fails, your browser may be too old to decode AVIF. Update to the latest version of Chrome, Edge, Firefox, or Safari, and try again.

If the JPG looks dull, the HDR or wide-gamut colours shifted during conversion. A small saturation adjustment in any photo app restores the punch.

If a transparent AVIF gets a black box in the JPG, the matte colour was black or unset. Change it to white or your page colour and convert again.

If the JPG is much larger than the AVIF, that is normal. AVIF is more efficient, and the larger JPG is the price of a format that opens everywhere.

AVIF Images from News Sites

News sites serve images as AVIF to make their pages load faster, so a photo saved from a news article is often an AVIF file. It opens in your browser but refuses to open in your photo editor.

Convert the AVIF to JPG before using it anywhere else. The JPG opens in Word, Photoshop, and every other app, while the AVIF may fail in half of them.

Check the image quality after converting. A news photo is often already compressed by the site, so the JPG version may look soft, and raising the quality cannot restore the lost detail.

For a photo you plan to keep, save the AVIF original as well. It is the best-quality copy you have from the site, and you can convert it again if you need a different format later.

Respect the photo's copyright when using it. A news photo belongs to its creator or agency, and converting the format does not change the ownership.

AVIF Images for Work

Work documents often need an image downloaded from a website, and that image is usually AVIF. Pasting it into Word or PowerPoint may fail, since older Office versions do not support AVIF.

Convert the AVIF to JPG before adding it to the document. The JPG embeds cleanly in every Office version, and the document opens correctly on any colleague's computer.

For a report with many web images, convert the whole set first, then build the document. That order prevents the frustrating discovery that half the images are broken after hours of work.

Check the file size after converting. A report with twenty 2MB JPGs is 40MB, which may exceed email limits, so compress the images if the document must be sent by email.

For a printed report, keep the quality at 90 percent or higher. Print reveals flaws that screens hide, and a high-quality JPG is the safest choice for paper.

AVIF Images for Printing

Print shops expect JPG or PNG, and an AVIF file may be rejected by their system. Convert to JPG before ordering prints, and check the shop's requirements first.

Keep the quality at 90 percent or higher for prints. A photo printed at 6 by 4 inches needs every pixel of detail, and a low-quality JPG shows its flaws on paper.

Check the dimensions before printing. A web image at 800 pixels wide prints well at 3 inches but poorly at 10 inches, so match the print size to the pixel count.

For large prints, use the highest-resolution source you have. An AVIF downloaded from a website is often small, and no conversion can add the pixels a large print needs.

Order a test print for an important photo. A single small print confirms the colours and sharpness before you commit to a large order or a canvas.

AVIF Images for Social Media

Social media platforms serve images as AVIF, and they accept AVIF uploads too. A photo posted as AVIF loads faster, though the platform converts it to its own format anyway.

For a photo post, upload a high-quality JPG instead. The platform compresses it anyway, and a JPG input is the most compatible starting point across all platforms.

For a graphic with text, upload a PNG or a high-quality WebP. The text stays sharper through the platform's compression, which matters for announcement graphics.

Check the platform's preferred dimensions before uploading. Instagram, Facebook, and X each have ideal sizes, and the right size matters more than the format.

Download the platform's version after uploading and check it. A quick look at the published post confirms the image survived the platform's processing intact.

Keeping AVIF Originals After Conversion

Converted JPGs are delivery copies, not masters. The AVIF originals are the best-quality versions you own, and they deserve safe storage.

Store the AVIF originals in a folder named for their source, such as website-downloads. A clear folder name makes them easy to find when you need them again.

Do not delete the originals after converting. A JPG can never become a perfect AVIF again, so the originals are your safety net if you need a different format later.

Back up the originals in two places. A local drive and a cloud backup protect against hardware failure, theft, and accidental deletion.

Review the originals every year or two. Formats change, and an AVIF archive may need migrating to a newer format before the old one becomes hard to open.

Frequently asked questions

What is AVIF and why do some apps fail to open it?

AVIF is a modern image format based on the AV1 video codec, and it compresses photos 30 to 50 percent smaller than JPG. Many apps and forms were built before AVIF existed, so they list only JPG and PNG as accepted formats. Converting to JPG makes the image open everywhere.

Which browsers can decode AVIF for conversion?

Chrome, Edge, Firefox, and Safari in recent versions all decode AVIF natively, which the converter uses. If your browser is too old, the conversion fails with a clear message, and updating the browser usually fixes it. The decoding happens entirely on your device.

Does converting AVIF to JPG lose quality?

A little, because JPG is lossier than AVIF, but at 85 percent quality the loss is invisible in normal viewing. The photo keeps its full resolution and looks the same on screen. Only HDR highlights and very saturated colours may shift slightly during conversion.

Will transparent AVIF areas turn black in the JPG?

Only if the matte colour is black or unset. JPG cannot store transparency, so the see-through areas are filled with the matte colour you choose. Pick white for most uses, or a hex code to match a specific background, and check the result before downloading.

What is the largest AVIF file accepted?

Each AVIF file can be up to 25MB, which covers high-resolution downloads and camera captures. Because decoding runs on your device, large files process at local speed with no upload waiting time.

Why is the JPG sometimes larger than the AVIF?

AVIF is far more efficient than JPG, so the same image needs more bytes as a JPG. The picture is unchanged, only the storage is less efficient. The larger size is the price of universal compatibility. Lowering the quality to 80 percent often halves the file again.
