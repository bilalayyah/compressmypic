AVIF vs JPG: Next-Gen vs Universal

AVIF vs JPG compared: file size, image quality, HDR, transparency, browser support, and which format to use for the web and beyond.

AVIF is the most efficient image format ever made for the web, and JPG is the most compatible format ever made, full stop. One wins on technology, the other wins on ubiquity, and the choice between them is a trade-off.

Websites are switching to AVIF because it halves their image weight. The rest of the world still runs on JPG because it opens everywhere.

This comparison looks at both formats honestly, so you can decide which one suits each situation.

AVIF at a Glance

AVIF is based on the AV1 video codec, adapted for still images. It compresses photos 30 to 50 percent smaller than JPG at the same visual quality, which is the largest efficiency gain of any modern format.

The format supports high dynamic range, wide colour gamut, transparency, and animation. It is the most capable image format available today in purely technical terms.

AVIF is open and royalty-free, developed by the Alliance for Open Media. That openness helped it spread quickly across browsers and content delivery networks.

Modern browsers all support AVIF now. Chrome, Edge, Firefox, and Safari display it natively, which covers nearly all web traffic.

JPG at a Glance

JPG is a lossy format from 1992, designed for photographs. It discards detail to shrink files, and it does that job well enough to have survived three decades.

Every device, browser, printer, and upload form on earth can open a JPG. There is no mainstream system that rejects it, and there never has been.

JPG supports only 8-bit colour and no transparency. A JPG is a flat rectangle of pixels, which is simple, reliable, and limiting at the same time.

The format's age shows in its efficiency. Modern formats pack the same quality into far fewer bytes, but JPG's universality keeps it relevant.

File Size: AVIF Wins Decisively

A 1MB JPG photo becomes a 500KB to 700KB AVIF at the same visual quality. That 30 to 50 percent saving is the largest of any format comparison on the web.

The saving is larger for detailed images. A busy landscape with endless texture leaves more room for AVIF's modern encoder to improve on JPG's ageing one.

Across a whole website, the savings are dramatic. A page with twenty images might drop from 10MB to 5MB, which is a visible improvement in loading time.

For mobile data, the saving matters even more. Visitors on slow connections notice the difference immediately, and they stay longer on fast pages.

Image Quality: AVIF Wins Clearly

At the same file size, AVIF looks better than JPG. Its modern compression preserves edges, textures, and gradients more faithfully than the ageing JPG standard.

At the same visual quality, AVIF is smaller. Either way you measure it, AVIF is the more efficient format for photographs.

The difference is subtle at high quality. A JPG at 90 percent and an AVIF at equivalent quality look identical to most eyes, though the AVIF file is far smaller.

The difference grows at low quality. A JPG at 60 percent shows blocky artefacts, while an AVIF at the same size stays cleaner and more natural.

HDR and Wide Colour Gamut

AVIF supports high dynamic range, which captures brighter highlights and deeper shadows than standard formats. A sunset photo in AVIF holds detail in both the bright sky and the dark foreground.

AVIF supports wide colour gamut too, including Display P3 colours from modern cameras. Those rich colours survive in AVIF, while JPG flattens them into standard colours.

JPG supports neither feature. Its 8-bit colour and standard range were designed in 1992, when HDR and wide gamut did not exist for consumers.

For photographers and designers, these features matter. For everyday sharing, they are invisible, since most screens cannot display HDR anyway.

Transparency: AVIF Wins Completely

JPG cannot store transparency at all. Any transparent area must be filled with a solid colour, which puts a visible box around logos on coloured backgrounds.

AVIF supports full transparency, including partial transparency for soft edges and shadows. An AVIF logo sits cleanly on any background, just like a PNG.

This makes AVIF a genuine replacement for PNG on the web, not just for JPG. Logos, icons, and stickers all work as AVIF with smaller files than PNG.

For any image that needs transparency, JPG is not an option at all. The comparison is between AVIF and PNG, and AVIF usually wins on size.

Browser Support: AVIF Catches Up Fast

Chrome, Edge, Firefox, and Safari all support AVIF now. That covers the overwhelming majority of web traffic, and the format is safe to use on modern websites.

JPG works in every browser ever made, including Internet Explorer. There is no visitor, however old their software, who cannot see a JPG.

The gap is now small for browsers, but large beyond them. Upload forms, printers, and older software lag years behind browsers in adding AVIF support.

For the few visitors on ancient browsers, serve a JPG fallback. The picture element in HTML lets you offer AVIF first and JPG second, so everyone sees the image.

Support Beyond Browsers

Desktop photo editors are catching up. Photoshop supports AVIF now, but many older editors do not, so JPG is still the safer choice for editing workflows.

Operating systems are split. Recent versions of Windows, macOS, and Android handle AVIF, but older versions show a broken icon instead of the image.

Printers and photo labs mostly expect JPG or PNG. An AVIF file sent to a print shop may be rejected, so convert to JPG for print work.

Email clients are inconsistent with AVIF. Some display it, others do not, so JPG remains the safe choice for images sent by email.

When to Use AVIF

Use AVIF for website images with a modern audience. The smaller files make pages load faster, which improves the visitor experience and the search ranking.

Use AVIF for product photos on an online store. A catalogue with hundreds of images loads far faster when each one is 40 percent smaller.

Use AVIF when you control the whole pipeline. If you serve the images, you choose the format, and modern browsers will display them.

Use AVIF as a master format for web images. Keep the AVIF originals, and convert copies to JPG only when a specific destination demands it.

When to Use JPG

Use JPG when the destination is unknown. A form, a client, a printer, or an email recipient will always accept JPG, while AVIF might be rejected.

Use JPG for email attachments. Email clients display JPG reliably, while AVIF support in email is still inconsistent across apps.

Use JPG for print work. Print shops expect JPG or PNG, and an AVIF file adds compatibility risk for no benefit on paper.

Use JPG as the fallback format on websites. Serve AVIF to modern browsers and JPG to older ones, and every visitor sees the image.

Converting Between AVIF and JPG

Converting AVIF to JPG is easy and safe at 85 percent quality. The photo keeps its dimensions and looks the same, while the file gets bigger.

Converting JPG to AVIF is possible in some tools, but it is rarely worth it. The JPG damage is already baked in, and the AVIF version is not better than the JPG it came from.

Always convert from the best original you have. An AVIF converted to JPG and back to AVIF loses quality at each lossy step, so avoid round trips.

Keep the AVIF originals after converting to JPG. If a destination rejects JPG, which is unlikely, the AVIFs are ready, and if a web job needs AVIF, they are already in the right format.

The Verdict: Which Format Wins?

AVIF wins on efficiency, quality, and features. It is smaller, sharper, richer, and more capable, and it is the better format in purely technical terms.

JPG wins on compatibility and simplicity. It opens everywhere, it is understood by every system, and it never surprises you with a rejection.

For the web, use AVIF with a JPG fallback. For everything else, use JPG. That combination gives you the best of both formats without the worst of either.

The future belongs to AVIF and its successors, but the present still needs JPG. Use each where it shines, and convert between them freely.

AVIF and JPG for Social Media

Social media platforms serve images as AVIF and accept AVIF uploads too. A photo posted as AVIF loads faster, though the platform converts it to its own format anyway.

For a photo post, upload a high-quality JPG instead. The platform compresses it anyway, and a JPG input is the most compatible starting point across all platforms.

For a graphic with text, upload a PNG or a high-quality WebP. The text stays sharper through the platform's compression, which matters for announcement graphics.

Check the platform's preferred dimensions before uploading. Instagram, Facebook, and X each have ideal sizes, and the right size matters more than the format.

Download the platform's version after uploading and check it. A quick look at the published post confirms the image survived the platform's processing intact.

AVIF and JPG for E-commerce

An online shop lives or dies by its images. AVIF product photos load far faster than JPG ones, and a fast shop converts better than a slow one.

Convert product photos to AVIF at high quality for the shop, with JPG as a fallback. Modern browsers get the smaller AVIF, older ones get the JPG, and every shopper sees the product.

For product thumbnails, AVIF saves the most. The thumbnails are small, the savings add up across hundreds of products, and the category pages load instantly.

Test the shop on a phone after switching. Mobile shoppers are the majority now, and a fast-loading shop on a slow connection converts far better than a slow one.

Keep the JPG originals as well, since some marketplaces demand JPG. A product listed on Amazon and on your own shop needs both formats ready.

AVIF and JPG for News

News sites publish dozens of images a day, and the volume makes AVIF savings enormous. A single article with five images saves megabytes, and a homepage with fifty saves far more.

Convert news photos to AVIF at high quality for the site, with JPG as a fallback. The images look clean, the pages load fast, and readers on mobile connections stay instead of leaving.

For breaking news, speed matters most. An AVIF image uploads and loads faster than a JPG, so the story reaches readers sooner, which is the point of news.

Automate the conversion in the publishing workflow if possible. A newsroom that converts automatically never forgets, while a manual step gets skipped under deadline pressure.

Keep a JPG fallback for older readers. The picture element serves AVIF to modern browsers and JPG to the rest, so every reader sees the image.

AVIF and JPG for Photography

Photographers care about quality above all, and AVIF preserves more detail than JPG at the same file size. A landscape in AVIF holds the fine texture that JPG simplifies away.

For a photography portfolio, AVIF at high quality shows the work at its best. The images load fast, the detail stays visible, and clients see the quality they are paying for.

For delivering photos to a client, JPG remains the safer choice. The client may use old software that cannot open AVIF, while a JPG works everywhere without a second thought.

Keep the masters as AVIF or RAW, and deliver JPG copies. That split gives you the best archive and the most compatible delivery, without compromising either goal.

For prints, neither format is ideal. A TIFF or PNG master prints best, while AVIF and JPG are both lossy formats designed for screens rather than paper.

The Format Race Ahead

AVIF is the current leader in efficiency, but JPEG XL waits in the wings with even better compression. The format race will continue, and today's winner may be tomorrow's runner-up.

JPG will remain the universal fallback throughout. It is thirty years old and supported by every system ever built, while newer formats take years to reach the same ubiquity.

Websites will adopt the newest formats first, since they benefit most from smaller files. Upload forms and printers will adopt them last, since they change slowly and value stability.

For website owners, the strategy is clear: serve the newest format with a JPG fallback. That approach works today and will keep working as formats change.

For everyone else, the advice is simpler: when a file will not open, convert it to JPG. That advice has worked for thirty years, and it will work for thirty more.

Frequently asked questions

Is AVIF better quality than JPG?

At the same file size, yes, AVIF looks better, because its modern compression is far more efficient. At the same visible quality, AVIF is 30 to 50 percent smaller. At high quality settings, though, the two look identical to the human eye, so quality alone is rarely the deciding factor.

Should I keep my photos as AVIF or convert them all to JPG?

Keep the masters as AVIF and convert copies to JPG only when sharing outside the modern web. Converting your whole collection doubles your storage use for no benefit, since most images never leave your devices. Convert per need, not in bulk, unless a specific job demands it.

Can Windows open AVIF files without converting them?

Recent Windows versions can, with the AV1 codec installed, but many users never install it, and older versions cannot at all. Even with the codec, some programs still reject AVIF. Converting to JPG is faster and more reliable than asking every Windows recipient to install a codec.

Which format is better for a website, AVIF or JPG?

AVIF is better for modern websites, since it is 30 to 50 percent smaller at the same quality, which makes pages load faster. JPG is the safe fallback for older browsers. Serve AVIF first with a JPG fallback, so every visitor sees the image and most get the faster version.

Does AVIF support transparency like PNG?

Yes. AVIF supports full transparency, including partial transparency for soft edges and shadows, just like PNG. That makes it a genuine replacement for PNG on the web, usually at a much smaller file size. JPG cannot store transparency at all.

Will AVIF replace JPG eventually?

Probably not soon. JPG is thirty years old and supported by every system ever built, while AVIF still lacks universal support outside browsers. Newer formats will keep competing for the same role. For now, JPG remains the universal photo format, and AVIF is the modern web format.
