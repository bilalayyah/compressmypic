Blur and Pixelate Images: Complete Guide

Learn how to blur and pixelate images online: hide faces, text, and details with smooth blur or mosaic pixelation, brush or whole-image modes.

Some parts of a photo should not be shared. A face in the background, an address on a letter, or a password on a screen can all cause real harm if they reach the wrong eyes.

Blurring and pixelating are the two ways to hide those details. Both make a region unreadable, but they look different and they protect differently.

This guide explains both methods, when to use each one, and how to apply them cleanly. It covers brush and whole-image modes, strength settings, and the mistakes that leave sensitive details readable. The free Blur and Pixelate Image tool puts both methods in one place, so you can hide a face or a line of text in seconds without uploading the photo anywhere.

What Blurring Does to an Image

Blurring softens a region by averaging the colours of neighbouring pixels. Sharp edges melt away, text becomes unreadable, and faces lose their features.

A light blur only softens the detail slightly, while a strong blur turns the region into a smooth wash of colour. The strength setting decides how much information survives.

Blur is best for backgrounds and gentle anonymisation. A softly blurred background keeps the photo looking natural while pushing the attention to the subject.

For hiding sensitive information, blur must be strong. A light blur over a face or an address can sometimes be sharpened back, so use a heavy blur when privacy matters.

What Pixelating Does to an Image

Pixelating replaces a region with large blocks of solid colour, like a mosaic. Each block is the average colour of the pixels beneath it, so all fine detail disappears.

The block size controls the strength. Small blocks keep a rough hint of the shape, while large blocks reduce the region to a few flat squares of colour.

Pixelation looks deliberately censored, which is useful when you want the viewer to know something was hidden. A blurred face looks artistic, while a pixelated face looks redacted.

For hiding text, pixelation is usually safer than light blur. A mosaic with large blocks destroys letter shapes completely, while a soft blur can leave them partly readable.

Why People Blur and Pixelate Photos

Privacy is the main reason. A photo shared online can reveal faces, addresses, number plates, and documents, and censoring those details protects the people in the image.

Parents blur their children's faces before posting family photos publicly. The children did not consent to being online, and a blur keeps them out of searchable images.

Journalists and researchers pixelate faces in sensitive stories. A source who spoke on condition of anonymity needs their identity hidden, not just softened.

Businesses blur screens in screenshots. A screenshot of a dashboard can reveal customer names, internal URLs, or passwords, and a careful blur removes them before the image is shared.

Brush Area Mode: Painting Over Details

Brush Area mode lets you paint the blur or pixelation exactly where you need it. You drag the brush over a face, a number plate, or a line of text, and only those strokes are censored.

The rest of the image stays sharp. That precision is what makes brush mode the right choice for most privacy work, where only a small part of the photo needs hiding.

Brush size matters. A large brush covers a face in a few strokes, while a small brush lets you trace around a single word or a small detail without touching the rest.

If you make a mistake while painting, the undo button removes the last stroke. You can also reset the brush entirely and start again, which is faster than trying to fix a bad stroke.

Whole Image Mode: Blurring Everything

Whole Image mode applies the effect to the entire photo at once. It is the right choice when the whole image is sensitive, or when you want a blurred background for a design.

A fully blurred photo makes a good background for text or a presentation slide. The colours and the mood remain, but nothing distracts from the words on top.

Switching to Whole Image mode changes the target area from your brush strokes to the full frame. The tool applies the current blur or pixelation strength to everything.

Use whole-image mode carefully for privacy. A fully blurred photo hides everything, but it also hides the subject, so it is rarely the right choice for sharing a photo publicly.

Smooth Blur Strength: How Strong Is Strong Enough?

Blur strength is a scale, and the right value depends on what you are hiding. A background needs only a light blur, while a face or an address needs a heavy one.

For faces, use a strong blur that removes all recognisable features. A light blur that leaves the eyes and the smile visible does not protect anyone.

For text, a strong blur is also needed. A lightly blurred word can sometimes be guessed from its length and its shape, especially when the context gives it away.

Test the result by zooming in. If you can still read the text or recognise the face at full size, increase the strength and check again.

Mosaic Block Size: Bigger Blocks, Safer Censoring

Mosaic block size works like blur strength: larger blocks hide more. A small block size keeps the rough shape of the region, while a large block size reduces it to a few flat squares.

For small screenshots, use a moderate block size. The text is small, so even a modest block destroys the letter shapes completely.

For large photos, use larger blocks. A face in a high-resolution photo needs big blocks to hide the features, because small blocks still leave the eyes and the mouth recognisable.

When in doubt, go bigger. A mosaic that is slightly too strong looks intentional, while a mosaic that is slightly too weak leaves the sensitive detail readable.

Brush Size: Matching the Tool to the Detail

Brush size should match the detail you are hiding. A face needs a large brush, a number plate needs a medium one, and a single word needs a small one.

A brush that is too large censors more than you intended. It can cover a neighbouring face or a useful part of the image that did not need hiding.

A brush that is too small takes many strokes to cover a large area, and the gaps between strokes can leave slivers of the sensitive detail visible.

Adjust the brush size as you work. Start large for the main area, then switch to a smaller brush to clean up the edges and cover any missed spots.

Hiding Faces Properly

A face needs full coverage to be hidden. The eyes, the nose, the mouth, and the hairline all carry identifying information, so the censored area must cover the whole head.

Pixelation is usually the safer choice for faces. A mosaic with large blocks destroys the features completely, while a light blur can leave enough for recognition.

Cover the whole head, not just the eyes. A black bar over the eyes is a movie cliché, not real protection, because the rest of the face is still recognisable.

Check the result at full size. Zoom into the censored face and confirm that no feature is visible, then check the background for reflections that might show the face again.

Hiding Text, Numbers, and Addresses

Text is harder to hide than faces, because even a partial letter can give away the word. A licence plate, an address, or a password needs complete coverage.

Pixelation with large blocks is the safest method for text. It destroys the letter shapes entirely, while a soft blur can leave the outline of the words readable.

Cover the whole line, not just the sensitive word. The length of a line and the words around it can give away the hidden part, so censor generously.

Check for the same text elsewhere in the image. An address on an envelope may also appear on a letter in the background, and both copies need censoring.

Blurring Backgrounds for Focus and Privacy

A blurred background makes the subject stand out. Portrait photographers have used this effect for decades, and a digital blur gives the same result after the fact.

Paint the blur over the background with the brush, keeping the subject sharp. The contrast between the sharp subject and the soft background draws the eye exactly where you want it.

For privacy, a blurred background can hide a location. A street sign, a house number, or a recognisable building in the background can reveal where a photo was taken.

Use a light blur for backgrounds and a heavy blur for sensitive details. The two strengths serve different purposes, and mixing them up weakens both.

Formats and Transparency

The tool accepts common image formats, including JPG, PNG, WebP, and HEIC, with files up to 20 MB. You can export as JPG, PNG, or WebP depending on your needs.

Exporting as PNG or WebP preserves transparency in the base image. If your source is a transparent PNG graphic, the censored export keeps the see-through areas intact.

For photos, JPG is usually the best export. It keeps file sizes small and works everywhere, and the censored areas compress cleanly.

The quality slider appears for JPG and WebP exports and hides for PNG, because PNG is lossless and has no quality setting to adjust.

Privacy: Censoring Without Uploading

All blurring and pixelating happens in your browser. Your photos are never uploaded to a server, which matters when the images contain faces, IDs, or private addresses.

This is the most important feature of a censoring tool. A photo that needs hiding should never be sent to a server you cannot see, just to blur a face.

The tool also strips EXIF GPS data on export. A censored photo downloaded from the tool carries no record of where it was taken, which is a second layer of protection.

Keep the uncensored original safe and separate. Once you share the censored version, the original is the only copy that still shows the full image.

Common Mistakes When Censoring Images

The most common mistake is censoring too lightly. A soft blur over a face or a small mosaic over text can leave the detail readable, which defeats the whole purpose.

Another mistake is missing a reflection. A face can appear in a mirror, a window, or a pair of sunglasses in the same photo, and every copy needs censoring.

People also forget the metadata. A photo with a blurred face can still carry GPS coordinates that reveal where it was taken, so check the export settings.

Finally, do not share the original by accident. A censored image and its original often sit side by side in a downloads folder, and attaching the wrong one undoes all the work.

A Workflow for Safe Sharing

Open the original photo and list everything that needs hiding: faces, addresses, number plates, screens, and documents. Check the background as well as the foreground.

Paint over each item with the brush, using pixelation for text and a strong blur or mosaic for faces. Zoom in after each stroke to confirm full coverage.

Export the censored version and check it at full size. Look for missed reflections, readable text, and any detail you overlooked on the first pass.

Share only the censored file. Keep the original in a separate folder, and never attach it to the same message or post as the censored version.

Blurring for Video Thumbnails and Covers

A blurred photo makes a good video thumbnail background. The soft colours set the mood, while the title text on top stays sharp and easy to read.

Use Whole Image mode with a moderate blur for thumbnails. The image should be recognisable as a scene, but soft enough that the text dominates the frame.

Keep the subject slightly visible in the blur. A thumbnail that is only colour feels empty, while one with a faint subject behind the text feels intentional.

Export thumbnails as JPG at a high quality setting. The file needs to look clean at small sizes, and a low quality export will soften the title text as well as the background.

Frequently asked questions

Can a blurred face be unblurred later?

Not from the downloaded file, if the blur was strong enough. Blurring averages the pixels and destroys the original detail, so there is nothing to recover. A very light blur can sometimes be sharpened, which is why faces need a strong blur or a large mosaic.

Is pixelation safer than blur for hiding a number plate?

Yes, usually. A mosaic with large blocks destroys the letter and number shapes completely, while a soft blur can leave their outlines readable. Use pixelation for text and numbers, and a strong blur or mosaic for faces.

How do I blur a face in a photo on my phone?

Open the Blur and Pixelate tool in your phone browser, upload the photo, and choose Brush Area mode. Paint over the face with your finger, using a large brush and a strong blur or mosaic, then preview and download the result.

Should I blur the whole background of a portrait?

Only if the background reveals something private, such as a street sign or a house number. A light blur also makes the subject stand out, which is a creative choice. Use a heavy blur for privacy and a light blur for focus.

What should I do if I miss a spot while painting?

Use the undo button to remove the last stroke, or switch to a smaller brush and paint over the missed area. Zoom in after each stroke to check for gaps. The reset button clears all brush strokes if you want to start again.

Does blurring a photo remove its location data?

Yes, exporting from the tool strips EXIF GPS tags from the file. The blur hides the visible details and the export removes the hidden location record. Keep the original file if you want to preserve that data.
