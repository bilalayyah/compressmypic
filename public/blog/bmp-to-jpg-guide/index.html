BMP to JPG: Complete Guide

BMP is the oldest image format on Windows, and it is also the most wasteful. A BMP screenshot can weigh 20MB, while the same image as a JPG weighs 500KB, with no visible difference.

Converting BMP to JPG is the fastest way to make old Windows images usable again. The file shrinks by 90 percent or more, and the result opens everywhere.

This guide explains why BMP files are so large, how the conversion works, and when JPG is the right target.

What Is BMP?

BMP stands for bitmap, and it is the native image format of Windows, dating back to the 1990s. It stores every pixel exactly, with little or no compression.

The format was designed for a time when processors were slow and storage was simple. Storing pixels raw was fast, and nobody worried about the file size.

BMP files come from old Windows software, scanners, and digital cameras that save in the format. They also appear as screenshots from legacy applications.

The format supports several colour depths, from 1-bit black and white to 24-bit full colour. Older files may use 8-bit palettes, while newer ones are usually 24-bit.

Why Are BMP Files So Huge?

BMP stores pixels with almost no compression. A 1920 by 1080 screenshot at 24-bit colour needs over 6MB just for the pixels, plus headers, with no way to shrink it.

JPG compresses the same screenshot to 500KB by discarding detail the eye barely notices. The difference is the compression, not the image itself.

Flat areas waste the most space in BMP. A white background that JPG stores in a few bytes needs thousands of bytes in BMP, since every white pixel is stored individually.

Noise wastes space too. A scanned image with paper grain has endless tiny variations, and BMP stores every one of them exactly, while JPG simplifies them away.

How Does BMP to JPG Conversion Work?

The converter reads the BMP file directly in your browser. BMP decoding is built into browsers, so no extra decoder is needed, and the file loads immediately.

The pixels are drawn onto a canvas and encoded as a JPG at the quality you chose. The image keeps its dimensions and looks the same, while the file shrinks dramatically.

Files up to 25MB each are accepted, which covers large screenshots and high-resolution scans without any preparation.

The conversion happens entirely on your device. Your scans and screenshots never leave your computer, which matters for personal and work files.

Quality Settings That Work

For scanned documents, 85 to 90 percent quality keeps the text readable while shrinking the file substantially. Small print blurs at lower settings, so stay high for documents.

For screenshots, 85 percent is the sweet spot. The interface stays crisp, the file shrinks by 90 percent, and the image looks clean.

For photographs saved as BMP, 80 to 85 percent is the right range. The photo looks clean, and the file is a fraction of the BMP size.

For images going to a form with a size limit, start at 85 and step down gradually. Check the size after each step, and stop as soon as the file fits.

What Happens to Colour Depth?

A 24-bit BMP converts cleanly to JPG, since JPG also uses 24-bit colour. The millions of colours in the original are preserved as well as JPG compression allows.

An 8-bit BMP with a colour palette converts too. The palette colours become ordinary RGB colours in the JPG, and the image looks the same.

A 1-bit black and white BMP, such as a fax scan, converts to a JPG with only black and white pixels. The file shrinks enormously, since the image is so simple.

Old 1-bit and 8-bit BMP files are fully supported. The converter reads the palette and the pixel data, and the JPG reproduces the image faithfully.

Why Scanned BMP Files Compress So Well

Scanned BMP files are mostly white paper with dark text. That simplicity compresses beautifully as JPG, since large flat areas need very few bytes.

A 10MB scanned BMP can become a 300KB JPG at 85 percent quality. That is a 97 percent saving, with the text still perfectly readable.

Paper grain is the only thing that resists compression. A noisy scan saves less than a clean one, though the saving is still dramatic.

For an archive of scanned BMP files, converting to JPG can free gigabytes of storage. A folder that filled a drive becomes a folder that fits on a USB stick.

Does BMP Have Transparency?

Standard BMP files do not support transparency. Every pixel has a solid colour, and there is no alpha channel for see-through areas.

Some rare 32-bit BMP files carry an alpha channel, but most software ignores it. For practical purposes, BMP images are always fully opaque.

Converting to JPG therefore loses nothing on transparency. The JPG is opaque too, and the image looks exactly the same as the BMP did.

If you need transparency, convert the BMP to PNG instead. The Image Converter can do that, though a BMP rarely has transparency worth preserving.

Batch Converting a Folder of BMP Scans

You can convert a whole folder of BMP scans in one batch. Each file converts independently, and the ZIP holds them all.

Use the same quality setting for the whole folder. Consistency matters when the scans belong to the same archive or document set.

Watch the batch summary after converting. A folder of BMP scans often shrinks by 95 percent overall, which is a dramatic number that justifies the effort.

Keep the BMP originals in an archive folder after converting. They are the masters, and you can always convert them again if you need a different format later.

Checking Quality Before Downloading

Click Compare on any card to see the BMP and the JPG side by side. Drag the split handle across the image and zoom into text and fine detail.

Check the smallest text first, since it suffers most from JPG compression. A scanned document at 85 percent quality should keep its print perfectly readable.

If the text looks soft, raise the quality by five points and convert again. The original BMP stays in the queue, so you never re-upload.

For a batch, check three or four representative files rather than every one. If those look good, the rest will too, since the settings are the same.

BMP to JPG or BMP to PNG?

Choose JPG when the goal is smaller files. A BMP scan converted to JPG shrinks by 90 percent or more, while the PNG version shrinks far less.

Choose PNG when the image will be edited many times. PNG is lossless, so repeated saves do not degrade it, while each JPG save adds a little damage.

Choose JPG for screenshots and scans going to a form or an email. The smaller file sends faster and uploads without hitting size limits.

Choose PNG for line art and diagrams where every pixel must stay exact. The lossless format preserves thin lines better than JPG does.

Where the Converted JPG Works

The converted JPG opens in any program, on any device. Windows Photos, macOS Preview, WhatsApp, and older printers all handle JPG without any trouble.

It uploads to every form, portal, and website that lists JPG as an accepted format. Government systems, job boards, and print shops all take JPG.

It attaches to emails without trouble. A BMP attachment is often too large to send, while the JPG version sails through without hitting limits.

It prints cleanly on any printer. Printers expect JPG, and a converted JPG prints exactly like a camera JPG of the same quality.

Common Problems and Fixes

If a BMP file fails to convert, it may use an unusual compression or an unsupported colour depth. Try opening it in Windows Paint first, and re-save it as a standard BMP.

If the text looks blurry, the quality was too low. Raise the slider to 90 percent and convert again from the original BMP.

If the JPG is barely smaller than the BMP, the image is probably very simple or very noisy. A simple image has little to compress, while noise resists compression in both formats.

If the colours look wrong, the BMP may use an unusual palette. Check the result carefully, and convert from a fresh export if the source file is suspect.

BMP Files from Old Scanners

Old scanners often save as BMP by default, producing enormous files for simple documents. A single scanned page can weigh 10MB as BMP, while the same page as JPG weighs 300KB.

Convert the scans to JPG at 85 to 90 percent quality. The text stays readable, the files shrink by 95 percent, and the archive fits on a fraction of the disk space.

For a whole archive of scans, convert in batches of fifty. Each batch runs smoothly, and a failure in one batch does not affect the others.

Keep the BMP originals in an archive folder after converting. They are the masters, and you can always convert them again if you need a different format later.

Check the scanner settings for future scans. Switching the scanner to JPG or PDF saves the conversion step entirely, and the files arrive in the right format from the start.

BMP Files from Legacy Software

Legacy Windows software often saves images only as BMP, since the format was the standard when the software was written. Those files need converting before they can be used anywhere else.

Export the image from the legacy program as BMP, then convert it to JPG. The two-step path works for every file, regardless of how old the software is.

Check the image after converting, since old software sometimes produces unusual BMP variants. A quick look at the JPG confirms the colours and the content survived.

For a large collection from legacy software, convert in batches and keep the originals. A batch of fifty files takes minutes, and the originals stay safe in an archive folder.

Document the conversion settings for the next person. A one-line note prevents the slow drift into mixed formats that plagues every legacy migration.

BMP Screenshots from Games

Old games save screenshots as BMP files, and those files are enormous. A 1920 by 1080 screenshot weighs over 6MB as BMP, while the same image as JPG weighs 500KB.

Convert the screenshots to JPG at 85 percent quality. The game graphics stay sharp, the files shrink by 90 percent, and the collection fits on a fraction of the disk space.

For a screenshot collection, convert in batches by game. Each batch gets the same settings, and the folders stay organised by title.

Keep the BMP originals for the screenshots that matter most. A favourite moment deserves a lossless master, while the JPG copies are for sharing.

Share the JPG versions online, since gaming forums and social media handle JPG natively. A BMP upload may fail or take far too long on a slow connection.

BMP Files from Digital Cameras

Some digital cameras save images as BMP, especially older models and industrial cameras. Those files are large, and converting them to JPG makes them usable.

Convert the camera BMPs to JPG at 85 percent quality. The photos look clean, the files shrink dramatically, and the images open in any photo app.

Check the camera settings for future shoots. Switching the camera to JPG saves the conversion step entirely, and the files arrive in the right format from the start.

For photos you plan to edit, keep a PNG copy as the master. The lossless format survives repeated edits, while each JPG save adds a little damage.

Store the converted JPGs in folders organised by date and event. A folder named 2026-11-family-trip is easy to find years later, while a folder named DCIM is not.

Archiving Converted JPGs

Converted JPGs deserve the same care as any archive. Store them in folders organised by date and purpose, so a file from two years ago is still findable.

Keep the original BMPs in a separate archive folder. They are the masters, and a clear separation prevents the two sets from getting mixed up.

Name the converted files by purpose, not by date. A name such as invoice-2024-03.jpg is useful years later, while a name such as scan001.jpg is not.

Back up the archive in two places. A local drive and a cloud backup protect against hardware failure, theft, and accidental deletion.

Review the archive every year or two. Old scans and screenshots that no longer matter can be deleted, freeing space for the files that do.
