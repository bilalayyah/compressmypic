BMP vs JPG: Why BMP Files Are So Large

BMP vs JPG compared: why BMP files are enormous, how much JPG saves, quality, colour depth, and when each format still makes sense.

BMP and JPG are both old Windows-era formats, yet they could hardly be more different. A BMP screenshot weighs 6MB, while the same image as a JPG weighs 500KB, and the two look identical on screen.

The difference is compression. BMP barely compresses at all, while JPG was built to compress aggressively, and that single design choice explains the entire size gap.

This comparison explains why BMP files are so large, how much JPG saves, and when each format still has a role.

BMP at a Glance

BMP is the native image format of Windows, dating back to the 1990s. It stores every pixel exactly, with little or no compression, which makes it simple and fast to read.

The format was designed when processors were slow. Storing pixels raw meant the computer could display them immediately, without spending time decompressing anything.

BMP supports several colour depths, from 1-bit black and white to 24-bit full colour. Older files may use 8-bit palettes, while newer ones are usually 24-bit.

The format is still produced by old Windows software, scanners, and some digital cameras. It is a legacy format, but it has not disappeared.

JPG at a Glance

JPG is a lossy format from 1992, designed for photographs. It discards detail to shrink files, and it does that job well enough to have survived three decades.

Every device, browser, printer, and upload form on earth can open a JPG. There is no mainstream system that rejects it, and there never has been.

JPG supports only 8-bit colour and no transparency. A JPG is a flat rectangle of pixels, which is simple, reliable, and limiting at the same time.

The format's compression is its defining feature. It groups pixels into blocks and simplifies the colour inside each block, which is why JPG files are so small.

Why BMP Files Are So Large

A BMP file stores every pixel individually. A 1920 by 1080 screenshot at 24-bit colour has over two million pixels, and each one needs three bytes, which totals more than 6MB.

There is no cleverness in the storage. A white background that JPG stores in a few bytes needs thousands of bytes in BMP, since every white pixel is written out in full.

Headers add a little more. The BMP header describes the dimensions, the colour depth, and the palette, but it is tiny compared to the pixel data itself.

Some BMP files use run-length compression, which helps a little for simple images. Most BMP files, though, are stored raw, with no compression at all.

How Much JPG Actually Saves

A 6MB BMP screenshot becomes a 500KB JPG at 85 percent quality. That is a 92 percent saving, with the image still looking clean.

A 10MB scanned BMP becomes a 300KB JPG at the same setting. That is a 97 percent saving, since scans are mostly white paper with dark text.

A 20MB BMP photograph becomes a 1.5MB JPG at 85 percent quality. That is a 92 percent saving, with the photo still looking good.

Simple graphics save the least. A flat icon with few colours may only shrink by half, since BMP already stores simple images relatively efficiently.

Image Quality: A Tie at Sensible Settings

At 85 to 90 percent quality, a JPG looks identical to the BMP it came from at normal viewing sizes. The discarded detail is invisible to the human eye.

At 100 percent quality, a JPG is nearly identical to the BMP at the pixel level too. The file is still far smaller, since JPG compression is efficient even at high quality.

At low quality, the difference appears. A JPG at 50 percent shows blocky artefacts and soft text, while the BMP stays perfect, since it never discards anything.

For everyday use, quality is not the deciding factor. Both formats look good at sensible settings, and the choice comes down to size and compatibility.

Colour Depth Compared

A 24-bit BMP and a JPG both use 24-bit colour, with millions of colours available. The conversion between them preserves the colour range exactly.

An 8-bit BMP uses a palette of 256 colours. Converting it to JPG expands the palette into ordinary RGB colours, and the image looks the same.

A 1-bit BMP holds only black and white. Converting it to JPG produces a black and white image with the same pixels, at a fraction of the size.

Colour depth rarely decides the format choice. The size difference is so large that JPG wins for delivery in nearly every case, regardless of depth.

Transparency: Neither Format Has It

Standard BMP files do not support transparency. Every pixel has a solid colour, and there is no alpha channel for see-through areas.

JPG does not support transparency either. It is a flat rectangle of pixels, with no way to store see-through parts.

Converting BMP to JPG therefore loses nothing on transparency. The image was opaque before, and it stays opaque after, with no visible change.

If you need transparency, neither format is the answer. Use PNG or WebP, which both store an alpha channel and keep transparent areas see-through.

Compatibility: JPG Wins Completely

JPG opens on every device, every operating system, every browser, and every upload form. There is no mainstream system that rejects a JPG.

BMP opens on Windows natively, but support elsewhere is patchy. Macs, phones, and web browsers may display BMP, yet many tools and forms reject it.

Email clients often block BMP attachments as too large. A 6MB BMP exceeds many attachment limits, while the 500KB JPG version sails through.

Websites rarely accept BMP uploads. A BMP image on a web page loads slowly and may not display at all in some browsers.

When BMP Still Makes Sense

BMP makes sense inside old Windows software that produces it natively. A legacy application may save only BMP, and converting is a separate step, not part of the workflow.

BMP makes sense as an intermediate format during editing. Some old tools work with BMP internally, since the raw pixels are fast to manipulate.

BMP makes sense for archival masters in rare cases. A lossless BMP is a perfect copy, though PNG is a better archival format, since it is lossless and far smaller.

For most people, BMP makes sense only as a source format. Convert it to JPG or PNG as soon as it leaves the program that created it.

When JPG Wins Clearly

Use JPG for sharing, emailing, and uploading. The small files send fast, upload without hitting limits, and open everywhere.

Use JPG for websites. A BMP image on a web page is slow and often unsupported, while a JPG loads quickly and displays in every browser.

Use JPG for archiving scans and screenshots. A folder of BMP scans that filled a drive becomes a folder of JPGs that fits on a USB stick.

Use JPG for any image that must work outside Windows. The moment a file leaves the Windows world, BMP becomes a liability and JPG becomes the safe choice.

A Strategy for Old BMP Files

Convert BMP files to JPG as soon as you receive them. The conversion takes seconds, the saving is enormous, and the result works everywhere.

Keep the BMP originals in an archive folder if they matter. They are the masters, and you can always convert them again if you need a different format later.

Use 85 percent quality as the default. It keeps text readable and photos clean, while shrinking the files by 90 percent or more.

For a whole archive, convert in batches. The BMP to JPG converter handles folders of scans, and the batch summary shows the total savings.

The Verdict: Which Format Wins?

JPG wins on size, compatibility, and practicality. It is smaller by 90 percent or more, it opens everywhere, and it looks identical at sensible quality settings.

BMP wins only on simplicity and lossless storage. It is a perfect copy of the pixels, which matters for archival masters and for old software that produces it.

For nearly every real use, JPG is the right format. BMP is a source format, not a delivery format, and converting it to JPG is one of the easiest wins in image work.

The numbers tell the story: a 6MB file becomes 500KB, a 10MB scan becomes 300KB, and nothing visible is lost. Convert early, convert often, and keep the originals safe.

BMP in Medical Imaging

Medical imaging software sometimes saves scans as BMP, since the format is simple and lossless. A single scan can weigh tens of megabytes, which makes storage and sharing difficult.

Convert the scans to JPG at 90 percent quality for sharing. The images stay sharp enough for review, while the files shrink dramatically and send easily.

For diagnosis, never rely on a lossy copy. A compressed scan can hide or invent details, so the original BMP or a lossless PNG is the only safe version for medical decisions.

Keep the original BMP files in a secure archive. Medical records have legal retention requirements, and the originals prove the images were converted faithfully.

Store the images with clear names and dates. A file named scan-2026-11-17.bmp is useful in a medical record years later, while a file named image001.bmp is not.

BMP in Industrial Software

Industrial software, such as machine vision and quality control systems, often saves images as BMP. The format is simple, fast to write, and easy for the software to read.

Convert the images to JPG before sharing them with colleagues. A 20MB BMP attachment exceeds most email limits, while the 500KB JPG version sends immediately.

For a quality control archive, keep the BMP originals. The lossless files are the evidence, while the JPG copies are for reports and presentations.

Automate the conversion if the volume is high. A machine that produces hundreds of images a day needs a batch process, not a manual conversion for each file.

Document the conversion settings for the team. A one-line note prevents the slow drift into mixed formats that plagues every industrial workflow.

Storage Costs of BMP

BMP files cost real money in storage. A folder of a thousand 6MB screenshots needs 6GB of disk, while the same folder as JPG needs 500MB, a twelvefold difference.

Cloud storage charges by the gigabyte, so a BMP archive costs twelve times more than a JPG archive. Converting the archive pays for itself in the first month.

Backups cost too. A BMP archive takes twelve times longer to back up, and the backup drive fills twelve times faster, which means more hardware and more time.

For a business with a large image archive, the saving is substantial. A terabyte of BMP files becomes 100GB as JPG, freeing space for years of new work.

Convert the archive in batches, verifying each batch before deleting the originals. A careful migration saves money without risking the files that matter.

BMP in Old Video Games

Old video games store textures and sprites as BMP files, since the format was the standard when the games were written. Those files are large, and modern tools prefer PNG or JPG.

Convert the game textures to PNG for editing. The lossless format preserves the pixels exactly, which matters for pixel art where every pixel is deliberate.

For sharing screenshots of old games, convert to JPG. The screenshots shrink by 90 percent, and the forums and social media handle JPG natively.

Keep the BMP originals for the game files that matter. A modder working on an old game needs the original textures, while the JPG copies are for previews and screenshots.

Respect the game's copyright when sharing converted files. A texture belongs to the game's creators, and converting the format does not change the ownership.

Migrating a BMP Archive

Migrating a BMP archive is a project, not a chore. Plan it in batches, one folder at a time, verifying each batch before moving on to the next.

Start with the least important folder, since a mistake there costs little. A successful first batch builds confidence for the folders that matter more.

Convert each batch at 85 percent quality, then verify a sample of the files. A quick check of five files per batch catches problems before they spread.

Keep the BMP originals until the whole migration is verified. Deleting them early risks losing files that cannot be recreated, while keeping them costs only disk space.

Document the migration in a short log. A line per folder, with the date and the file count, tells the story of the project and proves it was done carefully.

Frequently asked questions

Why are BMP files so huge compared to JPG?

BMP stores every pixel exactly with almost no compression, so a 1920 by 1080 screenshot needs over 6MB just for the pixels. JPG discards detail the eye barely notices, compressing the same image to 500KB. The difference is compression, not the image itself.

Does converting BMP to JPG lose image quality?

Very little, at sensible settings. At 85 to 90 percent quality, a JPG looks identical to the BMP at normal viewing sizes. The discarded detail is invisible to the human eye. Only at low quality settings, below 70 percent, does the damage become visible in text and fine detail.

Is BMP better quality than JPG for archiving?

BMP is lossless, so it is a perfect copy, but PNG is a better archival format, since it is also lossless and far smaller. JPG is lossy, so it is not ideal for masters. Keep BMP or PNG originals in an archive folder, and use JPG copies for sharing and delivery.

Can I convert BMP to JPG without losing the colours?

Yes, for practical purposes. A 24-bit BMP and a JPG both use 24-bit colour, so the conversion preserves the colour range. Very unusual palettes in old 8-bit files may shift slightly, but the difference is usually invisible. Check the result with Compare before downloading.

Why do some programs still save images as BMP?

Legacy Windows software was built around BMP, since raw pixels were fast to read and write on slow processors. The format persists in old applications, scanners, and some cameras, even though modern formats are far more efficient. Converting the files to JPG is a separate step after they leave the program.

Should I convert all my old BMP files to JPG?

Yes, for files you use, share, or upload, since the 90 percent saving and universal compatibility are worth it. Keep the BMP originals in an archive folder if they matter, since a JPG can never become a perfect BMP again. Convert the working copies, archive the masters.
