Bulk Image Resizer: Complete Guide

Resize dozens of images at once: Fit Max, Uniform Width, Exact pixels, and Percentage modes, no-upscale protection, presets, and one-click ZIP download — all in your browser.

A bulk image resizer changes the pixel dimensions of many images in one pass. Instead of opening each photo, typing a size, and downloading it separately, you drop a whole folder into the queue, choose one strategy, and download every result in a single ZIP file.

That matters because real image work is rarely one photo. A product shoot is forty photos, a blog folder is twenty, and a photo album is a hundred. The bulk image resizer turns that repetitive job into a single batch, with the same settings applied consistently to every file.

This guide explains the four resize strategies, the presets, the no-upscale protection, the format and quality controls, and how to handle mixed batches of portraits and landscapes. It also covers privacy, the ZIP workflow, and the difference from bulk compression.

What Is a Bulk Image Resizer?

A bulk image resizer applies one resize operation to a whole queue of images. You choose the strategy and the size once, and the tool processes every image in the batch with those settings. Each image keeps its own content and proportions, but all of them end up sized for the same purpose.

The key idea is consistency. A product catalogue looks professional when every image shares the same width or the same bounding box, and a blog looks tidy when every content image is 1200 pixels wide. Doing that by hand, one image at a time, invites typos and drift; a batch applies the same numbers everywhere.

Bulk resizing is not the same as resizing one image many times. The tool reads each file, draws it onto a canvas at the new size, and writes a fresh file, just as the single resizer does. The difference is only the queue: dozens of images, one set of settings, one download.

Everything runs locally in your browser, using HTML5 Canvas on your own device. Your photos are never uploaded to a server, which matters for client catalogues, unreleased products, and family albums. The batch is processed on your machine, and the ZIP is built there too.

The Four Resize Strategies

The bulk resizer offers four strategies, and choosing the right one is the main decision in the whole job. Each strategy answers a different question about what should stay the same across the batch, and the wrong choice produces a batch that looks inconsistent.

Fit Max W by H fits every image inside a bounding box, keeping its own aspect ratio. Uniform Width gives every image the same width and lets the height follow. Exact W by H forces every image to identical pixel dimensions. Percentage scales every image by the same proportion of its own size.

The strategies differ in what they guarantee. Fit Max guarantees nothing is cropped or stretched. Uniform Width guarantees a shared width. Exact guarantees identical dimensions. Percentage guarantees a consistent scale factor. Pick the guarantee your destination needs.

Fit Max W by H: The Bounding Box

Fit Max treats your width and height entries as an invisible box, defaulting to 1200 by 1200 pixels. Every photo shrinks to fit inside that box while keeping its natural aspect ratio, with zero cropping and zero stretching. The box is a limit, not a frame.

A 4000 by 2000 landscape becomes 1200 by 600 inside a 1200 by 1200 box, because the width hits the limit first. A 2000 by 4000 portrait becomes 600 by 1200, because the height hits the limit first. Both fit, both keep their shape, and neither loses any part of the picture.

Fit Max is the right strategy for mixed folders where the photos have different orientations. Product shoots, blog folders, and photo albums all mix tall and wide images, and Fit Max handles the mix without you sorting the folder first. It is the default for a reason.

Use Fit Max whenever the destination accepts varying dimensions inside a maximum size. Most websites, blogs, and marketplaces do: they display the image at its natural shape within a maximum width. The bounding box gives you that maximum, and the images do the rest.

Uniform Width: One Width for Every Image

Uniform Width gives every image in the batch the exact same width, while each height scales proportionally. Nothing is stretched or cropped; tall photos stay tall and wide photos stay wide, but they all share one horizontal measurement.

This is the strategy for vertical layouts where alignment matters. A newsletter column, a CMS article column, or a responsive gallery stream all display images in a column of fixed width, and images of different widths would look ragged. Uniform Width makes the column straight.

Choose Uniform Width when the display width is fixed and the height is free. A blog where every content image is 1200 pixels wide is the classic case: the images line up, the text column stays tidy, and each photo keeps its own natural height.

Exact W by H: Identical Dimensions

Exact W by H forces every image to the same width and height, whatever shape it started as. It is the strictest strategy, and it is the one to use when the destination demands identical dimensions, such as a product grid where every tile must be exactly the same size.

Because photos rarely share one shape, Exact needs a fit behaviour to handle the mismatch. Contain fits the full image inside the box with clean padding margins, so nothing is lost. Cover fills every pixel edge to edge by centre-cropping the overhanging sides. Stretch forces the image to the exact pixels directly, distorting it if the shapes differ.

Contain is the cautious choice for product shots where the edges carry detail. Cover is the tidy choice for grids and thumbnails where a filled frame matters more than the edges. Stretch is rarely the right answer, and should only be used when matching fixed bounds matters more than true proportions.

Percentage: Scale Everything by the Same Factor

Percentage mode multiplies every image's own width and height by your chosen factor, from 10 percent to 200 percent, with quick buttons for 25, 50, 75, 100, and 150 percent. Each image scales relative to itself, so the batch keeps its internal size relationships.

Choosing 50 percent turns a 4000 by 3000 photo into 2000 by 1500 and a 1000 by 800 icon into 500 by 400 in the same pass. The large photo stays large relative to the small one, which is what you want when the folder has a natural size hierarchy.

Percentage mode is the simplest way to halve a folder that is just too big. There are no dimensions to look up and no bounding box to think about: pick 50 percent, run the batch, and every image is half its former size in each direction, a quarter of its former pixel count.

Quick Presets for Common Sizes

Four quick preset buttons fill in common pixel sizes with one tap. They are starting points you can adjust afterwards, since the pixel fields accept any value from 1 to 12,000 pixels. Presets save typing and stop typos in the numbers that matter most.

  • Full HD 1920px: fills 1920 by 1080, the standard wide screen size.
  • Blog 1200px: sets 1200 by 1200, the bounding box for blog content images.
  • E-commerce 1000 by 1000: the square product size many marketplaces expect.
  • Thumbnail 600px: sets 600 by 600, the small preview size for lists and grids.

These presets cover the most common batch jobs: a video still at Full HD, a blog folder at 1200, a product catalogue at 1000 square, and a thumbnail set at 600. For social sizes, type the dimensions yourself: the fields accept any size, including 1080 by 1080 for a square post.

For a deeper look at choosing sizes per destination, read Bulk Resize Presets: Social, E-commerce and Print. It maps each preset to the platform or shop that expects it, and explains when a preset is wrong for the job.

No-Upscale Protection

The Do not enlarge smaller images option protects tiny files in a mixed batch. With it checked, large photos shrink to the target while small ones stay at their original dimensions, instead of being stretched up to the target size.

This matters because real folders mix resolutions. A shoot with 4000 pixel DSLR shots and 400 pixel badges would blur the badges badly if they were forced up to 1200 pixels. Leaving the protection on downscales the large photos cleanly and keeps the small files sharp.

Formats, Quality, and Matte Background

Batch resizing can convert formats at the same time. Switching Output Format from Original to WebP, JPG, or PNG resizes the dimensions and modernises the format in a single canvas pass, so a folder of mixed formats can come out uniform in both size and type.

The quality slider runs from 10 percent to 100 percent and applies to JPG and WebP output. Near 85 percent is the sweet spot for web images: crisp edges, clean gradients, and small files. Drop toward 65 to 75 percent only when the files must be very small, and raise toward 95 to 100 percent for print preparation.

Choosing JPG exposes the Matte BG colour picker, white by default. Any transparent background or soft drop shadow renders over your chosen matte colour before JPEG encoding, because JPG cannot store transparency. For logos that must stay transparent, keep PNG or WebP output instead.

Keeping Original format preserves transparency for PNG and WebP uploads, and exporting as PNG keeps alpha transparency intact across every resized item. That makes PNG the right output for logos, icons, and graphics with transparent backgrounds in a batch.

Handling Mixed Batches

Mixed batches are the normal case, not the exception. A product folder mixes landscape hero shots and portrait detail shots. A blog folder mixes wide screenshots and tall phone photos. The bulk resizer is built for that mix, and the strategy you choose decides how it is handled.

Fit Max is the best default for a mixed batch, because it respects every photo's own shape. Uniform Width works when the display is a fixed column. Exact with Cover works when the grid must be uniform and the edges are expendable. Percentage works when the folder just needs to be smaller, shapes and all.

Check the results card by card before downloading the ZIP. Each card shows the old and new pixel dimensions, so a portrait that came out wrong is visible immediately. Click any thumbnail or the Compare button to open the before-and-after viewer and drag the divider across fine detail.

If one image in the batch needs different treatment, remove it with the trash icon on its card and process it separately. The rest of the batch is unaffected, and you can add the corrected file back afterwards. The queue is flexible, not a one-shot commitment.

The ZIP Workflow

Downloading dozens of files one by one is slow and error-prone, so the bulk resizer packages the batch into one archive. Clicking Download All (ZIP) generates bulk_resized_compressmypic.zip locally in your browser, containing every successfully resized file from the queue.

The ZIP is built on your device, not on a server, so there is no upload and no waiting for a remote job. The file names inside stay clean and recognisable, which matters when the batch goes straight into a CMS or a marketplace upload form.

While processing runs, the action button shows the live progress as Resizing (X/Y), with spinning thumbnails on the cards still being processed. You always know how far along a large folder is, and a stalled card is visible rather than hidden behind a spinner.

Privacy and Local Processing

Every resize calculation, canvas draw, and ZIP bundle executes locally on your device. Confidential client catalogues, unreleased merchandise, and family albums are never sent to cloud servers, because there is no server involved in the resize at all.

That local processing has a useful side effect: resizing through a canvas redraws the pixel data without copying the raw EXIF headers. Location tags and camera serials are stripped automatically, so the resized files carry less metadata than the originals. For deliberate metadata removal, the remove metadata tool is the dedicated option.

Bulk Resize vs Bulk Compress

Bulk resizing and bulk compression sound similar, but they change different things. The bulk resizer actively changes pixel dimensions and bounding boxes, while the bulk compressor keeps the original pixel widths and heights untouched and reduces file size through compression alone.

Use the resizer when the images are the wrong size for their destination. Use the compressor when the images are the right size but too heavy. Use both, resizer first and compressor second, when a folder is both oversized and heavy, which is the usual case for camera photos.

The full comparison of the two ideas is in Resize vs Compress: What Is the Difference?. For the compressor itself, read Bulk Image Compressor: Complete Guide or open the bulk image compressor directly.

Common Mistakes

The most common mistake is picking Exact dimensions for a mixed batch without thinking about the fit mode. Exact with Stretch distorts every photo whose shape differs from the target, and the distortion is easy to miss at thumbnail size. Use Fit Max for mixed batches, or Exact with Contain or Cover deliberately chosen.

The second mistake is turning off no-upscale protection in a mixed folder. Small badges and icons stretched up to the batch target go soft, and the damage is visible in the final grid. Leave the protection on unless the grid genuinely demands uniform dimensions.

The third mistake is converting a whole batch to PNG. PNG is lossless and heavy for photos, so a batch of product photos converted to PNG can end up larger than the originals. For photos, choose WebP or JPG at 80 to 85 percent quality, and reserve PNG for graphics that need transparency.

Quick Checklist

Choose the strategy for the destination, set the size or preset, keep no-upscale protection on, pick the format and quality, check the first few results, and download the ZIP. That is the whole workflow, and it scales from five images to fifty.

  • Strategy chosen: Fit Max for mixed folders, Uniform Width for columns, Exact for grids, Percentage for simple shrinking.
  • Size set: a preset, or pixel values from 1 to 12,000, within the 25 MB per-file limit.
  • No-upscale protection on, unless the grid demands uniform dimensions.
  • Format and quality set: WebP or JPG near 85 percent for photos, PNG for transparency.
  • First results checked in the Compare view, then the ZIP downloaded.

Frequently asked questions

How many images can I resize at once in a batch?

There is no fixed count limit in the tool: you can queue dozens of images in one batch, each up to 25 MB. The practical limit is your device memory, because every image is drawn on a canvas in your browser. Very large batches of huge images may slow down on older phones, so process them in smaller groups if the browser feels sluggish.

Do I need an account or sign-up to resize images in bulk?

No. The bulk image resizer runs entirely in your browser with no account, no sign-up, and no upload. Open the page, drop your images in, choose a strategy, and download the ZIP. Nothing about your batch is stored on any server.

Can I resize images in bulk on my phone?

Yes. The bulk resizer works on mobile browsers with touch-friendly controls, and you can upload from your photo library. Keep batches modest on a phone, since large canvases use more memory, and stay within 4000 to 6000 pixels per side for the smoothest processing.

Will my original files be changed by bulk resizing?

No. The tool reads your originals and writes fresh resized files, leaving the originals untouched on your device. You can re-run the same batch with different settings without re-uploading, and you can always go back to the originals if a resize does not look right.

Can I use different settings for different images in one batch?

No, one batch uses one strategy and one set of settings for every image in the queue. If some images need different treatment, run them as a separate batch, or remove the odd ones with the trash icon on their cards and process them on their own afterwards.

What should I do if a photo looks blurry after batch resizing?

First check whether a small image was enlarged: make sure Do not enlarge smaller images is checked and re-run the batch. Then check the quality setting, since very low quality softens detail. If the source itself is low resolution, resize to a smaller target rather than enlarging it.
