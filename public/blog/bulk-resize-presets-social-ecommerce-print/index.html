Bulk Resize Presets: Social, E-commerce and Print

Choose the right bulk resize preset for each destination: social sizes, e-commerce squares, blog widths, thumbnails, Full HD, and print — plus when to type a custom size instead.

Every destination wants a different image size, and a preset is a shortcut to the right one. Instead of looking up a platform's numbers and typing them in by hand, you click a preset and the dimensions fill themselves in. The batch then applies that size to every image in the queue.

This guide maps the bulk resizer's presets and the common social sizes to the places that expect them: social feeds, online shops, blogs, thumbnails, screens, and print. It also explains when a preset is the wrong tool, and a custom size typed by hand is the right one.

Presets save time, but they do not replace judgement. A preset gives you the frame; your photos, your strategy, and your quality setting decide what the frame contains. Read the guide, then open the bulk image resizer and set up your batch.

What Presets Are

A preset is a saved size: a width and height pair that matches a common destination. Clicking it fills in the pixel fields, so there is nothing to type and nothing to mistype. Presets exist because the same sizes come up again and again, and remembering them all is a waste of effort.

The bulk resizer ships with four quick presets: Full HD 1920px, Blog 1200px, E-commerce 1000 by 1000, and Thumbnail 600px. Each one fills in the pixel fields for its size, and you can adjust either field afterwards, up to 12,000 pixels on a side.

Presets are starting points, not locked choices. If a marketplace asks for 1200 by 1200 instead of 1000 by 1000, click the E-commerce preset and change the fields. The preset gets you close in one tap, and a small edit finishes the job.

For sizes the bulk tool does not preset, such as social dimensions, type the numbers directly into the pixel fields. The fields accept any size from 1 to 12,000 pixels, so every destination size is reachable, preset or not.

Social Presets: Feeds, Stories, and Headers

Social platforms are the strictest destinations for image size, because they crop unpredictably when an image arrives wrong. A square post at 1080 by 1080 fills the feed cleanly, while a story at 1080 by 1920 fills the phone screen without black bars. Type those sizes into the bulk resizer for a social batch.

YouTube thumbnails want 1280 by 720, a wide frame that reads at small sizes. Facebook covers want 851 by 315, and X headers want 1500 by 500, both very wide and short. Those shapes are unforgiving: a photo composed for a square will lose its edges in a header frame unless you crop or reframe it first.

For a social batch, use Fit Max when the images share a shape, and Exact with Cover when the frame must be filled. Cover trims the edges to fill the frame, which suits a feed grid, while Fit Max keeps the whole image, which suits a story where nothing may be lost.

E-commerce Presets: The Square Product Shot

Online shops want consistency above all else. A listing grid where every tile is 1000 by 1000 looks professional, while a grid of mixed sizes looks careless, even when the products are identical in quality. The E-commerce preset exists to enforce that consistency across a whole catalogue.

Use Exact W by H at 1000 by 1000 with Cover for a product grid. Every tile becomes identical and filled edge to edge, and the grid lines up perfectly. Cover trims the overflow, so check that the product is centred in every shot before running the batch.

For a single product image on its own page, Fit Max at 1000 by 1000 or 1200 by 1200 is often better. The image keeps its whole shape, the customer sees the full product, and the page still loads quickly. The grid wants Exact; the product page wants Fit Max.

Watch the quality setting on product batches. Near 85 percent keeps edges crisp and colours true while keeping files light for shoppers on mobile connections. Lower quality saves little and risks making a product look worse than it is, which costs sales rather than saving bandwidth.

Blog and Web Presets: The 1200 Pixel Column

Blog content images live in a column of fixed width, usually around 1200 pixels, and the Blog preset is built for that column. Use Fit Max at 1200 by 1200 or Uniform Width at 1200 pixels, so every image is 1200 wide and each keeps its own natural height.

Uniform Width suits a blog column best, because it guarantees the shared width that makes the column straight. Fit Max suits a blog with mixed layouts, where some images are wide and some are tall, and each should fit inside the same maximum box.

Blog folders usually mix screenshots, photos, and graphics, so keep the output format on Original or convert the whole batch to WebP. WebP at 80 to 85 percent quality keeps screenshot text readable and photos light, which is exactly the combination a blog needs.

Thumbnail Presets: Small Images That Must Read

Thumbnails are small images that must still communicate, and the Thumbnail preset sets 600 by 600 for that job. Product lists, search results, and gallery grids all use thumbnails, and a consistent 600 pixel square keeps the grid tidy.

A thumbnail has different quality needs from a full image. Fine detail disappears at 600 pixels, so the subject must be large, centred, and high contrast in the frame. A wide landscape shrunk to a 600 square becomes a thin strip of unreadable detail, so crop the subject square before resizing.

Use Exact with Cover for thumbnail grids, so every tile is filled and identical. Contain leaves padding that makes some thumbnails look smaller than their neighbours, which breaks the grid. Cover trims the edges, but for a thumbnail the edges are rarely where the subject lives.

Full HD: The Wide Screen Size

Full HD at 1920 by 1080 is the standard wide screen size, and the preset fills it in with one tap. It suits hero images, video stills, presentation slides, and any image that must fill a desktop screen without softening.

A 1920 wide image is larger than a blog image needs, so do not use Full HD for content images inside an article. The extra pixels slow the page down for no visible benefit, because the display slot is only 1200 pixels wide. Match the preset to the slot, not to the largest size available.

For a batch of video stills or screenshots destined for a wide display, Full HD with Fit Max is the right combination. The images keep their wide shape, fit inside the 1920 by 1080 box, and stay sharp on a full screen. Check one still in the Compare view before downloading the ZIP.

Print Sizes: When Presets Do Not Apply

Print has no presets in the bulk resizer, because print sizes are set by inches and dots per inch rather than by screen conventions. A 6 by 4 inch print at 300 dots per inch needs 1800 by 1200 pixels, and a larger print needs proportionally more. Type those numbers into the pixel fields by hand.

Keep the quality at 95 to 100 percent for print batches, and prefer PNG or high-quality JPG output. Print shows every pixel, and the extra data is what keeps the print sharp. A web preset applied to a print job produces a soft print, because the pixel count is too low.

Never enlarge a small image to meet a print size. A 800 pixel photo stretched to 1800 pixels for print will look soft on paper, because the detail was never in the file. For print, start from the highest-resolution originals you have, and keep no-upscale protection on.

If a batch mixes print and web destinations, run two batches with different settings. One batch at 1800 pixels and high quality for print, and another at 1200 pixels and 85 percent quality for the web. Two quick batches beat one compromise that serves neither destination.

Custom Sizes: When to Type the Numbers

Presets cover the common cases, but many destinations publish their own exact sizes. A marketplace may demand 1200 by 1200, a newsletter may want 600 pixels wide, and a client may specify 2048 by 1152 for a banner. Those are custom sizes, and the pixel fields accept all of them.

Type a custom size when the destination gives you an exact number. Guessing with the nearest preset risks a rejection or an awkward crop on upload, while typing the published size guarantees the image arrives exactly as specified. The fields accept any value from 1 to 12,000 pixels.

Custom sizes also suit unusual shapes. A panoramic banner, a tall infographic, or a square avatar all have dimensions no preset covers, and the only way to reach them is to type the numbers. Keep the aspect ratio locked while typing, unless the shape itself must change.

Mixed Orientations and Presets

Presets set a size, but they do not solve the shape problem in a mixed batch. A 1000 by 1000 preset applied to a folder of landscapes and portraits produces different results depending on the strategy, and choosing the strategy is as important as choosing the preset.

Fit Max with a square preset is the safe combination for a mixed folder. Every image fits inside the 1000 by 1000 box, keeps its own shape, and nothing is cropped. The batch is consistent in its maximum size, even though the individual dimensions vary.

Exact with Cover and a square preset is the strict combination for a grid. Every image becomes exactly 1000 by 1000, filled edge to edge, with the overflow trimmed. It looks uniform, but it costs the edges of any photo whose shape differs from square.

Check one portrait and one landscape card before downloading the ZIP. The two orientations show the strategy's effect most clearly, and a problem visible in those two cards is a problem across the whole batch. Fix the strategy, not the preset, when the shapes look wrong.

Quality and Format per Preset

Each preset has a natural quality and format to pair with it. Social and web presets suit WebP or JPG near 85 percent quality, which keeps files light for fast loading. E-commerce presets suit JPG at 85 percent, which keeps product colours true and files small for shoppers.

Thumbnail presets can use slightly lower quality, near 80 percent, because fine detail is invisible at 600 pixels anyway. The savings are small per file but add up across a catalogue of hundreds of thumbnails, and the visible difference is nil.

Print sizes need the opposite treatment: quality at 95 to 100 percent and PNG or high-quality JPG output. The file will be larger, but that is correct for print, where every pixel is visible and compression artefacts show on paper.

Transparency follows the format, not the preset. A logo batch resized to any preset keeps its transparency only with PNG or WebP output, or with Original format for PNG and WebP sources. Choosing JPG for a logo batch fills the transparent areas with the matte colour, whatever the preset says.

When Not to Use a Preset

Do not use a preset when the destination gives you an exact size that differs from it. A preset that is close is still wrong if the platform rejects the image or crops it badly. Type the published size instead, and treat the preset as a starting point you have outgrown.

Do not use a square preset for a batch of wide images that must stay wide. Forcing a wide photo into a square frame with Exact and Cover throws away a third of the picture, and with Stretch it distorts the subject. Use Fit Max with a width limit instead, and let the images keep their shape.

Do not use the Full HD preset for images that will be displayed small. A 1920 pixel image in a 600 pixel slot wastes bandwidth and slows the page, with no visible benefit. Match the preset to the display slot, and save Full HD for heroes and full-screen images.

Preset Mistakes to Avoid

The most common preset mistake is applying one preset to every destination. A single image resized to 1080 by 1080 cannot serve a square post, a tall story, and a wide thumbnail at once. Resize from the original for each destination, with the preset that matches it.

The second mistake is forgetting that presets set dimensions, not quality. A 1000 by 1000 product image saved as PNG is still heavy, and a 1200 pixel blog image saved at 50 percent quality is still soft. Set the format and quality alongside the preset, every time.

The third mistake is using a preset as a substitute for checking the result. Presets fill in numbers, but they cannot see your photos. Open the Compare view on a portrait and a landscape card, confirm the shapes and the sharpness, and only then download the ZIP.

The fourth mistake is resizing a resized copy to a new preset. Each generation loses a little quality, and the loss compounds across presets. Always go back to the original file for each new size, so every preset version is as sharp as the source allows.

Preset Cheat Sheet

Keep this list near your workspace. It covers the sizes this guide recommends for each destination, and the strategy that pairs with each one.

  • Instagram Post: 1080 by 1080, Exact with Cover for a filled square feed tile.
  • Instagram Story: 1080 by 1920, Fit Max so nothing in the tall frame is lost.
  • YouTube Thumb: 1280 by 720, Fit Max for a wide thumbnail that reads small.
  • Facebook Cover: 851 by 315, Exact with Cover for the wide header frame.
  • X Header: 1500 by 500, Exact with Cover for the very wide banner frame.
  • E-commerce product: 1000 by 1000, Exact with Cover for a uniform grid.
  • Blog content: 1200 wide, Uniform Width or Fit Max for a tidy column.
  • Thumbnail: 600 by 600, Exact with Cover for identical small tiles.
  • Full HD hero: 1920 by 1080, Fit Max for a sharp wide screen image.
  • Print 6 by 4 inch: 1800 by 1200 at 300 dots per inch, typed by hand.

Frequently asked questions

Which preset should I use for a product listing?

Use the E-commerce preset at 1000 by 1000 with Exact dimensions and Cover, so every tile in the listing grid is identical and filled edge to edge. That consistency makes the shop look professional. For a single product page rather than a grid, Fit Max at 1000 or 1200 pixels is often better, because it keeps the whole product visible.

Can I use a social preset for a whole batch at once?

Yes. Type the social size, such as 1080 by 1080 for a square post, into the pixel fields and run the batch. The bulk resizer presets cover web and shop sizes, but the fields accept any size from 1 to 12,000 pixels, so every social size is reachable. Resize from the originals for each platform separately.

What preset is best for blog images?

The Blog preset at 1200 pixels, using Fit Max or Uniform Width, is the best default for blog content images. It makes every image 1200 pixels wide while each keeps its own natural height, so the article column stays tidy. Hero images are the exception: use Full HD at 1920 pixels wide for those.

Should thumbnails use the same preset as full images?

No. Thumbnails need their own size, usually 600 by 600 with Exact dimensions and Cover, so every tile in a list or grid is identical. A full image resized to 1200 pixels is too large for a thumbnail slot and slows the page down. Run a separate thumbnail batch from the originals.

Do presets change the image quality or format?

No. A preset only fills in the width and height fields; it does not touch format or quality. You still need to choose the output format and the quality slider yourself. For web presets, WebP or JPG near 85 percent quality is the usual pairing, and for print sizes, quality near 95 to 100 percent.

What size should I use for a printed photo?

Print sizes are typed by hand, not chosen from a preset. A 6 by 4 inch print at 300 dots per inch needs 1800 by 1200 pixels, and a larger print needs proportionally more. Keep quality at 95 to 100 percent, use PNG or high-quality JPG, and never enlarge a small image to reach a print size.
