Contrast for Readability: Text Over Photos

How to make text readable over photos: contrast between letters and background, darkening, overlays, panels, and testing on small screens.

Text over a photo is only useful if people can read it. A headline that vanishes into a bright sky or a caption that blends into a dark shadow fails at its only job.

Contrast is what makes text readable. It is the difference in brightness between the letters and the background behind them, and it can be fixed in the photo, in the text, or in both.

This article explains how to build that contrast. It covers darkening, overlays, panels, colour choices, and the testing habits that catch unreadable text before you publish.

Why Contrast Matters More Than Font

A beautiful font that nobody can read is a failed design. The first job of text on an image is to be read, and contrast decides whether that job gets done.

Most images are viewed on phones, in feeds, at small sizes. Text that is clear at full size on a computer can be unreadable in a feed preview, where the image is only a few centimetres wide.

Contrast also affects trust. A post with crisp, clear text looks professional, while a post with faint, low-contrast text looks careless, even when the photo itself is good.

Fix the contrast before you worry about the font. If the words are hard to read, no typeface will save them, and a plain font with strong contrast beats a fancy one with none.

Text vs Background: The Core Relationship

Readability comes from the gap between the text brightness and the background brightness. White text on a dark background has a large gap, and black text on a light background does too.

The problem comes when the background varies behind the same line. A caption that starts over a dark shadow and ends over a bright sky will be readable at one end and invisible at the other.

Check the contrast across the whole line, not just at one point. Every word should be readable, and the background should not change under the line.

When the background varies, fix the background, not the text. Darken the photo, add an overlay, or place a panel behind the words to create a consistent zone.

Choosing a Text Colour for Contrast

White is the safest colour for text on photos, because most photos have enough dark areas for it to read, and a dark outline makes it work on bright areas too.

Black works well on light, airy photos such as beaches, snow scenes, and white walls. It fails on dark photos, where it disappears into the shadows.

Bright colours such as yellow or cyan can work for short headlines, but they are harder to read in long captions. Reserve strong colours for a few words.

Use one colour for the main text and a second for emphasis at most. Mixing many colours looks chaotic, while a consistent pair keeps the design clean and readable.

Darkening the Photo Behind the Text

Darkening the whole photo is the simplest way to make white text readable. A brightness decrease of 20 to 30 percent creates a dark zone that white letters stand out against.

Use the Image Brightness Editor to darken the photo before you add text. The darker image gives the letters a clear background without hiding the photo entirely.

Check the photo after darkening. It should still be recognisable as the same image, just moodier, with the subject visible and the text clearly readable.

For a photo that must stay bright, darken only the area behind the text. A local darkening, or an overlay, keeps the rest of the image bright while the text zone goes dark.

Lightening the Photo Behind Dark Text

Lightening works the same way in reverse. A brightness increase behind black text creates a light zone that dark letters stand out against.

This suits bright, airy photos where the text should feel light and clean. A white wall or a pale sky brightened further gives black text a perfect background.

Watch the highlights when you lighten. A sky that is already bright can blow out into pure white, which is fine behind text but looks odd elsewhere in the photo.

For most photos, darkening for white text is the safer choice. Light photos are less common than dark ones, and white text on a darkened photo is the classic readable combination.

Overlays and Background Panels

An overlay is a semi-transparent layer placed over the whole photo. A dark overlay makes white text readable everywhere, while the image still shows through.

A panel is a smaller version of the same idea: a semi-transparent box placed behind the text only. A dark panel makes white text readable, and a light panel does the same for dark text.

Panels are the most reliable readability fix, because they create a consistent background that the photo cannot provide. They work on any image, no matter how busy it is.

The trade-off is coverage. A panel hides part of the photo, so keep it as small as the text allows and place it over the least important area.

Contrast for Headlines

Headlines need the strongest contrast, because they are the first thing the eye finds. A headline that is hard to read makes the viewer skip the whole image.

Use large, bold text with a strong colour gap. White bold letters on a darkened photo, or black bold letters on a light photo, are the two reliable combinations.

Add an outline or a shadow to a headline when the background varies. The effect creates a local contrast zone around each letter, which keeps the headline readable anywhere.

Test the headline at feed size. A headline that is clear at full size may be unreadable in a small preview, where the image is shown much smaller.

Contrast for Captions and Small Text

Captions and small text need even more care than headlines, because small letters are harder to read. A caption with weak contrast is invisible in a feed preview.

Use a panel behind small text. A caption over a busy photo needs the consistent background that only a panel can provide, since outlines alone may not be enough.

Keep captions short. A few words with strong contrast work far better than a long sentence with weak contrast, which nobody will read anyway.

Increase the size if the contrast cannot be fixed. Larger letters are easier to read at the same contrast level, so a size bump can rescue a caption that is slightly too faint.

Testing Readability Before You Publish

Test the text on a phone, in a feed, at the size it will be shown. Export the image, open it on your phone, and check whether the words are readable without zooming.

Check the image in different light too. A phone screen in bright sunlight washes out faint text, so a caption that is readable indoors may fail outdoors.

Ask someone else to read it. A fresh pair of eyes will spot unreadable text far faster than the person who made it, because the maker already knows what the words say.

Squint at the image. If the text is still visible when you squint, the contrast is strong enough. If it vanishes, the contrast needs work.

Contrast for Social Media Text

Social feeds are crowded and fast, and text must be readable at a glance. A post with weak contrast gets scrolled past, while a post with strong contrast stops the thumb.

Keep social text large and high contrast. Headlines need a size near the top of the range, with a strong colour gap against the photo behind them.

Remember that social platforms compress your images. A faint caption may become invisible after compression, so test the text on a downloaded copy of the post.

Consider whether the text belongs on the image at all. A clean photo with the words in the caption may perform better than a marked image with unreadable text.

Contrast for Slides and Presentations

Slides are viewed from a distance, so the text needs very strong contrast. A headline that is readable on a laptop may be invisible from the back of a room.

Darken the photo behind slide text generously. A brightness decrease of 30 percent or more gives white text a clear background, even on a large projection screen.

Use large, bold text for slides. A headline should be readable from the back row, which means bigger letters and stronger contrast than a social post needs.

Test the slide on the actual projector if you can. Projectors wash out colours and reduce contrast, so a slide that looks fine on a screen may fail in the room.

Accessibility: Contrast Everyone Can Read

Good contrast helps everyone, but it matters most for people with low vision. High contrast, large size, and a plain background make text readable for far more people.

Avoid relying on colour alone to convey meaning. A red word and a green word may look identical to a colourblind viewer, so use size or weight as well.

Keep the text short and the language simple. A clear caption in plain words is accessible to more readers than a clever caption full of jargon.

Add alt text when you share the image. The alt text describes the image and repeats the caption, which helps people who use screen readers.

Common Contrast Mistakes

The most common mistake is white text on a bright photo with no outline, shadow, or panel. The letters vanish into the sky, and the viewer sees only the photo.

Another mistake is text placed over a face or a busy pattern. The eye cannot separate the letters from the texture underneath, and the words become hard to follow.

People also use too many colours. Three or four text colours on one image look chaotic, while one consistent colour with strong contrast keeps the design clean.

Finally, do not place text where the platform will crop it. Social apps trim the edges of images for different screens, so keep important words well inside the frame.

Contrast, Filters, and Text Together

Filters change the contrast of a photo, which affects the text on top of it. A high-contrast filter can make the background busier, which hurts readability.

Apply the filter first, then check the text contrast. A filter that darkens the photo may improve white text, while one that brightens it may ruin the same text.

For text-heavy graphics, use a mild filter or none at all. A clean photo with strong text contrast beats a heavily filtered photo with unreadable words.

When a filter and text must coexist, add a panel behind the text. The panel restores the consistent background that the filter took away.

Before and After: What Good Contrast Looks Like

A photo with white text over a bright sky and no outline is the classic failure. The words are invisible, and the viewer sees only the photo.

The same photo with a dark overlay becomes readable instantly. The sky darkens, the white letters stand out, and the image still shows through the overlay.

A caption over a busy crowd with a panel behind it is the reliable fix. The panel creates a clean zone, and the words are readable without hiding the whole photo.

Compare your work with these examples. If your text looks like the failures, add an outline, a shadow, a panel, or a darkening step, and check again.

A Readability Checklist Before You Publish

Check the contrast across the whole line of text. Every word should be readable, not just the first few, and the background should not change under the line.

Check the size on a phone in a feed preview. If the words are hard to read without zooming, increase the size or shorten the text.

Check the text in bright sunlight. A caption that is readable indoors may fail outdoors, where the screen washes out faint letters.

Check the placement. The text should sit over a plain area, away from faces and edges, with enough space around it to breathe.

Frequently asked questions

How do I make white text readable on a bright photo?

Darken the photo behind the text, add a dark outline or shadow around the letters, or place a semi-transparent dark panel behind the words. A brightness decrease of 20 to 30 percent usually creates enough contrast for white text to stand out clearly.

Should I darken the whole photo or just the area behind the text?

Darkening the whole photo is simpler and suits slides and posters. A panel or overlay behind the text only is better when the rest of the photo must stay bright. Both work; choose based on whether the photo itself or the text matters more.

What contrast level do I need for text over a photo?

As much as you can get. White text on a dark background and black text on a light background are the two reliable combinations. Check the whole line, since a background that varies under the text can make one end readable and the other invisible.

Why does my text look fine on my computer but unreadable on my phone?

The phone shows the image much smaller, especially in a feed preview, and small text with weak contrast vanishes at that size. Test the image on your phone at feed size, increase the text size, and strengthen the contrast with an outline or panel.

Does a filter affect how readable text is on a photo?

Yes. Filters change the contrast and brightness of the photo, which changes the background the text sits on. Apply the filter first, then check the text contrast. Add a panel behind the text if the filter made the background busier.

How do I test text contrast before publishing?

View the image on your phone at feed size, check it in bright sunlight, and squint at it: if the text is still visible when you squint, the contrast is strong enough. Ask someone else to read it too, since a fresh pair of eyes spots weak contrast fast.
