HEIC vs JPG: Compatibility, Quality and File Size

HEIC vs JPG compared honestly: file size, image quality, compatibility, and which format to use for photos, forms, and the web.

HEIC and JPG are the two formats that matter most for phone photos. One is modern and efficient, the other is old and universal, and choosing between them is a trade-off, not a clear win.

Apple chose HEIC for iPhones because it halves file sizes. The rest of the world still runs on JPG because it opens everywhere.

This comparison looks at both formats honestly, so you can decide which one suits each situation.

HEIC at a Glance

HEIC is based on the HEIF container with HEVC compression, the same technology used in modern video. It stores photos efficiently and supports features JPG cannot.

A HEIC photo holds the same visible quality as a JPG in roughly half the bytes. That is why iPhones switched to it: twice as many photos in the same storage.

HEIC also stores depth maps, burst sequences, and Live Photo video in the same file. A portrait photo and its depth data travel together, which JPG cannot do.

The format supports 16-bit colour and transparency. Those features matter to professionals, though everyday users rarely notice them.

JPG at a Glance

JPG has been the standard photo format since 1992. Every camera, phone, computer, printer, website, and upload form built in the last thirty years can open it.

The format uses lossy compression based on grouping pixels into blocks. It is old technology, and it is less efficient than HEIC, but it is understood by everything.

JPG supports only 8-bit colour and no transparency. A JPG photo is a flat, simple image, which is exactly why it is so reliable.

The format's age is its strength. Nothing in the JPG world is new or surprising, and no system rejects a JPG for being unfamiliar.

File Size: HEIC Wins Clearly

A typical 12-megapixel iPhone photo is about 2MB as a HEIC and about 4MB as a JPG at 90 percent quality. The HEIC is half the size for the same visible quality.

The gap holds across photo types. Landscapes, portraits, and indoor shots all compress better in HEIC, because the modern encoder understands image structure better.

For storage, this is a large win. A phone with 128GB of space holds roughly twice as many HEIC photos as JPG photos, which is a real difference over years.

For sharing, the win disappears. A HEIC file that the recipient cannot open is useless, no matter how small it is, so compatibility often outweighs the size saving.

Image Quality: Nearly a Tie

At the same file size, HEIC looks better than JPG. At the same visible quality, HEIC is smaller. Either way you measure it, HEIC is the more efficient format.

At high quality settings, the difference is invisible. A JPG at 90 percent and a HEIC at equivalent quality look identical on screen and in print.

The difference appears at low quality. A JPG at 60 percent shows blocky artefacts in skies and skin, while a HEIC at the same size stays cleaner.

For everyday photos, quality is not the deciding factor. Both formats look good at sensible settings, and the choice comes down to compatibility and size.

Compatibility: JPG Wins Clearly

JPG opens on every device, every operating system, every browser, and every upload form. There is no mainstream system that rejects a JPG.

HEIC opens on Apple devices and recent Android phones, but not on Windows by default, not on many Linux desktops, and not on most upload forms.

Websites are split. Modern browsers can display HEIC in some contexts, but most content systems and social platforms convert or reject it on upload.

Printers and photo labs are also split. Some accept HEIC, but many still ask for JPG, and a print order that bounces back is a wasted trip.

Features Beyond the Photo

HEIC stores Live Photos, keeping the still image and the short video together. JPG has no equivalent, so a Live Photo converted to JPG loses its motion.

HEIC stores depth data for portrait mode, which lets you adjust the background blur after taking the photo. A JPG portrait bakes the blur in permanently.

HEIC supports 16-bit colour, which preserves subtle gradients better than JPG's 8-bit colour. The difference matters for editing, though not for casual viewing.

HEIC also supports transparency, which JPG cannot do at all. That feature is rarely used for photos, but it exists for graphics saved in the format.

When to Use HEIC

Use HEIC for storage on Apple devices. Your iPhone camera roll, your Mac photo library, and your iCloud storage all benefit from the smaller files.

Use HEIC when you share within the Apple ecosystem. An AirDrop between iPhones, or a shared iCloud album, keeps the format and its features intact.

Use HEIC when you edit photos professionally on a Mac. The 16-bit colour and depth data give editing apps more to work with than a JPG does.

Use HEIC as your master format. Keep the originals as HEIC, and convert copies to JPG only when a specific destination demands it.

When to Use JPG

Use JPG whenever the recipient is unknown. A form, a client, a printer, or a website with unclear rules will always accept JPG.

Use JPG for Windows users. Windows does not decode HEIC by default, so a JPG is the only photo format guaranteed to open there.

Use JPG for upload forms, job portals, and government websites. These systems list JPG and PNG as accepted formats, and a HEIC file bounces straight back.

Use JPG for the web when you are unsure about browser support. A JPG image on a page loads everywhere, while a HEIC image may not load at all.

Converting Between HEIC and JPG

Converting HEIC to JPG is easy and safe at 90 percent quality. The photo keeps its resolution and looks the same, though the file gets bigger.

Converting JPG to HEIC is possible in some tools, but it is rarely worth it. The JPG damage is already baked in, and the HEIC version is not better than the JPG it came from.

Always convert from the HEIC original, not from a previously converted JPG. Each lossy step adds damage, so the original is the best source.

Keep the HEIC originals after converting. They are the best-quality copies, and you can convert them again if you need a different format later.

A Storage Strategy for Both Formats

Keep your masters as HEIC on your iPhone or Mac. They take half the space, and they hold the depth data and Live Photo video.

Convert copies to JPG only when you need to share outside Apple. A JPG for the form, a JPG for the Windows user, a JPG for the print shop.

Do not convert your whole library to JPG just in case. That doubles your storage use for no benefit, since most photos will never leave the Apple world.

Back up the HEIC originals, not the JPG copies. If a JPG is lost, you can make another from the HEIC, but the reverse is not true.

HEIC and JPG on the Web

Websites should serve JPG or WebP, not HEIC. Most browsers cannot display HEIC images on a page, so a HEIC photo on a website is invisible to many visitors.

WebP is the modern alternative for the web, beating JPG on size while keeping compatibility with modern browsers. It is the format to choose for new websites.

JPG remains the safe fallback for the web. When a browser does not support WebP, the JPG version loads instead, and every visitor sees the image.

For a personal blog or shop, convert HEIC photos to JPG or WebP before uploading. The JPG to WebP converter shrinks them further for fast pages.

The Verdict: Which Format Wins?

HEIC wins on efficiency and features. It is smaller, richer, and more capable, and it is the better format in purely technical terms.

JPG wins on compatibility and simplicity. It opens everywhere, it is understood by every system, and it never surprises you with a rejection.

For most people, the answer is both: HEIC for storage, JPG for sharing. Keep the masters in HEIC, and convert copies to JPG when the destination demands it.

That strategy gives you the best of both formats without the worst of either. Small files at home, universal files everywhere else.

HEIC on Android Phones

Android phones handle HEIC better than Windows does, since recent Android versions include a HEIC decoder. Most modern Android phones display iPhone photos without any conversion.

Older Android phones are less reliable. A phone from five years ago may show a broken icon instead of the photo, and the owner has no idea why.

Some Android camera apps save photos as HEIC too, following Apple's lead. Those files face the same compatibility problems when shared with Windows users or upload forms.

For sharing from Android to anywhere else, convert to JPG first. The JPG opens on every device, and the conversion takes seconds in the phone browser.

Check the Android version before assuming support. Android 9 and later generally handle HEIC, while earlier versions do not, and the difference matters for older devices.

HEIC in Cloud Storage

Cloud storage services handle HEIC inconsistently. Google Photos displays HEIC natively, while other services may show a thumbnail but fail to open the full image.

iCloud stores HEIC natively, since it is Apple's format. Photos stay in HEIC in iCloud, and they convert automatically when downloaded to a non-Apple device.

Google Drive stores HEIC files as they are, without converting them. A HEIC file downloaded from Drive to a Windows computer still needs converting before it opens.

Dropbox previews HEIC files in the browser but may struggle with full-resolution viewing. Downloading the file and converting it locally is more reliable.

For a cloud archive, keep the HEIC originals and convert copies for sharing. The originals are the best-quality versions, and the cloud is a safe place to store them.

Editing HEIC vs Editing JPG

Editing a HEIC file preserves more detail than editing a JPG, since HEIC stores 10-bit or 12-bit colour while JPG stores only 8-bit. The extra depth gives editing apps more to work with.

Most phone editing apps handle HEIC natively. The iPhone Photos app edits HEIC files without converting them, preserving the quality through every adjustment.

Desktop editors are mixed. Photoshop handles HEIC now, but many older editors do not, so a HEIC file may need converting to JPG before it can be edited on a computer.

For serious editing, convert the HEIC to PNG rather than JPG. PNG is lossless, so repeated edits do not degrade it, while each JPG save adds a little damage.

For casual edits such as cropping and filters, the format hardly matters. A quick crop on a JPG is fine, since the damage from one save is invisible.

The Future of Phone Photo Formats

Phone photo formats will keep changing. HEIC is the current standard on iPhones, but AVIF and JPEG XL are competing for the same role, and the next iPhone may switch again.

JPG will remain the universal fallback throughout. It is thirty years old and supported by every system ever built, while newer formats take years to reach the same ubiquity.

Websites will adopt the newest formats first, since they benefit most from smaller files. Upload forms and printers will adopt them last, since they change slowly and value stability.

For photographers, the lesson is to keep masters in the best format available and convert copies for each destination. That strategy survives any format change.

For everyone else, the lesson is simpler: when a photo will not open, convert it to JPG. That advice has worked for thirty years, and it will work for thirty more.

HEIC and JPG in Messaging Apps

Messaging apps handle HEIC inconsistently. WhatsApp converts HEIC photos to JPG automatically, while Telegram may send the original file unchanged, and the recipient's experience varies.

For a photo sent to a group, convert to JPG first. A JPG opens for everyone in the group, while a HEIC file fails for the members on Windows or older phones.

Check the photo quality after the app processes it. Messaging apps compress images aggressively, so a photo sent as JPG may arrive softer than the original.

For a photo that must stay sharp, send it as a document rather than as a photo. A document attachment skips the app's compression, preserving the full quality.

After sending, keep the HEIC original on your phone. It is the best-quality copy, and you can convert it again if a different recipient needs a different format.

Frequently asked questions

Is HEIC better quality than JPG?

At the same file size, yes, HEIC looks better, because its modern compression is more efficient. At the same visible quality, HEIC is about half the size. At high quality settings, though, the two look identical to the human eye, so quality alone is rarely the deciding factor.

Should I keep my photos as HEIC or convert them all to JPG?

Keep the masters as HEIC and convert copies to JPG only when sharing outside Apple. Converting your whole library doubles your storage use for no benefit, since most photos never leave your devices. Convert per need, not in bulk, unless a specific job demands it.

Can Windows open HEIC files without converting them?

Windows can, but only after installing the HEIF codec from the Microsoft Store, and many users never do. Even with the codec, some programs still reject HEIC. Converting to JPG is faster and more reliable than asking every Windows recipient to install a codec.

Does converting HEIC to JPG lose the Live Photo video?

Yes. The conversion exports the still key photo as a JPG and discards the short video clip. If the motion matters, keep the original HEIC or share the Live Photo from the Photos app, where the video survives. The JPG is a still image only.

Which format is better for uploading to a website?

JPG is the safe choice, since every website accepts it, and WebP is the modern choice for faster pages. HEIC is the wrong choice for the web, because most browsers cannot display it. Convert HEIC photos to JPG or WebP before uploading them anywhere.

Will HEIC replace JPG eventually?

Probably not soon. JPG is thirty years old and supported by every system ever built, while HEIC still lacks universal support after years on iPhones. Newer formats such as AVIF and WebP are also competing for the same role. For now, JPG remains the universal photo format.
