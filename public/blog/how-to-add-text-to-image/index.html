How to Add Text to an Image

Add text to any image in minutes: upload, type your caption, pick a font and size, set the colour and outline, position the layers, and download.

Adding text to a photo takes only a few minutes. You upload the image, type your words, choose a font and a colour, and place the text where it belongs.

This how-to walks through the whole process step by step. It covers single and multiple layers, font and size choices, outlines and shadows, and the export settings that keep text sharp.

By the end you will be able to caption a photo, make a meme, label a screenshot, or design a social graphic on any device. Keep the original photo until you have checked the result.

Before You Start: Plan Your Words

Write your text before you open the tool. A caption decided in advance is shorter and clearer than one invented while staring at the editor.

Keep it short. A headline of a few words and a caption of one line work far better than a paragraph, which hides the photo and overwhelms the viewer.

Decide the mood as well. A bold font suits a meme or a sale banner, while a light serif suits a wedding photo or a quote graphic.

Open the Add Text to Image tool in your browser. Everything runs locally, so your photo is never uploaded to a server.

Add Text to an Image in Six Steps

The process is the same for a caption, a meme, or a label. Upload, type, style, position, check, and download.

Take your time with the styling step. Font, size, colour, and outline decide whether the text is readable or whether it vanishes into the photo.

  1. Upload your image by dragging it into the dropzone, choosing it from your device, or pasting a screenshot with Ctrl+V.
  2. Click Add Text and type your caption in the text content box, using Enter for extra lines.
  3. Choose a font, set the text size between 2% and 22%, and pick a colour that contrasts with the photo.
  4. Add a text outline or drop shadow if the background is busy, then drag the layer into position.
  5. Add more layers for extra lines, selecting each one separately to style it on its own.
  6. Choose the export format and quality, then click Download to save the captioned image.

Open the downloaded file once before you share it. Confirm the text reads clearly and nothing important in the photo is covered.

Adding Your First Text Layer

Click Add Text to create your first layer. The tool places a default caption on the image, and you replace it with your own words in the text content box.

Type the words exactly as you want them to appear. Spelling mistakes on an image are hard to miss, so read the text twice before moving on.

The layer appears in the centre of the image by default. Drag it to the position you want, such as the top for a headline or the bottom for a caption.

Each layer is independent. You can style one layer in a large bold font and another in a small light font, without the two affecting each other.

Choosing a Font

The tool includes a set of built-in fonts, from bold sans-serifs to classic serifs. Pick the one that matches the mood of your image and your message.

For memes and sale banners, use a bold sans-serif. The heavy letters stay readable at small sizes and on busy backgrounds, which is exactly what a meme needs.

For quotes and wedding photos, use a serif or an elegant font. The lighter strokes feel formal and calm, which suits a reflective message.

Check the font at the size you will use it. A decorative font that looks beautiful at large sizes can become unreadable when it is small.

Setting the Text Size

Text size runs from 2 percent to 22 percent of the image. A headline needs a larger size near the top of the range, while a caption or a credit line needs a smaller one.

Percentage sizing keeps the text consistent across different image sizes. The same setting looks right on a small thumbnail and a huge camera photo.

Test the size at the dimensions you will share. A headline that looks bold at full size may be tiny in a social feed preview, where the image is shown much smaller.

When you have two layers, size them differently. A large headline with a smaller caption creates a clear hierarchy, and the viewer knows what to read first.

Choosing a Text Colour

White text works on dark backgrounds and black text works on light ones, but most photos contain both. Check the colour against the actual area where the text will sit.

Place the text over the plainest part of the photo. A caption over a clear sky or a blank wall is far easier to read than one over a busy crowd.

Bright colours can work for short headlines, but they are harder to read in long captions. Reserve strong colours for a few words, and use white or black for the rest.

If no colour works, add a background panel. A semi-transparent dark box behind white text makes it readable anywhere, though it covers more of the image.

Adding an Outline or Drop Shadow

An outline draws a border around each letter. A thin dark outline around white text keeps it readable on a bright sky, which is why memes use it.

A drop shadow lifts the text off the background. It adds a soft dark edge behind the letters, which helps them stand out without the hard look of an outline.

Use one or the other, not both at full strength. An outline plus a heavy shadow makes the text look cluttered, while a single effect keeps it clean.

For classic meme text, use white letters with a thick dark outline. The combination is readable on almost any background, which is why it became the standard.

Moving and Selecting Layers

Drag a layer to move it anywhere on the image. The tool lets you select each layer separately when you have several, so you can edit one without touching the others.

Click the layer you want to edit before changing its font, size, or colour. Editing the wrong layer is a common mistake, and it is easy to avoid by checking which layer is active.

Top and bottom are the classic positions for captions. A headline at the top and a punchline at the bottom is the meme format, and it works because the eye reads top to bottom.

Keep text away from the edges. A caption that touches the border looks cramped, and platforms often crop the edges again for different screen sizes.

Rotating Text Layers

Text layers can be tilted at custom angles, which adds energy to a design. A slightly diagonal headline feels dynamic, while straight text feels calm and formal.

Use rotation sparingly. A small tilt of a few degrees adds character, while a steep angle makes the text hard to read and looks like a mistake.

Diagonal text works well on posters and social graphics, where the design is meant to catch the eye. It works poorly on instructions and labels, where clarity matters most.

Check rotated text at the size it will be viewed. A tilt that looks fine at full size can make small text unreadable in a feed preview.

Multi-Line Captions and Meme Layouts

Press Enter inside the text content box to create a new line in the same layer. This lets you write a caption that spans two or three lines without extra layers.

For a meme, use one layer for the top caption and one for the bottom. Separate layers let you position each line exactly where it belongs.

Keep lines short. A caption that runs across the whole width of the image is hard to read, while short lines centred on the subject are easy to follow.

Break lines at natural pauses in the sentence. A line break in the middle of a phrase feels awkward, while a break between phrases feels deliberate.

Making a Meme in Minutes

A meme is a photo with a short, funny caption, usually in white text with a thick dark outline at the top and the bottom. The format is simple, and that simplicity is the point.

Upload the photo, add a top layer for the setup and a bottom layer for the punchline. Keep each line to a few words, so the joke lands quickly.

Use a bold, chunky font with a dark outline. The classic meme look stays readable on almost any background, which is why it became the standard.

Preview the meme at the size it will be shared. A joke that is clear at full size may be unreadable in a small feed preview, so check before you post.

Previewing and Exporting

Always preview before downloading. Check that the text reads clearly, that nothing important in the photo is covered, and that the layers are in the right places.

Choose JPG for photos with text at a high quality setting. Text edges can soften slightly under JPG compression, so keep the quality high for small captions.

Choose PNG for screenshots and graphics with text. PNG is lossless, so the edges of letters stay sharp, and it preserves transparency when you need it.

Remember that the quality slider hides for PNG, because PNG is lossless and has no quality setting to adjust. That is normal behaviour, not a problem with the tool.

Adding Text on a Phone or Tablet

The tool works on touchscreens, so you can caption a photo on the phone that took it. Upload from your gallery, type the text, and drag the layer into place with your finger.

Typing on a phone keyboard is slower, so write your caption before you open the tool. A short, planned caption is easier to type than one invented on the spot.

Dragging layers with a finger is less precise than with a mouse. Use the centre, top, and bottom areas as guides, and zoom in to check the placement.

Downloaded files save to your usual downloads folder. From there you can share the captioned image directly to a chat app, an email, or a social post.

Troubleshooting Common Text Problems

If the text is unreadable, the colour probably matches the background. Change the colour, add an outline, or move the layer to a plainer part of the photo.

If you edited the wrong layer, click the layer you meant to change and try again. The tool selects layers one at a time, so check which one is active before styling.

If a layer will not move, check whether you selected it first. A layer must be active before it can be dragged, and clicking it once usually selects it.

If the text looks blurry after export, the JPG quality setting may be too low. Re-export at a higher quality, or switch to PNG for the sharpest text edges.

Quick Tips for Better Text on Images

Keep the text short. A few well-chosen words strengthen an image, while a paragraph crammed onto a photo hides the very thing it was meant to describe.

Use one font for the headline and one for the caption at most. Mixing many typefaces looks chaotic, while a consistent pair keeps the design clean.

Check the text at the size it will be viewed. A caption that looks fine at full size can be unreadable in a social feed preview.

Save the original photo without text. Once text is flattened into an export, it cannot be removed from that file, so the original is your only clean copy.

Adding Text to a Presentation Slide Image

A photo used as a slide background often needs a title on top. Add the title as a text layer, using a large bold font that stays readable from the back of the room.

Place the title over the plainest part of the photo. A headline over a clear sky or a blank wall is far easier to read than one over a busy crowd.

Use a dark outline or a shadow when the background is busy. The effect lifts the letters off the photo just enough to read, without hiding the image underneath.

Export the slide image as JPG at a high quality setting. The text needs to stay sharp when the slide is projected, and a low quality export will soften the letters.

Frequently asked questions

How do I add text to a photo on my phone?

Open the Add Text to Image tool in your phone browser, upload the photo from your gallery, and tap Add Text. Type your caption, choose a font and size, drag the layer into position with your finger, then download the result.

Can I add two different captions to one image?

Yes. Add a separate layer for each caption, such as one at the top and one at the bottom. Select each layer on its own to style it differently, so the headline can be large and bold while the caption stays smaller.

Why does my text look blurry after I download the image?

The JPG quality setting is probably too low, which softens the edges of the letters. Re-export at a higher quality setting, or switch to PNG, which is lossless and keeps text edges sharp.

How do I make a classic meme with top and bottom text?

Upload the photo, add one layer for the top line and another for the bottom line, and use a bold sans-serif font in white with a thick dark outline. Keep each line to a few words so the joke lands quickly.

Can I move text after I have typed it?

Yes. Select the layer and drag it anywhere on the image. You can move, resize, recolour, and rotate a layer at any time before you download. Once the image is exported, the text is flattened in and cannot be moved.

What size should text be for an Instagram post?

Large enough to read in a small feed preview, not just at full size. Headlines need a size near the top of the 2 to 22 percent range. Test the result by viewing the exported image at the size it will appear in the feed.
