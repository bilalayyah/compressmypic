How to Adjust Image Brightness

Adjust image brightness in minutes: upload, drag the slider, try a preset, compare before and after, and download a clean, well-lit photo.

Adjusting brightness takes only a few minutes. You upload the photo, drag a slider, check the result, and download the brighter version.

This how-to walks through the whole process step by step. It covers the slider, the quick presets, the compare modes, and the export settings that keep the result looking natural.

By the end you will be able to fix a dark portrait, a dull landscape, or a murky screenshot on any device. Keep the original photo until you have checked the result.

Before You Start: Judge How Dark It Is

Look at the photo before you open the tool. Is it slightly dark, very dark, or only dark in one area? The answer tells you how much brightening it needs.

A slightly dark photo needs 10 to 15 percent. A very dark one may need 30 percent or more, but large moves risk washing out the highlights.

Check whether the photo is also flat or dull. A dark, flat photo needs brightness and contrast together, so plan for both edits, not just one.

Open the Image Brightness Editor in your browser. Everything runs locally, so your photo is never uploaded to a server.

Adjust Brightness in Five Steps

The process is the same for a photo, a screenshot, or a scanned document. Upload, adjust, compare, export, and verify the download.

Take your time with the slider. Small moves make a big difference, and the preview shows you exactly when the photo looks right.

  1. Upload your image by dragging it into the dropzone, choosing it from your device, or pasting a screenshot with Ctrl+V.
  2. Drag the Brightness Level slider to the right to brighten or to the left to darken, watching the live preview.
  3. Try a quick preset for a one-click test, then fine-tune with the slider if the result is close but not quite right.
  4. Use the After, Before, Split Compare, or Fullscreen Compare modes to check the result against the original.
  5. Choose the export format and quality, then click Download to save the adjusted image.

Open the downloaded file once before you use it. Confirm the photo looks naturally lit and no bright areas turned white.

Using the Brightness Slider

The slider runs from negative to positive values, with zero meaning no change. Moving it right brightens the image, and moving it left darkens it.

Drag slowly and watch the preview. The live update shows exactly how the image responds, so you can stop at the point where the photo looks right.

Small moves are better than large ones. A change of 10 to 15 percent is often enough, and larger moves quickly wash out the highlights.

The tool previews your photo at plus 15 percent right after upload. That starting point gives you an immediate sense of what a moderate brightening looks like.

Using the Quick Presets

The tool includes quick presets that apply common brightness values in one click. They are a fast way to test different levels without dragging the slider.

Click a preset and look at the preview. If the result is close but not quite right, fine-tune with the slider from that point.

Use presets to compare options quickly. A preset at plus 15 percent next to one at plus 30 percent shows you how much brightening the photo can take.

For a batch of similar photos, the same preset keeps the results consistent. A set of dark indoor shots all brightened by the same amount looks like a series.

Using the Compare Modes

Compare modes show you the original and the edited version together. The After view shows the brightened image, and the Before view shows the original.

Split Compare is the most useful for brightness work. It shows both versions side by side with a clear line between them, so you can see exactly what changed.

Fullscreen Compare gives you the largest view. It is the best way to judge a brightened photo, because the full-size image shows details that a small preview hides.

Check the compare view before every download. A quick look catches over-brightening that a small preview might hide.

Brightening a Dark Photo

Upload the dark photo and drag the slider to plus 15 percent. Check the preview: the subject should be clearly visible, and the bright areas should still show detail.

If the photo is still too dark, increase the slider in small steps of 5 percent. Each step adds a little more light, and the preview shows when to stop.

Watch the highlights as you go. The forehead, the sky, and any white surfaces will blow out first, and a photo with white patches looks worse than a dark one.

If the photo looks flat after brightening, add contrast. Open the Image Contrast Editor and apply a small boost to restore depth.

Darkening a Bright Photo

Drag the slider to the left for a photo that is too bright. Negative values like minus 15 or minus 30 percent deepen the shadows and restore richness.

Check the shadows as you darken. They should gain depth without turning into solid black patches with no detail at all.

Darkening also helps when a photo will sit behind text. A darkened image makes white text readable, which suits slides, posters, and social graphics.

For a photo that is both too bright in the sky and too dark in the foreground, accept a compromise. Brightness moves everything together, so neither direction alone will fix both areas.

Fixing a Dark Screenshot

Paste a screenshot directly with Ctrl+V to skip the save step. The image appears in the editor immediately, ready to brighten.

Increase the brightness until the text is clear and the background is white. A document with a clean white background and dark text is easy to read and to print.

Watch for noise in dark areas. A heavily brightened screenshot can show faint block patterns, especially in a JPEG that was already compressed.

Export screenshots as PNG. The lossless format keeps the small text sharp, which matters when the screenshot shows a setting or an error message.

Previewing Before You Download

Always preview before downloading. Check that the photo looks naturally lit, that no bright areas turned white, and that the subject is clearly visible.

Use the split compare view to check the change. It shows the original and the edited version side by side, so you can see exactly what the brightness did.

Zoom into the bright areas at full size. A sky or a forehead that looks fine in a small preview may be blown out when viewed large.

If the result looks washed out, reduce the slider by 5 percent and check again. Small reductions often restore the detail without making the photo dark again.

Choosing the Export Format

Choose JPG for photos you plan to share. It keeps file sizes small and works everywhere, and a high quality setting keeps the brightened photo looking clean.

Choose PNG for screenshots and graphics with text. PNG is lossless, so the edges of letters stay sharp, and it preserves transparency when you need it.

Choose WebP when you want small files with good quality. It works well for web images, though a few older systems still reject the format.

Remember that the quality slider hides for PNG, because PNG is lossless and has no quality setting to adjust. That is normal behaviour, not a problem with the tool.

Adjusting Brightness on a Phone or Tablet

The tool works on touchscreens, so you can fix a dark photo on the phone that took it. Upload from your gallery, drag the slider with your finger, and download the result.

Dragging a slider with a finger is less precise than with a mouse. Use the quick presets for exact values, then fine-tune with small drags.

Check the preview at full size before downloading. A brightness change that looks fine on a small phone screen may be too strong when viewed on a computer.

Downloaded files save to your usual downloads folder. From there you can share the brightened photo directly to a chat app, an email, or a social post.

Troubleshooting Common Brightness Problems

If the photo looks washed out, the slider is too high. Reduce it in small steps until the highlights show detail again, then check the shadows.

If the photo is still dark after brightening, the original may be too dark to rescue. A photo that is almost black cannot be fixed by brightness alone, and a retake is the better option.

If noise appears in the shadows, the brightening is too strong for the source. Reduce the slider and accept a slightly darker photo, or start from a better original.

If the preview looks different from the download, check the export format and quality. A low JPG quality setting can soften the whole image, including the bright areas.

Brightness Plus Contrast: The Common Pair

A photo that is both dark and flat needs brightness and contrast together. Brighten first to lift the overall level, then add contrast to restore depth.

Use the Image Contrast Editor after brightening when the image looks grey and lifeless. A small contrast boost brings back the shadows and the highlights.

Do the steps in order: brightness first, then contrast. Reversing them can make the contrast adjustment harder to judge, because the image is still dark.

Check the compare view after each step. The split view shows exactly what each adjustment did, so you can stop when the photo looks right.

Saving Your Settings for Next Time

Note down the brightness value that worked for a set of similar photos. A short note, such as indoor shots plus 20 percent, saves you from guessing next time.

For a batch of photos taken in the same light, use the same value for all of them. Consistent brightness across a set looks professional, while mixed values look random.

Keep the original files. A brightness value that works for one photo may not suit another, and the original lets you try a different level without extra damage.

Review your notes occasionally. As your camera and your lighting improve, the brightness values you need will change, and old notes can mislead you.

Quick Tips for Better Brightness

Brighten in small steps. A change of 5 percent at a time gives you control, while a large jump can overshoot the right value before you notice.

Check the highlights, not just the shadows. A photo is over-brightened when the bright areas lose detail, even if the dark areas look good.

Use the compare view every time. A quick look at the original next to the edit catches mistakes that a small preview hides.

Save the original photo without edits. Once a brightness change is flattened into an export, it cannot be undone from that file.

Brightening a Photo for a Presentation

A photo used on a slide needs to be bright enough to see from the back of the room. A dark image on a large screen looks muddy, while a well-lit one reads clearly.

Brighten slide photos a little more than you would for a feed. Projectors wash out colours and reduce brightness, so a photo that looks fine on a laptop may look dark when projected.

Check the photo on the actual projector if you can. A test slide shows whether the brightness holds up in the room, where the light is very different from your desk.

Keep the brightening even across the slide. A photo that is bright in one corner and dark in another distracts the audience from the words on top of it.

Brightening Old Scanned Photos

Old scanned photos are often dark and faded. Age dulls the print, the scanner misreads the tones, and the result is a grey, murky image that needs a lift.

Increase the brightness in small steps and watch the faces. The goal is a photo that looks like the original print did when it was new, not a washed-out version of it.

Old scans often need contrast as well as brightness. The faded print has little tonal range, so a brightness lift plus a small contrast boost restores the depth.

Work from the best scan you have. A high-resolution scan brightens far better than a low-resolution one, because there is more detail for the edit to reveal.

Frequently asked questions

How do I brighten a photo on my phone?

Open the Image Brightness Editor in your phone browser, upload the photo from your gallery, and drag the slider to the right with your finger. Use a quick preset for an exact value, check the compare view, and download the result.

What brightness value should I use for a dark indoor photo?

Start at plus 15 percent, which is the tool preview value, and increase in steps of 5 percent until the subject is clearly visible. Most indoor photos need 15 to 30 percent. Stop before the bright areas, such as windows or lamps, turn white.

Can I darken a photo that is too bright?

Yes. Drag the slider to the left into negative values, such as minus 15 or minus 30 percent. Darkening deepens the shadows and restores richness to an overexposed photo. Check the shadows so they do not turn into solid black patches.

Why does my brightened photo show grainy noise?

Brightening reveals noise that was hidden in the shadows, and JPEG compression makes it worse. Reduce the slider and accept a slightly darker photo, or start from a better original taken in good light. A gentle lift shows far less noise than a strong one.

Should I adjust brightness before or after contrast?

Brightness first, then contrast. Brightening lifts the overall level, and contrast restores the depth that brightening can wash out. Doing them in this order makes each adjustment easier to judge in the compare view.

Can I undo a brightness change after downloading?

Not from the downloaded file, since the change is flattened into the export. Keep the original photo so you can try a different brightness level. In the tool, use the reset option to return to zero before you download.
