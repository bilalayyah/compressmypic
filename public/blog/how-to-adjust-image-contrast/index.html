How to Adjust Image Contrast

Adjust image contrast in minutes: upload, drag the slider, try a preset, check highlights and shadows, and download a punchy, well-balanced photo.

Adjusting contrast takes only a few minutes. You upload the photo, drag a slider, check the highlights and shadows, and download the punchier version.

This how-to walks through the whole process step by step. It covers the slider, the quick presets, the compare modes, and the export settings that keep the result looking natural.

By the end you will be able to fix a flat landscape, soften a harsh portrait, or sharpen a dull screenshot on any device. Keep the original photo until you have checked the result.

Before You Start: Diagnose the Photo

Look at the photo before you open the tool. Is it flat and grey, harsh with deep shadows, or correctly spread? The answer tells you which direction to move the slider.

A flat photo needs a positive boost of 10 to 20 percent. A harsh photo needs a negative move of 10 to 20 percent to soften the extremes.

Check whether the photo is also too dark or too light. A dark, flat photo needs brightness first, because contrast alone cannot fix the level.

Open the Image Contrast Editor in your browser. Everything runs locally, so your photo is never uploaded to a server.

Adjust Contrast in Five Steps

The process is the same for a photo, a screenshot, or a scanned document. Upload, adjust, compare, export, and verify the download.

Take your time with the slider. Small moves make a big difference, and the preview shows you exactly when the photo looks right.

  1. Upload your image by dragging it into the dropzone, choosing it from your device, or pasting a screenshot with Ctrl+V.
  2. Drag the Contrast Level slider to the right to increase contrast or to the left to decrease it, watching the live preview.
  3. Try a quick preset for a one-click test, then fine-tune with the slider if the result is close but not quite right.
  4. Use the compare modes to check the highlights and shadows against the original before downloading.
  5. Choose the export format and quality, then click Download to save the adjusted image.

Open the downloaded file once before you use it. Confirm the photo looks punchy without losing detail in the bright or dark areas.

Using the Contrast Slider

The slider runs from negative to positive values, with zero meaning no change. Moving it right increases contrast, and moving it left decreases it.

Drag slowly and watch the preview. The live update shows exactly how the image responds, so you can stop at the point where the photo looks right.

Small moves are better than large ones. A change of 10 to 20 percent is often enough, and larger moves quickly clip the highlights and crush the shadows.

The tool previews your photo at plus 20 percent right after upload. That starting point gives you an immediate sense of what a moderate contrast boost looks like.

Using the Quick Presets

The tool includes quick presets that apply common contrast values in one click. They are a fast way to test different levels without dragging the slider.

Click a preset and look at the preview. If the result is close but not quite right, fine-tune with the slider from that point.

Use presets to compare options quickly. A preset at plus 10 percent next to one at plus 30 percent shows you how much contrast the photo can take.

For a batch of similar photos, the same preset keeps the results consistent. A set of hazy landscapes all boosted by the same amount looks like a series.

Using the Compare Modes

Compare modes show you the original and the edited version together. The After view shows the contrast-boosted image, and the Before view shows the original.

Split Compare is the most useful for contrast work. It shows both versions side by side, so you can see exactly which shadows deepened and which highlights brightened.

Fullscreen Compare gives you the largest view. It is the best way to judge a contrast edit, because the full-size image shows clipping that a small preview hides.

Check the compare view before every download. A quick look catches clipped highlights and crushed shadows that a small preview might hide.

Boosting a Flat Photo

Upload the flat photo and drag the slider to plus 15 percent. Check the preview: the shadows should deepen, the highlights should brighten, and the image should feel solid.

If the photo is still flat, increase the slider in small steps of 5 percent. Each step adds a little more punch, and the preview shows when to stop.

Watch the highlights as you go. Clouds, white shirts, and bright skin will clip first, turning into featureless white patches with no detail.

If the photo looks harsh after boosting, reduce the slider by 5 percent. A small reduction often restores the detail without making the photo flat again.

Softening a Harsh Photo

Drag the slider to the left for a photo taken in harsh midday sun. Negative values like minus 10 or minus 20 percent soften the strong shadows on a face.

Check the face as you soften. The shadows should fade into a gentler look, while the overall shape of the face stays clear and defined.

Negative contrast also suits dreamy, soft landscapes. A misty morning scene with low contrast feels calm and quiet, which matches the mood of the photo.

Do not overdo it. A photo with too little contrast looks grey and lifeless, as though it were taken through fog, even on a clear day.

Fixing a Dull Screenshot

Paste a screenshot directly with Ctrl+V to skip the save step. The image appears in the editor immediately, ready to adjust.

Increase the contrast until the text is dark and the background is white. A document with strong black text on a clean white background is easy to read and to print.

Watch for noise in the background. A heavily contrasted scan can show speckles and artefacts, especially in a JPEG that was already compressed.

Export screenshots as PNG. The lossless format keeps the small text sharp, which matters when the screenshot shows a setting or an error message.

Checking Shadows and Highlights

After every contrast change, check the darkest and brightest parts of the image separately. The shadows crush first under a boost, and the highlights clip first under the same move.

Zoom into a shadow area and confirm it still shows detail. A coat, a tree, or a dark corner should have visible texture, not a solid black patch.

Zoom into a highlight area and confirm it still shows detail. A cloud, a white shirt, or a bright forehead should have visible shape, not a white blank.

If either extreme lost detail, reduce the slider by 5 percent and check again. Small reductions usually restore the detail without losing the punch.

Previewing Before You Download

Always preview before downloading. Check that the photo looks punchy, that no bright areas turned white, and that no dark areas turned black.

Use the split compare view to check the change. It shows the original and the edited version side by side, so you can see exactly what the contrast did.

Zoom into the bright and dark areas at full size. A sky or a shadow that looks fine in a small preview may be clipped or crushed when viewed large.

If the result looks harsh, reduce the slider by 5 percent and check again. Small reductions often restore the detail without making the photo flat again.

Choosing the Export Format

Choose JPG for photos you plan to share. It keeps file sizes small and works everywhere, and a high quality setting keeps the contrast-edited photo looking clean.

Choose PNG for screenshots and graphics with text. PNG is lossless, so the edges of letters stay sharp, and it preserves transparency when you need it.

Choose WebP when you want small files with good quality. It works well for web images, though a few older systems still reject the format.

Remember that the quality slider hides for PNG, because PNG is lossless and has no quality setting to adjust. That is normal behaviour, not a problem with the tool.

Adjusting Contrast on a Phone or Tablet

The tool works on touchscreens, so you can fix a flat photo on the phone that took it. Upload from your gallery, drag the slider with your finger, and download the result.

Dragging a slider with a finger is less precise than with a mouse. Use the quick presets for exact values, then fine-tune with small drags.

Check the preview at full size before downloading. A contrast change that looks fine on a small phone screen may be too strong when viewed on a computer.

Downloaded files save to your usual downloads folder. From there you can share the edited photo directly to a chat app, an email, or a social post.

Troubleshooting Common Contrast Problems

If the photo looks harsh, the slider is too high. Reduce it in small steps until the skin, sky, and shadow areas show texture again.

If the photo is still flat after boosting, the original may be too hazy to rescue. A photo taken through thick fog cannot gain detail that was never captured.

If the highlights are clipped, reduce the slider and check the bright areas at full size. A small reduction usually restores the cloud or skin detail.

If the preview looks different from the download, check the export format and quality. A low JPG quality setting can soften the whole image, including the contrast edges.

Contrast Plus Brightness: The Common Pair

A photo that is both dark and flat needs brightness and contrast together. Brighten first to lift the overall level, then add contrast to restore depth.

Use the Image Brightness Editor before adding contrast when the image is dark. A contrast boost on a dark photo only deepens the shadows.

Do the steps in order: brightness first, then contrast. Reversing them can make the contrast adjustment harder to judge, because the image is still dark.

Check the compare view after each step. The split view shows exactly what each adjustment did, so you can stop when the photo looks right.

Saving Your Settings for Next Time

Note down the contrast value that worked for a set of similar photos. A short note, such as hazy landscapes plus 20 percent, saves you from guessing next time.

For a batch of photos taken in the same light, use the same value for all of them. Consistent contrast across a set looks professional, while mixed values look random.

Keep the original files. A contrast value that works for one photo may not suit another, and the original lets you try a different level without extra damage.

Review your notes occasionally. As your camera and your lighting improve, the contrast values you need will change, and old notes can mislead you.

Quick Tips for Better Contrast

Adjust in small steps. A change of 5 percent at a time gives you control, while a large jump can overshoot the right value before you notice.

Check the extremes, not just the middle. A photo is over-contrasted when the highlights clip or the shadows crush, even if the midtones look good.

Use the compare view every time. A quick look at the original next to the edit catches mistakes that a small preview hides.

Save the original photo without edits. Once a contrast change is flattened into an export, it cannot be undone from that file.

Adding Contrast to a Product Photo

Product photos need clear contrast so the item stands out from the background. A flat product shot looks dull, while a punchy one draws the eye to the item.

Use a moderate boost of 10 to 20 percent for most products. The item should look solid and defined, with clear edges against the background.

Watch the product colours as you increase contrast. A boost that is too strong can make the colours look harsh, so check the item itself, not just the background.

Keep the contrast consistent across the whole catalogue. A shop with matching contrast levels looks tidy and trustworthy, while mixed levels look careless.

Frequently asked questions

How do I add contrast to a photo on my phone?

Open the Image Contrast Editor in your phone browser, upload the photo from your gallery, and drag the slider to the right with your finger. Use a quick preset for an exact value, check the compare view, and download the result.

What contrast value should I use for a hazy landscape?

Start at plus 15 to 20 percent and check the sky. Haze flattens the tonal range, so a moderate boost restores depth. Stop before the clouds turn into white blanks, and reduce the slider if the highlights clip.

Can I reduce contrast to soften a harsh portrait?

Yes. Drag the slider to the left into negative values, such as minus 10 or minus 20 percent. Negative contrast softens strong shadows on the face without losing its shape. Check the skin so it stays defined and does not turn grey.

Why do my photo highlights turn white after adding contrast?

The contrast was pushed too far, which clips the brightest areas into pure white. Reduce the slider in small steps until the clouds, skin, and white surfaces show detail again. A 10 to 20 percent boost is usually enough.

Should I adjust contrast before or after brightness?

Brightness first, then contrast. Brightness sets the overall level, and contrast refines the spread around that level. A dark photo needs brightening before contrast, since contrast alone cannot fix the level.

Can I undo a contrast change after downloading?

Not from the downloaded file, since the change is flattened into the export. Keep the original photo so you can try a different contrast level. In the tool, use the reset option to return to zero before you download.
