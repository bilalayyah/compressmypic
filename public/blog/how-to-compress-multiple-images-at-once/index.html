How to Compress Multiple Images at Once

Step-by-step: compress dozens of images at once with presets, a progress bar, and a single ZIP download. Works for blogs, shops, and albums.

One image takes a minute to compress. Fifty images take an afternoon, unless you batch them.

Batch compression processes a whole folder at once. You choose a preset, watch the progress bar, and download everything together as a ZIP.

This guide walks through the whole workflow, from gathering files to checking the finished ZIP.

What You Need Before You Start

Gather the original images in one folder. Working from originals gives the best quality; working from compressed copies adds damage.

Decide what the images are for. Thumbnails need the Smaller File preset, while portfolio shots need High Quality.

Check how many images you have. A batch of twenty runs quickly anywhere, while a batch of two hundred may need splitting on a phone.

Make sure you have enough free space. The ZIP download needs room on your device, roughly the size of the compressed batch.

Step 1: Open the Bulk Compressor

Open the Bulk Image Compressor in your browser. No account or install is needed.

The tool runs entirely on your device. Your photos are processed locally and never uploaded to a server.

If you are on a laptop, the page works best with drag and drop. On a phone, the upload button opens your gallery.

Bookmark the page if you compress batches often. It is the fastest route to a smaller photo library.

Step 2: Add Your Images

Drag your images onto the upload area, or tap to browse and select them. You can add a whole folder at once on most browsers.

JPG, PNG, WebP, and HEIC files are accepted. Mixed batches work fine, so photos and screenshots can share a batch.

Check the queue after adding files. Each image should appear with its name and size, so a missing file is easy to spot.

Add more files later if you forgot some. The queue accepts new images even after the first batch has finished.

Step 3: Choose a Preset

Pick Balanced for most jobs. It starts at 70 percent quality and gives a good middle ground between size and looks.

Pick Smaller File for thumbnails, avatars, and images that will be shown small. The extra compression is invisible at small sizes.

Pick High Quality for portfolios, galleries, and showcase images. The files stay larger, but the detail is worth it.

The preset sets the starting point. Each image is still optimised individually, so a simple portrait and a detailed landscape get different treatment.

Step 4: Run the Batch

Start the batch and watch the progress bar. It shows which files are done and how much total size has been saved so far.

Processing happens one image at a time. That keeps the browser responsive instead of freezing while it works.

Do not close the tab while the batch runs. The work happens in your browser, so closing the page stops the compression.

Large batches take a few minutes. Use the time to prepare the destination folder or write the blog post the images belong to.

Step 5: Preview Individual Results

When the batch finishes, preview the results before downloading. Click any image to see it at full size.

Check the hardest images first. A detailed photo or a screenshot with small text will show problems before a simple portrait does.

Compare a few results with their originals. Small differences are normal; unreadable text or blotchy skin is not.

If one image looks bad, re-compress it alone with different settings. You do not need to re-run the whole batch for a single file.

Step 6: Download the ZIP

Click Download All to get a single ZIP file with every compressed image inside. The ZIP keeps the set organised in one package.

Extract the ZIP into its own folder. That keeps the compressed set separate from the originals and easy to find later.

Check the extracted files. Open a few at full size and confirm they look right before uploading them anywhere.

Rename files clearly if the automatic names are not descriptive enough. A clear name saves confusion when the set is large.

Fixing Problems in a Batch

If one image grew larger, leave it as it is. The tool warns you when re-encoding makes a file bigger, and the original is the better file.

If an image looks blurry, the source may have been too small. Re-compress that one file from a larger original with the High Quality preset.

If colours look wrong, check for transparency. Converting a transparent PNG to JPG fills the background, usually with white.

If the batch is slow, split it into smaller groups. Phones especially benefit from batches of twenty rather than two hundred.

Workflow: A Set of Blog Thumbnails

Gather the thumbnails for a set of posts and upload them as one batch. Choose the Balanced preset for consistent results.

Preview three or four results, not just the first one. A single bad thumbnail can hide among good ones.

Download the ZIP, extract it, and upload the files to your blog. Keep the originals in case a post needs a larger image later.

Check the published posts on a phone and a laptop. The thumbnails should load fast and look sharp on both.

Workflow: A Product Catalog

Collect the product shots for a launch and upload them as one batch. Use the High Quality preset so the products look their best.

Preview the most detailed products first. Fabric texture and printed labels are the first things to soften.

Download the ZIP and upload the images to the shop. Check a few product pages to confirm the images display correctly.

Keep the originals in a shared drive. When the next season launches, the team can compress fresh from the masters.

Workflow: A Shared Photo Album

Gather the photos for the album and upload them as one batch. The Balanced preset keeps the album light without hurting the memories.

Preview a few favourites before downloading. A beloved photo deserves a careful check, even in a batch of a hundred.

Download the ZIP and share it with family or friends. A compressed album downloads quickly, even on a slow connection.

Keep the originals safe. The shared copies are disposable, and the originals are what you will want in ten years.

Selecting a Whole Folder at Once

Most desktop browsers let you select an entire folder, not just individual files. That is the fastest way to start a batch.

Look for the folder option in the file picker. It appears as a separate choice in Chrome, Edge, and Firefox on desktop.

Check the queue after selecting a folder. Subfolders may be included or ignored depending on the browser, so verify the count matches.

Remove any files that do not belong. A folder often contains a stray screenshot or an old file that should not be compressed.

On phones, folder selection is limited. Pick files in groups from the gallery instead, and keep each group to around twenty images.

Reading the Progress Bar

The progress bar shows how far the batch has gone and how much size has been saved so far. It updates as each image finishes.

A steady pace means the batch is healthy. If the bar stalls on one file, that file may be unusually large or complex.

Do not close the tab while the bar is moving. The work happens in your browser, and closing the page stops the batch.

If the browser tab goes to sleep in the background, the batch may pause. Keep the tab visible until the batch finishes.

For very large batches, the bar is your best friend. It turns a long wait into visible progress, file by file.

Understanding Per-File Results

Each finished file shows its own result: original size, compressed size, and percentage saved. The numbers vary widely across a batch.

Simple portraits often save 90 percent or more. Detailed landscapes may save only 70 percent, because they have more data worth keeping.

A file that saved almost nothing was probably already compressed. The tool did its best, but there was little left to remove.

A file that grew is a warning, not a failure. Keep the original for that file and move on with the rest of the batch.

Sort the results by savings when you can. The smallest savers are the files most likely to need individual attention.

Retrying Failed or Poor Files

Some files fail or come out poorly in a batch. That is normal, and the fix is to handle them individually.

Pull the problem file out of the set. Compress it alone with the High Quality preset and preview the result carefully.

If the format is the issue, switch it. A PNG photo re-compressed as JPG often turns from a failure into a clean result.

If the source is the issue, find a better original. A file that was already compressed cannot be rescued by any setting.

Replace the bad file in the final set. The batch stays intact, and only the problem file changes.

Uploading the Batch Results

After extraction, upload the files to their destination in groups. Uploading fifty files at once can overwhelm some platforms.

Check the first few uploads carefully. Confirm they display correctly before uploading the rest of the set.

Match filenames to their destinations. A product image uploaded to the wrong product page is an embarrassing and avoidable error.

Keep the ZIP until the uploads are confirmed. If an upload fails, the ZIP has the file ready without a re-run.

Delete the local compressed copies only after the uploads are verified. The originals stay safe regardless.

How Long Does a Batch Take?

A batch of twenty phone photos takes about a minute on a modern laptop. The same batch may take three minutes on a phone.

Larger files take longer. A batch of 10MB camera photos needs more processing time per file than a batch of 2MB phone shots.

The preset affects speed too. High Quality runs more optimisation passes than Smaller File, so it takes slightly longer.

Plan for the wait. Start the batch, then use the time to write the post, prepare the product descriptions, or organise the destination folder.

For very large batches, run them when you do not need the computer for other heavy work. Compression uses the processor, and multitasking slows it down.

Sharing the ZIP With Others

The ZIP file is the easiest way to share a compressed set. One file carries every image, ready to extract and use.

Send the ZIP by email when it is small enough. Most email systems accept attachments up to 20MB or 25MB.

For larger ZIPs, use a file-sharing link. Upload the ZIP to a drive and share the link instead of the file itself.

Tell the recipient what is inside. A short note about the preset and the purpose prevents confusion on their end.

Keep a copy of the ZIP yourself. If the recipient loses it, you can resend without re-running the batch.

Cleaning Up After a Batch

After a batch is uploaded and verified, clean up the working files. A tidy workspace prevents mix-ups in the next batch.

Delete the extracted compressed copies once they are safely uploaded. The ZIP remains as a backup of the set.

Keep the originals forever. They live in their own folder, separate from every compressed copy.

Archive the ZIP with the project it belongs to. A ZIP stored with the blog post or product launch is findable later.

A clean workspace is a fast workspace. Five minutes of cleanup now saves an hour of searching later.

A Final Check Before You Upload

Open three random images from the finished batch at full size. If they look clean, the batch is good; if one looks soft, check the rest before uploading.

Confirm the ZIP extracted correctly and the file count matches the original batch. A missing file now is easier to fix than a missing image on a live page.

Frequently asked questions

How do I compress a whole folder of images at once?

Open the bulk compressor and drag the folder onto the upload area, or select all the files at once. Choose a preset, run the batch, and download the results as a single ZIP. Each image is compressed individually for the best quality.

Which preset should I use for blog thumbnails?

Balanced works well for most thumbnails, starting at 70 percent quality. Use Smaller File if the thumbnails are shown very small, and High Quality if they are large feature images. Preview a few results before downloading the whole batch.

Can I compress images on my phone in bulk?

Yes, the tool works in a mobile browser. Keep batches smaller on a phone, around twenty images at a time, since phones have less memory. Download the ZIP to your Files app and extract it there.

What happens if my phone runs out of memory during a batch?

The batch may slow down or stop. Split the remaining images into a smaller group and run them separately. Desktop browsers handle larger batches more comfortably, so use a laptop for very big jobs.

Do I need to resize images before bulk compression?

No, the bulk compressor keeps the original pixel dimensions and only reduces file size. If you also need smaller dimensions, resize the images first with the image resizer, then compress the resized files in a batch.

How do I know the batch actually saved space?

The batch summary reports the total size before and after compression, plus the percentage saved. A typical photo batch drops by 80 to 90 percent. You can also compare the ZIP size with the original folder size.
