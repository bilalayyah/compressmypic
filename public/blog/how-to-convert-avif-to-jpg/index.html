How to Convert AVIF to JPG

Convert AVIF images to JPG in a few steps. Upload, set quality, handle transparency, and download — one file or a whole batch.

An AVIF image downloaded from a modern website often refuses to open in the app you need. Converting it to JPG takes less than a minute, and the result works everywhere.

The only tricky part is transparency, and the only requirement is a modern browser that can decode AVIF in the first place.

This guide walks through each step, so your first conversion comes out clean.

Before You Start: Check Your Browser

You need a modern browser that decodes AVIF natively. Recent versions of Chrome, Edge, Firefox, and Safari all do, so update yours if it is old.

Find the AVIF files on your device. They usually come from downloads, where a website served AVIF instead of JPG without telling you.

Check whether the images have transparency. Photos usually do not, while logos and stickers might, and transparent areas need a matte colour.

Files up to 25MB each are accepted. That covers high-resolution downloads and camera captures without any preparation.

Step 1: Open the AVIF to JPG Tool

Open the AVIF to JPG tool in your browser. The page works on Windows, Mac, Linux, ChromeOS, Android, and iOS.

You will see an upload area, a matte colour selector, and a quality slider. Those three controls are the whole job.

No account is needed, and nothing is uploaded. The conversion happens on your device, which matters for personal images and downloaded files.

If you are on a phone, the page works in your mobile browser. Tap the upload area to pick AVIF files from your downloads or gallery.

Step 2: Upload Your AVIF Files

Drag your AVIF files onto the upload area, or click it to open the file picker. You can select many files at once for a batch.

Each file appears as a card with a thumbnail, its dimensions, and its size. A quick scan confirms the right files loaded and nothing extra slipped in.

If a file fails to load, your browser may not decode AVIF. Update the browser to the latest version and try again.

You can also paste a copied image with Ctrl+V on a computer. That is handy when the AVIF is in your clipboard from another app.

Step 3: Pick the Matte Colour

Click the matte colour selector to choose the fill for transparent areas. White is the default and suits most uses.

Pick black for dark designs, or enter a hex code to match a specific website background exactly. A matched background makes the logo look like it belongs there.

If your AVIF has no transparency, this setting does nothing. Photos pass through unchanged, whatever colour you pick.

Check a logo card after converting to see how the matte looks. A wrong colour is obvious immediately, and you can change it and convert again.

Step 4: Set the Quality Slider

For most AVIF photos, 85 percent JPG quality is the sweet spot. The photo looks clean, the file is reasonable, and the loss is invisible.

Use 90 percent or higher for images going to print. Print reveals flaws that screens hide, and the extra size is cheap for a single important image.

For images going to a form with a size limit, start at 85 and step down gradually. Check the size after each step, and stop as soon as the file fits.

AVIF photos are often very high quality, so they tolerate conversion well. The JPG at 85 percent usually looks identical to the AVIF at normal viewing sizes.

Step 5: Convert and Preview the Result

Click the Convert button. Each file converts independently, so you can watch progress card by card.

Each card shows the new JPG size next to the old AVIF size. Expect the JPG to be larger, since AVIF is more efficient than JPG.

Click Compare on a card to inspect the result side by side. Drag the split handle across the image and check faces, text, and colours.

If the result looks soft, raise the quality by five points and convert again. The original AVIF stays in the queue, so you never re-upload.

Step 6: Download the JPG Files

Click Download on a single card to save one JPG. The filename keeps your original name with a .jpg extension.

For a batch, click Download All to get a single ZIP with every converted JPG inside. That is far quicker than downloading them one by one.

Check the downloaded files once before you use them. Open a JPG in your photo viewer, confirm it looks right, and check that it opens in the app that needs it.

Keep the original AVIF files as well. They are the best-quality copies you own, and you can always convert them again if you need a different format later.

Converting Several AVIF Downloads at Once

Select many AVIF files at once in the file picker. They all load into the queue, and one click converts them all to JPG.

Use the same matte colour and quality for the whole batch. Consistency matters when the images belong to the same project or document.

Watch for error badges as the batch runs. A file that your browser cannot decode shows a warning, and updating the browser usually fixes it.

Download the batch as a ZIP and unzip it into a fresh folder. Mixing converted JPGs with the original AVIF files in one folder causes confusion later.

Fixing Common Problems

If the conversion fails, update your browser. AVIF decoding needs a recent version, and an old browser cannot read the files at all.

If the JPG looks dull, the HDR or wide-gamut colours shifted during conversion. A small saturation adjustment in any photo app restores the punch.

If a transparent AVIF gets a black box, the matte colour was black or unset. Change it to white or your page colour and convert again.

If the first result looks wrong, tweak the settings and try again. The original stays in the queue, so a second attempt costs only seconds.

After Converting: What to Do Next

If the JPGs are for a form, check the size limit before uploading. A converted JPG at 85 percent quality may exceed a 2MB cap, and you may need to compress it.

Use the 100KB compressor or the target file size compressor when a portal demands an exact size. Convert first, then compress, in that order.

If the JPGs are for a website, consider converting them to WebP instead. The JPG to WebP converter takes your new JPGs and shrinks them for the web.

If the JPGs are for printing, keep them at 90 percent or higher. Print reveals compression flaws, and a high-quality JPG is the safest choice.

Tips for Phones and Tablets

On a phone, tap the upload area and pick AVIF files from your downloads or gallery. The conversion runs on the phone, and the JPGs download to your device.

Keep phone batches small, since large AVIF files take time to decode on a mobile processor. Ten files at a time is comfortable.

Downloads land in your phone's Downloads folder. On iPhone, check the Files app; on Android, check your file manager.

For a large batch of downloads, use a desktop. The faster processor and bigger screen make many files far easier to manage.

Converting AVIF for a Form

Upload forms list JPG and PNG as accepted formats, and an AVIF file bounces straight back. Convert to JPG before uploading, and check the form rules first.

Convert the image to JPG at 85 percent quality. The photo looks clean, the file is a reasonable size, and the form accepts it without any trouble.

Check the file size limit after converting. A form that caps uploads at 2MB may reject a 4MB JPG, and you may need to compress it with the 100KB or 200KB tool.

Check the dimensions as well as the format. A form that demands a specific pixel size will reject an image that is the right format but the wrong shape.

Upload the converted image early, before the deadline. A last-minute conversion leaves no time to fix a problem, while an early upload leaves room to try again.

Converting AVIF for an Email

Email clients display JPG reliably, while AVIF support is inconsistent. An AVIF attachment may show as a broken image, which looks unprofessional in a business email.

Convert the AVIF to JPG before attaching it. The JPG displays correctly in every email client, on every device, and the message arrives intact.

Keep the quality at 85 percent for email images. The photo looks clean, the file is small enough to send, and the recipient sees the same image you do.

For several images, convert the batch and attach the JPGs together. A set of AVIF files converted to JPG is far smaller and far more compatible than the originals.

Check the total size before sending. A quick glance at the attachment sizes prevents the frustrating bounce message that arrives after you thought the email was sent.

Converting AVIF for a Presentation

Presentation slides need images that open on the presentation computer, and AVIF may fail on an older machine. Convert to JPG first, and the slides work everywhere.

Convert the AVIF images to JPG at 85 to 90 percent quality before adding them to the deck. The JPGs embed cleanly in every PowerPoint version.

For a deck with many images, convert the whole set at once. A batch conversion takes minutes, and the JPGs are ready to drop into the slides.

Check the total deck size after converting. A presentation over 50MB may struggle to email or upload, so compress the images if the file is too large.

Test the deck on the presentation computer before the talk. A slide that looks fine on a laptop may show a broken image on an older machine, and a quick test catches it.

Converting AVIF for a Client

Clients often receive AVIF files from a website and cannot open them. Converting the files to JPG gives them images that work in every app they own.

Ask the client what they need the images for before converting. A client who needs them for a website wants WebP, while a client who needs them for a document wants JPG.

Deliver both formats when in doubt. The JPG files work everywhere, while the AVIF originals stay small for the web, and the client can choose per use.

Name the converted files clearly, with the purpose and the date. A file called hero-photo.jpg tells the client what it is, while a file called download.avif tells them nothing.

Include a short note with the delivery explaining the formats. A one-line note prevents the client from asking why there are two copies of every image.

Converting AVIF on an Old Computer

An old computer may not decode AVIF at all, since the format needs a recent browser. Update the browser first, and the conversion should work.

If the browser cannot be updated, transfer the AVIF files to a newer device and convert them there. A phone or a newer laptop handles the decoding without trouble.

For a single image, an online converter on a newer device is the fastest path. Convert it, download the JPG, and transfer the JPG back to the old computer.

Keep the converted JPGs on the old computer after transferring them. The JPGs open in every app on the old machine, while the AVIF files never will.

Consider updating the old computer's software if it is used often. A modern browser solves the AVIF problem and many others, and the update is usually free.

Converting AVIF for a Portfolio

A portfolio must look perfect, and the conversion settings should reflect that. Convert AVIF images to JPG at 90 percent quality, so the photos stay sharp even when a client zooms in.

Choose the strongest images first, then convert them in order of importance. The hero image deserves the most care, since it sets the impression for the whole portfolio.

Check each image after converting, not just a sample. A portfolio has few images, and each one matters, so a quick review of all of them is worth the time.

Update the portfolio site with the JPG files, and test it on desktop and phone. A broken image on a portfolio is a lost client, so verify every image loads.

Keep the AVIF originals in the portfolio folder. If a client asks for a WebP or AVIF version, it is ready without a fresh download.

Frequently asked questions

How do I convert several AVIF downloads in one go?

Select many AVIF files at once in the file picker, or drag them onto the upload area. One click converts them all with the same quality and matte settings, and you download the whole batch as a single ZIP. Each file converts independently, so one bad file does not stop the rest.

What should I do if my browser cannot decode an AVIF file?

Update your browser to the latest version, since AVIF support arrived in recent releases of Chrome, Edge, Firefox, and Safari. If the file still fails, confirm it is a real AVIF by opening it in a different browser. A corrupted download needs a fresh copy from the source website.

Which quality percentage suits AVIF photos best?

85 percent is the best default for most AVIF photos, balancing size and quality well. Use 90 percent or higher for images going to print, and step down gradually only when a file size limit forces it. AVIF photos are high quality, so they tolerate conversion well.

Can I check the converted JPG before downloading?

Yes. Click Compare on any card to see the AVIF and the JPG side by side. Drag the split handle across the image and check faces, text, and colours. If the first result looks wrong, tweak the quality or matte colour and convert again from the original, which stays in the queue.

Does AVIF to JPG work on mobile browsers?

Yes. Open the tool in your phone browser, pick AVIF files from your downloads or gallery, and download the JPGs. The conversion runs on the phone, and the files download to your device. Keep phone batches small, since large AVIF files take time to decode on mobile.

When should I convert AVIF to JPG instead of AVIF to PNG?

Choose JPG when the destination demands it or when compatibility matters most, since JPG opens everywhere and the files are smaller. Choose PNG when you need to keep transparency or plan to edit the image many times, since PNG is lossless and preserves the alpha channel.
