How to Convert an Image Format

Convert any image to JPG, PNG or WebP in six quick steps. Upload, pick a format, set quality, preview and download — all in your browser.

Converting an image format sounds technical, but the whole job takes less than a minute. You upload a file, pick the format you need, and download the result.

No software to install, no account to create, and no waiting for an upload to a distant server. Everything happens in your browser, on your own device.

This guide walks through each step in order, with the settings that matter and the mistakes to avoid. By the end, you will convert any image with confidence.

Before You Start: What You Need

You need the image file and a modern browser. Chrome, Edge, Firefox, and Safari all work, on desktop and on mobile. There is nothing to download first.

Find the best original you have. A conversion cannot add detail, so start from the highest-quality copy. If you have both a JPG and a PNG of the same picture, use the PNG.

Know which format you need before you begin. A form that asks for JPG will reject a PNG, and a website that wants WebP will not take a BMP. Check the requirement first to avoid converting twice.

Files up to 20MB are accepted here. That covers phone photos, camera shots, and large screenshots. If your file is bigger, shrink it first with the compressor.

Step 1: Upload Your Image

Open the Image Converter tool in your browser. You will see a large upload area in the middle of the page.

Drag your image file onto that area, or click it to open your file picker. You can also paste a screenshot straight from your clipboard with Ctrl+V, which is handy for quick conversions.

The file appears in the queue with its name, dimensions, and size. A thumbnail shows you exactly which image you loaded, so a wrong file is easy to spot.

You can add more files at this point if you want to convert several at once. Each file converts independently, and you download them together as a ZIP later.

Step 2: Pick the Output Format

Look for the Output Format selector and choose JPG, PNG, or WebP. This is the single most important choice in the whole process, so take a second to think.

Choose JPG for photographs, especially when the file goes to a form, an email, or an older program. JPG works everywhere and keeps photos small.

Choose PNG when the image has transparency, sharp text, or flat colours. Logos, icons, and screenshots with readable text all belong in PNG.

Choose WebP when the image is for a website. It is smaller than JPG at the same quality and supports transparency, which makes it the best all-rounder for the web.

Step 3: Set the Quality Slider

If you picked JPG or WebP, a quality slider appears. It runs from 10 percent to 100 percent, and it controls how much detail the encoder keeps.

For most photos, 80 to 85 percent is the sweet spot. The file shrinks a lot, yet the image still looks clean at normal viewing sizes.

Push the slider to 90 percent or higher for images you plan to print or edit further. Print shows flaws that screens hide, and editing a low-quality file only makes things worse.

If you picked PNG, the slider disappears. PNG is lossless, so there is no quality level to choose, and the file stores every pixel exactly as it was.

Step 4: Handle Transparency (JPG Only)

This step matters only when your source image has transparent areas and your target is JPG. Since JPG cannot store transparency, those areas need a fill colour.

Click the Transparency Fill Colour swatch to pick a colour. White is the default and suits most documents, web pages, and forms.

Pick black if the image will sit on a dark background, or enter a hex code to match a specific website colour exactly. A matched background makes the logo look like it belongs there.

If your target is PNG or WebP, skip this step entirely. Both formats keep transparency, so the see-through areas stay see-through without any fill.

Step 5: Convert and Preview the Result

Click the Convert button and wait a second or two. The tool decodes your image, redraws it on a canvas, and encodes it in the new format.

Each converted card shows the new file size next to the old one. That comparison tells you immediately whether the conversion saved space.

Click Compare on a card to open the side-by-side viewer. Drag the split handle across the image and look closely at faces, text, and edges.

If the result looks soft or the colours look wrong, change the quality or the matte colour and convert again. The original stays in the queue, so you never re-upload.

Step 6: Download Your Converted Image

Click the Download button on a single card to save one file. The filename keeps your original name with the new extension, so it is easy to find.

If you converted several files, click Download All to get a ZIP archive with everything inside. The ZIP is named converted_images_compressmypic.zip.

Check the downloaded file once before you use it. Open it in your photo viewer, confirm it looks right, and check that the format is the one you wanted.

Keep the original file as well. A converted JPG can never become a perfect PNG again, so the original is your safety net if you need a different format later.

Converting Images on Your Phone

The converter works the same way on a phone browser. Open the page, tap the upload area, and pick a photo from your gallery.

iPhone photos are the most common phone conversion. HEIC files from the camera roll convert to JPG in seconds, and the result opens in any app.

Touch controls work for every setting. The quality slider, the format picker, and the fill colour swatch are all tappable, and the Compare viewer works with a finger drag.

Downloads land in your phone's Downloads folder. On iPhone, check the Files app; on Android, check the Downloads folder in your file manager.

Converting Many Files at Once

Drop a whole batch into the queue instead of converting one file at a time. The tool accepts mixed formats, so JPGs, PNGs, and HEICs can sit in the same batch.

Every file converts to the same output format you selected. If you need different formats for different files, run two batches instead of one.

Watch for error badges while the batch runs. A single corrupted file shows a warning without stopping the rest, so the good files still finish.

Use Download All to grab the whole batch as one ZIP. That is far quicker than tapping Download on twenty separate cards.

Fixing Common Problems

If a file fails to convert, check its format first. A file named .jpg that is actually a WebP will confuse the decoder, so confirm the real format before retrying.

If the output looks too big, lower the quality slider by ten points and convert again. A drop from 90 to 80 percent often halves the file size with little visible change.

If a transparent logo gets a black box in the JPG, you forgot the matte fill. Set the fill colour to white or your page colour and convert again.

If an animated GIF comes out as a still image, that is expected. The converter draws only the first frame, so use the GIF Compressor when the motion matters.

Checking the Result Before You Send It

Open the converted file in the app that will use it. A JPG for a form should open in your photo viewer; a WebP for a website should open in your browser.

Check the file size against any limit. A form that caps uploads at 2MB will reject a 3MB JPG, even if the format is right.

Zoom into the image at full size once. Small text, thin lines, and faces are the first places where low quality shows, and a quick zoom catches problems early.

Confirm the dimensions did not change. Conversion keeps width and height, but it is worth a glance, especially for images with strict size rules.

When to Use a Dedicated Converter Instead

The general converter is perfect for everyday jobs, but dedicated tools do specific pairs better. The HEIC to JPG converter is tuned for iPhone photos, with a 90 percent default quality that suits them.

For logos, the SVG to PNG converter offers scale choices up to 8000 pixels, which the general converter does not. Scale matters when a small icon must become a large print file.

For whole folders, the Bulk Image Converter adds filename suffixes and a batch summary, which help when you convert hundreds of files at once.

Use the general converter when you have a mixed batch and a simple need. Switch to a dedicated tool when the job has special requirements.

Choosing Between Single and Batch Conversion

A single conversion suits a one-off job, such as a photo for a form or a logo for a document. The general converter handles it in seconds, and you download the result immediately.

A batch conversion suits a folder of images that all need the same format. The bulk converter applies one setting to every file, which is far faster than converting them one by one.

Use the single converter when the files need different settings. A photo that wants 85 percent quality and a logo that wants PNG cannot share one batch setting, so convert them separately.

Use the batch converter when the files share a destination. A website image library, a product catalogue, or a vacation album all want one format and one quality level, which is exactly what batch conversion does.

When in doubt, start with a single file. Convert one image, check the result, and if the settings work, apply them to the whole batch with confidence.

Converting Images from Email Attachments

Images often arrive as email attachments in the wrong format. A client sends a HEIC photo, a colleague sends a BMP screenshot, and you need a JPG before you can use either.

Save the attachment to your device first, then drop it into the converter. Converting straight from the email app rarely works, since the file must exist on your device.

Check the attachment size before converting. Email services often compress images automatically, so the attachment may already be lower quality than the original photo.

After converting, reply with the JPG attached rather than forwarding the original. The recipient can open a JPG anywhere, while the original format may fail on their device too.

Keep the original attachment in your email as a backup. If the conversion goes wrong, you can download it again and try different settings without asking the sender to resend it.

Converting Images for Social Media

Social media platforms accept JPG and PNG uploads, and they convert everything to their own formats anyway. A high-quality JPG is the simplest input for most platforms.

Check the platform's size and dimension rules before converting. Instagram, Facebook, and X each have preferred sizes, and a conversion cannot fix a wrongly sized image.

For a profile picture, convert to JPG at 90 percent quality. The small size keeps the face sharp, and the file uploads quickly even on a slow connection.

For a post with text, such as an announcement graphic, use PNG or WebP instead. The text stays sharper in those formats, while JPG smears small letters.

Download the converted file and upload it from your device rather than sharing a link. A direct upload preserves the quality you chose, while a shared link may trigger another round of compression.

What to Do When the Result Looks Wrong

If the converted image looks washed out, the colour profile may have shifted. Compare it against the original and check whether the difference matters for the purpose, since most shifts are subtle.

If the image looks blurry, the quality setting was too low or the source was already soft. Raise the quality to 90 percent and convert again from the original file.

If the colours look wrong, the source may use an unusual colour space such as CMYK. Convert an RGB export of the image instead, since browsers and screens expect RGB.

If the file is the wrong format, check the output selector before converting. It is easy to leave the format on PNG when you meant JPG, and the fix is a single click.

If nothing helps, try the dedicated converter for that format pair. The HEIC to JPG tool and the SVG to PNG tool handle their specific pairs with tuned defaults that the general converter does not offer.

That is the whole process: upload, choose, convert, download. Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to convert an image format?

Usually one or two seconds per file on a modern device, since the conversion runs locally in your browser. Very large photos or HEIC files may take a little longer because the decoder has more data to read. Batch conversions process each file in turn, so twenty files take roughly twenty times as long.

Can I convert an image format on my phone without an app?

Yes. Open the converter in your phone browser, tap the upload area, and choose a photo from your gallery. All settings work with touch, and the converted file downloads to your phone. No app install or account is needed.

What format should I convert my photo to for a job application?

JPG is the safest choice for job portals and application forms, since every system accepts it. Keep the quality at 85 percent or higher so your photo looks professional. Check the portal rules first, since some also demand a maximum file size or exact dimensions.

Can I convert a screenshot to a smaller format?

Yes. Screenshots saved as PNG are often several megabytes, and converting them to JPG at 80 percent quality usually cuts the size dramatically. If the screenshot has text you need to keep sharp, use WebP instead, which preserves edges better at small sizes.

Why is my converted WebP file bigger than the original JPG?

This happens with simple or already-compressed JPGs, where WebP has little left to save. It also happens when the quality slider is set very high. Try lowering the quality to 80 percent, or keep the JPG if the WebP is not actually smaller.

Can I convert the same image to two formats without re-uploading?

Yes. After the first conversion, change the output format and convert again. The original file stays in the queue, so you can produce a JPG and a WebP from one upload. Just remember to download each result before switching formats.
