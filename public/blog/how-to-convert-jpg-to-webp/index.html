How to Convert JPG to WebP for Faster Pages

Convert JPG photos to WebP in a few steps. Upload, set quality, preview the savings, and download — one photo or a whole folder.

Converting a JPG to WebP is one of the easiest speed wins for a website. The photo looks the same, the file is a third smaller, and the page loads faster.

The process takes seconds per photo, and you can convert a whole folder at once. Everything runs in your browser, with nothing to install.

This guide walks through each step, with the settings that matter for a clean result.

Before You Start: What You Need

You need the JPG files and a modern browser. Chrome, Edge, Firefox, and Safari all work, on desktop and on mobile.

Find the best JPG originals you have. A conversion cannot add detail, so start from the highest-quality copies, not from already-compressed thumbnails.

Know where the WebP files are going. A website wants 80 percent quality, while a photo you plan to zoom into wants 85 percent.

Files up to 25MB each are accepted. That covers high-resolution camera photos and large screenshots without any preparation.

Step 1: Open the JPG to WebP Tool

Open the JPG to WebP tool in your browser. The page works on Windows, Mac, Linux, ChromeOS, Android, and iOS.

You will see an upload area, a quality slider, and a convert button. Those three controls are the whole job.

No account is needed, and nothing is uploaded. The conversion happens on your device, which matters for client photos and personal images.

If you are on a phone, the page works in your mobile browser. Tap the upload area to pick photos from your gallery.

Step 2: Upload Your JPG Files

Drag your JPG files onto the upload area, or click it to open the file picker. Both .jpg and .jpeg extensions are accepted.

You can upload many files at once for a batch. Each appears as a card with its name, dimensions, and size, so you can confirm the right files loaded.

Check the dimensions on each card. The WebP will keep the exact pixel width and height of the JPG, so a wrong file is easy to spot here.

You can also paste a copied image with Ctrl+V on a computer. That is handy when the JPG is in your clipboard from another app.

Step 3: Set the Quality Slider

The quality slider controls how much detail the WebP encoder keeps. For most website photos, 80 percent is the right setting.

Use 75 percent for large hero images where loading speed matters most. The slight softening is invisible at full-screen sizes, and the file shrinks further.

Use 85 percent for product photos that visitors zoom into. The extra quality protects the fine detail that buyers inspect closely.

Do not go below 70 percent for photos people look at. The softness becomes visible, and the extra savings are small compared to the damage.

Step 4: Convert the Files

Click the Convert button. The tool decodes each JPG, redraws it on a canvas, and encodes it as WebP.

Conversion is fast, usually a second or two per photo. Larger files take a little longer, since there are more pixels to encode.

Each card updates with the new WebP size next to the old JPG size. A good conversion shows a 25 to 35 percent saving on most photos.

If a file fails, read the error message. A corrupted JPG or a misnamed file is the usual cause, and the rest of the batch keeps converting.

Step 5: Preview and Check the Savings

Click Compare on a card to see the JPG and the WebP side by side. Drag the split handle across the image to inspect faces, text, and fine detail.

Check the file sizes as well. If the WebP is larger than the JPG, the original was already heavily compressed, and you should keep the JPG for that file.

Look at the colours too. Most photos keep their colours accurately, though very saturated images may shift slightly during conversion.

If the result looks soft, raise the quality by five points and convert again. The original JPG stays in the queue, so you never re-upload.

Step 6: Download the WebP Files

Click Download on a single card to save one WebP. The filename keeps your original name with a .webp extension.

For a batch, click Download All to get a single ZIP with every converted WebP inside. That is far quicker than downloading them one by one.

Check the downloaded files once before you use them. Open a WebP in your browser and confirm it displays correctly.

Keep the original JPGs as well. If a destination rejects WebP, you can upload the JPGs instead, without converting again.

  2. Set the quality slider to 80 percent for websites.
  3. Convert and check the savings on each card.
  4. Preview with Compare, then download the WebP files.

Converting a Whole Folder of Blog Images

Select all the JPGs in your blog image folder at once. They load into the queue, and one click converts them all to WebP.

Use the same quality setting for the whole folder. Consistency matters when the images share a page, since mixed quality looks uneven.

Watch the batch summary after converting. It totals the savings across all files, and a good batch often shrinks by a third overall.

Upload the WebP files to your site in place of the JPGs. Update the image references, and test the page to confirm everything displays correctly.

Changing Quality After the First Conversion

You can change the quality slider after converting without re-uploading. The original JPGs stay in the queue, so a new setting is one click away.

Try 80 percent first, then 75 percent, and compare the two results. The difference is often invisible, and the smaller file is the right choice.

For a single important photo, try 85 percent and 90 percent as well. The best setting is the lowest one where you cannot see any difference.

Download each version before switching settings. The download button saves the current conversion, so grab it before you change anything.

Fixing Common Problems

If the WebP comes out larger than the JPG, the original was already heavily compressed. Keep the JPG for that file and move on.

If the image looks soft, the quality was too low. Raise the slider to 85 percent and convert again from the original JPG.

If a file fails to convert, check that it is a real JPG. A file named .jpg that is actually a PNG or WebP will confuse the decoder.

If the colours look shifted, the source may have an unusual colour profile. Compare the WebP against the JPG carefully, and keep the JPG if the shift matters.

Uploading WebP to Your Website

Upload the converted WebP files to your media library like any other image. WordPress, Shopify, and Wix all accept WebP uploads now.

Test one image first. Upload it, view the page, and confirm it displays correctly on desktop and on your phone.

For older browsers, serve a JPG fallback alongside the WebP. Most platforms handle this automatically, or you can use the picture element in your HTML.

Measure the page speed after switching. A page with WebP images should load noticeably faster, which is the whole point of the conversion.

Converting a Blog's Image Library

A blog's image library grows over years, and converting it all at once is a project, not a chore. Plan it in batches, one month or one category at a time.

Start with the most visited posts, since they benefit most from faster loading. A popular post with WebP images serves thousands of readers better than an obscure one does.

Convert each batch at 80 percent quality, then update the image references in the posts. A broken reference is worse than a slow image, so check every post after updating it.

Keep the JPG originals in the media library as a fallback. If a reader's browser cannot display WebP, the JPG version loads instead, and nobody sees a broken image.

Track progress in a simple list. A checked-off month is done, and an unchecked one is still waiting, which prevents posts from being converted twice or not at all.

Converting Product Photos for a Shop

Product photos need care, since customers zoom into them. Convert at 80 to 85 percent quality, so the fabric texture, stitching, and small details stay visible.

Sort the products by category before converting. Shoes, shirts, and accessories may need different settings, and sorting first means one clean batch per group.

Check three or four products after converting, rather than all five hundred. If those look good, the rest will too, since the settings are the same across the batch.

Upload the WebP files and update the product listings. Test a few product pages on desktop and phone to confirm the images display correctly.

Keep the JPG originals as well, since some marketplaces demand JPG. A product listed on Amazon and on your own shop needs both formats ready.

Converting Photos for a Portfolio

A portfolio must look perfect, and the conversion settings should reflect that. Use 85 percent quality, so the images stay sharp even when a client zooms in closely.

Choose the strongest images first, then convert them in order of importance. The hero image deserves the most care, since it sets the impression for the whole portfolio.

Check each image after converting, not just a sample. A portfolio has few images, and each one matters, so a quick review of all of them is worth the time.

Update the portfolio site with the WebP files, and test it on desktop and phone. A broken image on a portfolio is a lost client, so verify every image loads.

Keep the JPG originals in the portfolio folder. If a client asks for a JPG version, it is ready without a fresh conversion.

Converting Images for Social Media

Social media platforms convert uploads to their own formats, so the source format matters less than the quality. A high-quality WebP input gives a better result than a low-quality JPG input.

Convert the image to WebP at 85 percent quality before uploading. The platform's compression has less work to do, and the final image looks cleaner.

Check the platform's preferred dimensions before converting. Instagram, Facebook, and X each have ideal sizes, and a conversion cannot fix a wrongly sized image.

For a graphic with text, WebP at 85 percent keeps the letters sharper than JPG. The text stays readable after the platform's compression, which is the point of the graphic.

Download the converted file and upload it from your device rather than sharing a link. A direct upload preserves the quality you chose, while a shared link may trigger another round of compression.

Updating Old JPG References

After converting, the website still references the old JPG files. Updating those references is the step people forget, and a forgotten reference means the WebP files sit unused.

Search the site for image references to the converted files. A simple search for .jpg in the content reveals every place that needs updating.

Update the references to point at the WebP files, keeping the JPGs as a fallback. The picture element serves WebP to modern browsers and JPG to the rest.

Test the updated pages thoroughly. Click through the site, check every image, and confirm nothing is broken before moving on to the next batch.

Remove the old JPG references only after testing. A tested page with working WebP images is safe, while an untested change may hide a broken image.

Converting Images for a New Website Launch

A new website launch needs every image in the right format from day one. Converting the JPG photos to WebP before launch makes the site fast from the first visitor.

Convert the launch images in one batch at 80 percent quality. The photos look clean, the files are a third smaller, and the pages load fast on every device.

Test the launched site on desktop and phone before announcing it. A broken image on launch day is a bad first impression, while a quick test catches it.

Keep the JPG originals in the media library as a fallback. If a visitor's browser cannot display WebP, the JPG version loads instead, and nobody sees a broken image.

Document the format choice in the project notes. A one-line note prevents the next person from uploading JPGs to a site that should use WebP.

That is the whole process: upload, set quality, convert, download. Frequently asked questions

How do I convert a folder of JPG blog images to WebP?

Select all the JPGs in the folder at once in the file picker, or drag the folder onto the upload area. Set the quality to 80 percent, convert, and download the ZIP. Upload the WebP files to your site in place of the JPGs, then test the page to confirm everything displays.

Can I change the quality after converting to WebP?

Yes. The original JPGs stay in the queue after the first conversion, so you can move the quality slider and convert again without re-uploading. Download each version before switching settings, since the download button saves the current conversion.

What quality setting is best for product photos?

80 to 85 percent is best for product photos, since customers zoom into them and inspect fine detail. Lower settings soften fabric texture and small features, which can cost sales. For large hero images, 75 percent is fine, since the slight softening is invisible at full-screen size.

Will my WebP images work in WordPress?

Yes. WordPress has supported WebP uploads since version 5.8. Upload the converted files to the media library like any other image. Test one image first to confirm it displays correctly, then upload the rest of the batch.

Can I convert JPG to WebP without an internet connection?

Once the page has loaded, the conversion runs locally in your browser and does not need the internet. You need a connection only to open the page the first time. After that, you can convert JPGs offline until you close the tab.

What should I do if a WebP file will not open on an old device?

Convert that image back to JPG with the WebP to JPG converter, and use the JPG for the old device. For websites, serve a JPG fallback alongside the WebP, so modern browsers get the smaller file and older ones get the compatible one.
