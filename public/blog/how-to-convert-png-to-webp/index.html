How to Convert PNG to WebP in Seconds

Convert PNG images to WebP in a few steps. Upload, set quality, check transparency, and download — one logo or a whole icon set.

A PNG logo is crisp but heavy, and a PNG hero image can weigh several megabytes. Converting to WebP keeps the transparency and the sharpness while shrinking the file dramatically.

The process takes seconds per image, and you can convert a whole icon set at once. Everything runs in your browser, with nothing to install.

This guide walks through each step, with the settings that protect transparency and text.

Before You Start: What You Need

You need the PNG files and a modern browser. Chrome, Edge, Firefox, and Safari all work, on desktop and on mobile.

Check whether your PNGs have transparency. Logos, icons, and product cutouts usually do, while screenshots and photos usually do not.

Know where the WebP files are going. A website wants 80 to 85 percent quality, while a logo that must stay perfect wants 90 percent.

Files up to 25MB each are accepted. That covers large screenshots, Retina captures, and high-resolution product renders without any preparation.

Step 1: Open the PNG to WebP Tool

Open the PNG to WebP tool in your browser. The page works on Windows, Mac, Linux, ChromeOS, Android, and iOS.

You will see an upload area, a quality slider, and a convert button. Those three controls are the whole job.

No account is needed, and nothing is uploaded. The conversion happens on your device, which matters for client logos and private screenshots.

If you are on a phone, the page works in your mobile browser. Tap the upload area to pick PNGs from your gallery.

Step 2: Upload Your PNG Files

Drag your PNG files onto the upload area, or click it to open the file picker. You can select many files at once for a batch.

Each file appears as a card with a thumbnail, its dimensions, and its size. A quick scan confirms the right files loaded and nothing extra slipped in.

You can also paste a copied image with Ctrl+V on a computer. That is handy when the PNG is in your clipboard from another app.

Interlaced PNGs are supported. The decoder reads them normally, so you do not need to convert them first.

Step 3: Set the Quality Slider

For transparent logos, set the quality to 85 to 90 percent. The edges stay crisp, and the file is far smaller than the PNG.

For screenshots with text, 85 percent keeps the letters readable while shrinking the file substantially. Lower settings soften small text.

For photographs saved as PNG, 80 to 85 percent is the sweet spot. The photo looks clean, and the file is a fraction of the PNG size.

For hero images, 80 percent balances size and quality well. The image fills the screen, yet the file stays small enough for fast loading.

Step 4: Convert the Files

Click the Convert button. The tool decodes each PNG, draws it onto a canvas, and encodes it as WebP.

Conversion is fast, usually a second or two per file. Larger files take a little longer, since there are more pixels to encode.

Each card updates with the new WebP size next to the old PNG size. A good conversion shows a large saving, especially for photos and screenshots.

If a file fails, read the error message. A corrupted PNG or a misnamed file is the usual cause, and the rest of the batch keeps converting.

Step 5: Verify Transparency Survived

Click Compare on a logo card to see the PNG and the WebP side by side. Zoom into the edges and confirm the transparency looks clean.

Look for halos or fringes around the logo. A white or coloured edge where transparency should be means the quality was too low, and raising it fixes the problem.

Check soft shadows too. A drop shadow should fade gradually, not in visible bands, which would mean the quality setting was too aggressive.

If the transparency looks wrong, raise the quality by five points and convert again. The original PNG stays in the queue, so you never re-upload.

Step 6: Download the WebP Files

Click Download on a single card to save one WebP. The filename keeps your original name with a .webp extension.

For a batch, click Download All to get a single ZIP with every converted WebP inside. That is far quicker than downloading them one by one.

Check the downloaded files once before you use them. Open a WebP in your browser and confirm it displays correctly with transparency intact.

Keep the original PNGs as well. A WebP can never become a perfect PNG again, so the originals are your safety net.

Converting a Whole Icon Set

Select all the PNG icons at once in the file picker. They load into the queue, and one click converts them all to WebP.

Use the same quality setting for the whole set. Consistency matters when icons appear together, since mixed quality looks uneven.

Check two or three icons after converting, rather than all fifty. If those look good, the rest will too, since the settings are the same.

Upload the WebP icons to your site in place of the PNGs. Update the references, and test the page to confirm everything displays correctly.

Changing Quality After Converting

You can change the quality slider after converting without re-uploading. The original PNGs stay in the queue, so a new setting is one click away.

Try 85 percent first, then 80 percent, and compare the two results. The difference is often invisible, and the smaller file is the right choice.

For a single important logo, try 90 percent as well. The best setting is the lowest one where you cannot see any difference.

Download each version before switching settings. The download button saves the current conversion, so grab it before you change anything.

Fixing Common Problems

If the WebP comes out larger than the PNG, the image is probably very simple. Keep the PNG for that file and move on.

If the logo edges look fringed, the quality was too low. Raise the slider to 90 percent and convert again from the original PNG.

If the colours look shifted, the PNG may have a colour profile that did not survive. Compare the WebP against the PNG carefully, and keep the PNG if the shift matters.

If a file fails to convert, check that it is a real PNG. A file named .png that is actually a JPG or WebP will confuse the decoder.

Uploading WebP to Your Website

Upload the converted WebP files to your media library like any other image. WordPress, Shopify, and Wix all accept WebP uploads now.

Test one image first. Upload it, view the page, and confirm it displays correctly with transparency intact.

For older browsers, serve a PNG fallback alongside the WebP. Most platforms handle this automatically, or you can use the picture element in your HTML.

Measure the page speed after switching. A page with WebP images should load noticeably faster, which is the whole point of the conversion.

Converting a Logo for a Website

A website logo must load fast and look sharp on every screen. Converting it from PNG to WebP at 90 percent quality gives you both, with transparency intact.

Check the logo on the actual website background after converting. A logo that looks perfect on white may show a faint fringe on a coloured header if the quality was too low.

Test the logo on desktop and phone before publishing it. A logo that is sharp on a large screen may look soft on a small one, and a quick test catches the problem.

Keep the PNG original as a fallback for older browsers. The picture element serves WebP to modern browsers and PNG to the rest, so every visitor sees the logo.

For a logo that appears on every page, the speed saving matters most. A header logo loads on each page view, so a smaller file improves every visit.

Converting Icons for an App

App icons appear at many sizes, from a tiny favicon to a large store listing. Converting them to WebP at 90 percent quality keeps them sharp while using less storage than PNG.

Use the same quality setting for the whole icon set. Consistency matters when icons appear together, since mixed quality looks uneven.

Test the icons on light and dark backgrounds before shipping them. An icon that relies on a white background will vanish on a dark theme, and the fix is a transparent version.

For an app store listing, check the platform's format rules first. Some stores accept WebP, while others demand PNG, and the format must match the store's list.

Keep the PNG masters in the project folder after converting. If a store or a platform rejects WebP, the PNGs are ready without a fresh conversion.

Converting Screenshots for a Blog

Blog screenshots need readable text, and WebP at 85 percent keeps the letters sharp. The text stays cleaner than JPG at the same size, which matters for tutorial images.

Convert the screenshots in one batch, using the same quality for all of them. Consistency matters when the images share a post, since mixed quality looks uneven.

Check the smallest text after converting, using the Compare viewer. Zoom into menus and body text and confirm they read cleanly before publishing.

Update the image references in the blog post after converting. A broken reference is worse than a slow image, so check every image on the page.

Keep the PNG originals in the media library as a fallback. If a reader's browser cannot display WebP, the PNG version loads instead.

Converting Product Images

Product images need care, since customers zoom into them. Convert to WebP at 80 to 85 percent quality, so the fabric texture and small details stay visible.

Sort the products by category before converting. Shoes, shirts, and accessories may need different settings, and sorting first means one clean batch per group.

Check three or four products after converting, rather than all five hundred. If those look good, the rest will too, since the settings are the same across the batch.

Upload the WebP files and update the product listings. Test a few product pages on desktop and phone to confirm the images display correctly.

Keep the JPG or PNG originals as well, since some marketplaces demand them. A product listed on Amazon and on your own shop needs both formats ready.

Converting Images for a Presentation

Presentation images need to be sharp and reasonably sized. A PNG screenshot in a slide looks crisp, while a WebP version is far smaller and loads faster.

Convert screenshots to WebP at 90 percent quality for slides. The text stays readable on a projector, and the file is small enough to keep the deck manageable.

For a full-screen photo on a slide, WebP at 85 percent is the right choice. The photo looks clean, the slide loads fast, and the audience sees the image, not the pixels.

Check the total deck size after converting. A presentation over 50MB may struggle to email or upload, so converting the images is often the fix.

Test the presentation on the projector before the talk. A slide that looks sharp on a laptop may look soft when projected, and a quick test catches the problem early.

Converting a Client's Brand Assets

Client brand assets arrive as PNGs, and the website needs them as WebP. Converting the whole set at once, with one quality setting, keeps the brand consistent.

Agree on the quality setting with the client before converting. A logo at 90 percent and a photo at 80 percent need different batches, so sort the assets first.

Check the brand colours after converting, since saturated colours can shift slightly. A quick comparison against the PNG original confirms the colours match.

Deliver both formats to the client. The WebP files go on the website, while the PNG originals stay in the brand folder for editing and print work.

Document the conversion settings in the project notes. A one-line note prevents the next person from guessing whether the assets are WebP at 80 or 90 percent.

WebP for Online Courses

Online courses are full of screenshots and diagrams, and WebP keeps them readable at a fraction of the PNG size. A course with hundreds of images loads far faster as WebP.

Convert the course screenshots to WebP at 85 percent quality. The text stays readable, the files shrink dramatically, and the lessons feel noticeably quicker.

For diagrams where every label must stay perfect, consider lossless WebP or PNG instead. Lossy WebP is excellent, but not perfect for fine print.

Update the image references after converting, and test the course. A broken image reference is worse than a slow one, so check every lesson after updating it.

Keep the PNG originals in the course repository as a fallback. If a student's browser cannot display WebP, the PNG version loads instead, and nobody sees a broken image.

Frequently asked questions

How do I verify transparency was preserved after converting?

Click Compare on a logo card to see the PNG and WebP side by side. Zoom into the edges and look for halos or fringes, which mean the quality was too low. Check soft shadows too, since they should fade gradually rather than in bands. If anything looks wrong, raise the quality and convert again.

Can I convert PNG screenshots to WebP without blurry text?

Yes, if you keep the quality at 85 percent. WebP preserves text better than JPG at the same file size, so screenshots stay readable. Use the Compare viewer to zoom into the smallest text before downloading, and raise the quality if the letters look soft.

What is the maximum PNG size accepted for conversion?

Each PNG file can be up to 25MB, which covers large screenshots, Retina captures, and high-resolution product renders. Because conversion runs on your device, large files process at local speed with no upload waiting time.

Can I change quality after converting without uploading again?

Yes. The original PNGs stay in the queue after the first conversion, so you can move the quality slider and convert again. Download each version before switching settings, since the download button saves the current conversion.

Does PNG to WebP work on mobile browsers?

Yes. Open the tool in your phone browser, pick PNGs from your gallery, and download the WebP files. The conversion runs on the phone, and the files download to your device. Keep phone batches modest, since large PNGs take time to encode on mobile.

When should I choose PNG to WebP instead of PNG to JPG?

Choose WebP when the image has transparency or will live on a website, since WebP keeps the alpha channel and produces smaller files. Choose JPG when the destination demands it, such as a form, a printer, or an email, where compatibility matters more than size.
