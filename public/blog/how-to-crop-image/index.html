How to Crop an Image

Crop any image online in seconds: upload, drag the selection box, pick an aspect ratio, check the dimensions, and download a clean crop.

Cropping an image takes less than a minute once you know the steps. You upload the photo, drag a box around the part you want, and download the result.

This how-to walks through the whole process. It covers choosing an aspect ratio, using the grid for better framing, checking the output size, and picking the right export format.

By the end you will be able to crop a profile picture, a banner, a screenshot, or a document cleanly on any device. Keep the original file until you have checked the download.

Before You Start: Pick the Destination First

Decide where the cropped image will live before you open the tool. A profile picture needs a square, a banner needs a wide rectangle, and a print needs a specific ratio.

The destination decides the aspect ratio, and the ratio decides the shape of your selection box. Starting with the wrong shape means re-doing the crop later.

Use the original photo, not a compressed or edited copy. Cropping from the best available source keeps the kept pixels as sharp as possible.

Open the Image Cropper tool in your browser. Everything runs locally, so your photo is never uploaded to a server.

Crop an Image in Five Steps

The process is the same for a photo, a screenshot, or a scanned document. Upload, frame, check, export, and verify the download.

Take your time with the framing step. A careful crop now saves you from re-doing the work when the image looks wrong in its destination.

  1. Upload your image by dragging it into the dropzone, choosing it from your device, or pasting a screenshot with Ctrl+V.
  2. Drag the selection box over the part you want to keep, using the eight handles to resize it from any side or corner.
  3. Pick an aspect ratio preset such as 1:1, 4:3, or 16:9, or leave it free for a custom shape.
  4. Check the output pixel dimensions shown by the tool and confirm they are large enough for the destination.
  5. Choose the export format and quality, then click Download to save the cropped image.

Open the downloaded file once before using it. Confirm the framing is right and nothing important was cut off at the edges.

Dragging the Selection Box

The selection box is the rectangle that defines your crop. Drag inside it to move it, or drag one of the eight handles to resize it.

The corner handles resize from two sides at once, which is the fastest way to frame a subject. The edge handles adjust one side only, which is useful for fine-tuning.

When an aspect ratio preset is active, the handles keep the box in proportion. The shape stays locked while the size changes, so a square stays a square.

Everything outside the box darkens on the stage. That dimming helps you see exactly what will be kept and what will be cut away.

Choosing an Aspect Ratio Preset

Pick 1:1 for a square profile picture or an Instagram post. The box stays perfectly square while you drag it, so the framing stays consistent.

Pick 4:3 for a traditional photo shape or a document crop. It is slightly wider than it is tall, which suits most printed photos and scanned pages.

Pick 16:9 for a wide banner, a video thumbnail, or a presentation slide. The wide shape stretches across screens without leaving empty space.

Use free mode when no preset fits. You can drag the box to any shape, which is useful for cutting a single paragraph from a page or a detail from a product photo.

Using the Rule-of-Thirds Grid

The grid divides the selection box into nine equal parts. Place the subject on one of the lines or at a crossing point for a more natural composition.

For a portrait, put the eyes on the upper horizontal line. For a landscape, put the horizon on the upper or lower line rather than in the middle.

The grid also helps you spot a tilted subject. If a horizon runs diagonally across the grid, the photo may need straightening before or after the crop.

Ignore the grid when the subject works better in the centre. A symmetrical building, a logo, or a face-on portrait often looks best dead centre.

Zooming In for Precise Framing

Zoom in when the detail you want is small. A product feature, a face in a crowd, or a single line of text all benefit from a closer look before you place the box.

Zooming does not change the output quality. It only magnifies the preview so you can position the selection accurately, and the export still uses the original pixels.

After zooming, drag the image to centre the detail, then size the box around it. Check the dimension readout to confirm the crop is still large enough.

If the zoomed crop is too small for its destination, widen the box or use a larger source image. A tiny crop stretched to fit a banner will look soft.

Checking Output Dimensions Before Downloading

The tool shows the exact output pixel dimensions before you download. This is the most important check in the whole process, and it takes two seconds.

Compare the number with what your destination requires. A profile picture needs only a few hundred pixels, while a website banner may need over a thousand.

If the crop is too small, widen the selection box. A wider box captures more pixels, which gives the destination more to work with.

The Select Full button resets the box to the whole image. Use it when you have cropped too far and want to start the framing again from scratch.

Choosing the Export Format and Quality

Choose JPG for photos. The quality slider lets you balance file size against detail, and a setting around 85 to 90 keeps most photos looking clean.

Choose PNG for screenshots, logos, and graphics with text. PNG is lossless, so the edges of letters stay sharp, and it preserves transparency when you need it.

Choose WebP when you want small files with good quality. It works well for web images, though a few older systems still reject the format.

Remember that the quality slider disappears for PNG, because PNG has no lossy compression to adjust. That is normal, not a bug in the tool.

Cropping a Screenshot

Paste a screenshot directly with Ctrl+V to skip the save step. The image appears in the cropper immediately, ready to trim.

Drag the box around the part that matters: the error message, the setting, or the button. Cut away the browser toolbar, the taskbar, and any empty space.

Export screenshots as PNG. The lossless format keeps small text readable, while JPG compression blurs the edges of letters and makes them harder to read.

Name the file clearly, such as error-message.png. A clear name makes the screenshot easy to find when you attach it to a message or a bug report.

Cropping on a Phone or Tablet

The cropper works with finger gestures on touchscreens. Drag the box with one finger, and pinch to zoom when you need a closer look at the framing.

Upload from your gallery or take a photo directly. The tool handles the file the same way it does on a computer, and the export options are identical.

Small screens make precise framing harder, so zoom in before placing the box. A careful crop on a phone is still better than a rushed one on a desktop.

Downloaded crops save to your usual downloads folder. From there you can share them to a chat app, an email, or a social post without extra steps.

Troubleshooting Common Crop Problems

If the selection box will not change shape, check whether an aspect ratio preset is locked. Switch to free mode to drag the box to any shape you like.

If the downloaded crop looks blurry, the selection was probably too small. Return to the original file and widen the box, or start from a higher-resolution source.

If the crop is the wrong shape for its destination, check the ratio preset. A 1:1 crop will not fit a wide banner, and a 16:9 crop will not fit a square profile slot.

If the image looks dark outside the box, that is the stage dimming the area that will be removed. It is a preview effect, not a change to your file.

Quick Tips for Better Crops

Crop from the original file every time. Re-cropping a JPG that was already cropped and saved adds damage with each round.

Leave a little breathing room around the subject. A face with no headroom looks cramped, and a product touching the edge looks like a mistake.

Check the crop at full size before downloading. Zoom into the face or the text and confirm that nothing important is soft or cut off.

Save the cropped file with a clear name. A name like banner-16x9.png is useful months later, while a name like IMG_4821.png is not.

Cropping Product Photos

Product photos need consistent framing, because a shop page with mismatched crops looks careless. Crop every product image to the same ratio so the grid lines up neatly.

Leave a little space around the product. A tight crop that touches the edges makes the item look cramped, while a small margin gives it room to breathe.

Use a plain background and crop away any clutter at the edges. A clean product shot with a white or light background sells better than a busy one with distractions.

For a shop that shows many products, crop in a batch style: same ratio, same margins, same background. Consistency across the catalogue matters more than any single perfect crop.

Cropping Images for Email

Images in emails need to be small and correctly shaped, because a large photo can push the text off screen or make the message slow to load.

Crop the image to the part that matters before attaching it. A full screenshot with a tiny detail is useless in an email, while a tight crop of the detail is immediately clear.

Export email images as JPG for photos and PNG for screenshots. The smaller file size keeps the email light, and the right format keeps the content readable.

Check the cropped image on a phone before sending. Most email is read on mobile, and a crop that looks fine on a desktop may be too small or too wide on a phone screen.

Cropping and Accessibility

A good crop helps everyone, but it matters most for people who view images at large sizes or with screen magnifiers. A tight crop of the important content is easier to follow than a wide shot full of distractions.

When you crop a diagram or a chart, keep the labels and the legend inside the frame. A chart without its labels is meaningless, no matter how sharp the crop is.

For instructional images, crop to one step at a time. A single clear crop per step is easier to follow than one large image that shows the whole process at once.

Add a short caption or alt text when you share a cropped image. The caption explains what the crop shows, which helps people who cannot see the image clearly.

Saving Time with Repeatable Crops

If you crop the same kind of image often, build a habit around it. Use the same ratio preset, the same export format, and the same naming pattern every time.

A consistent routine saves minutes on every crop. You stop deciding the ratio and the format from scratch, and you start producing clean crops without thinking about it.

Keep a note of the ratios your platforms need. A short list on your desk, such as profile 1:1 and banner 16:9, removes the guesswork from every cropping session.

Review your crops occasionally. Platforms change their size rules, and a ratio that worked last year may no longer fit the slot you are targeting.

Frequently asked questions

How do I crop an image into a square for Instagram?

Upload the photo, select the 1:1 ratio preset, and drag the square box over the part you want. Keep the subject centred with a little breathing room, check the output dimensions, then download as JPG for a photo or PNG for a graphic.

Can I crop a photo without installing any app?

Yes. The cropper runs entirely in your browser on any device, with no install or account needed. Upload the image, drag the selection box, choose a ratio, and download the result. Your photo is never uploaded to a server.

What is the best format to download a cropped screenshot in?

PNG, because it is lossless and keeps small text sharp. JPG compression blurs the edges of letters, which makes screenshots harder to read. Use JPG only for cropped photos, where the smaller file size matters more than pixel-perfect edges.

How do I crop out a person or object at the edge of a photo?

Drag the selection box so the unwanted person or object falls outside it, then download the crop. The dimmed area on the stage shows exactly what will be removed. If the object sits in the middle of the frame, cropping cannot remove it, so blur it instead.

Why is my cropped image blurry when I upload it?

The selection box was probably too small, so the crop has fewer pixels than the destination needs. Return to the original file, widen the box, and check the output dimensions before downloading. Starting from a higher-resolution original also helps.

Can I undo a crop after downloading it?

Not from the downloaded file, since cropping permanently removes the cut-away pixels. Keep the original photo so you can crop it again differently. In the tool, use the Select Full button to reset the box before you download.
