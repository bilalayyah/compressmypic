How to Make a Photo Collage

Make a photo collage in minutes: pick your photos, choose a grid or strip layout, arrange them, set gaps and background, and export a share-ready image.

Making a photo collage is a short job: pick a few photos, choose a layout, arrange them, and export one image. This guide walks through each step in order, from choosing photos to sharing the finished collage.

You need just one thing before you start: the photos you want to combine. Four to nine strong photos with similar light make the best collage. A set of photos from one trip, one event, or one product range works better than a random mix.

The image collage maker does the work in your browser. Nothing uploads to a server, there is no account, and the export is free. Follow the steps below and you will have a finished collage in a few minutes.

Step 1: Choose Your Photos

Pick the photos that belong together. A collage tells a small story, so the photos should share a subject: a trip, a birthday, a product range, or a family gathering. Photos from one event feel like a collection; photos from five events feel like a random pile.

Choose four to nine photos. Four fill a 2 by 2 grid cleanly, and nine fill a 3 by 3 grid. Fewer than four leaves empty cells, and more than nine makes each photo too small to enjoy. Quality beats quantity in a collage.

Pick photos with similar light and color. A set where one photo is dark and blurry draws the eye to the weak photo, while a set with consistent light looks like a deliberate collection. If one photo is much darker than the rest, brighten it before you start or leave it out.

Choose a hero photo. One strong image will become the feature photo, enlarged above the rest, so pick the shot that best represents the set. The hero is usually the clearest, brightest, or most emotional photo of the group.

Step 2: Upload the Photos

Open the collage maker and drop your photos onto the upload zone, or click to choose them from your device. You can upload them all at once, and the tool will place them into the grid in the order you chose them.

The tool accepts JPG, PNG, WebP, HEIC, GIF, BMP, and SVG files up to 20 MB each. Phone photos, screenshots, and graphics all work, and a mix of formats in one collage is fine. An iPhone HEIC photo uploads like any other file.

Dropping five or more photos auto-picks the 3 by 3 grid, while four or fewer auto-picks the 2 by 2 grid. If you upload fewer photos than the grid has cells, the empty cells wait for more photos, and you can add them later.

If you uploaded the wrong photo, remove it without starting over. The tool lets you remove a single photo from a cell and replace it, so a mistake costs seconds rather than a rebuild of the whole collage.

Step 3: Pick a Layout

Choose a grid or a strip layout. The 2 by 2 grid suits four photos, the 3 by 3 grid suits nine, the vertical strip suits a sequence read top to bottom, and the horizontal strip suits a sequence read left to right.

Grids suit sets where every photo matters equally: a trip, an event, or a product range. The square shape fits feeds and listings, and the equal cells give every photo the same weight.

Strips suit sequences and comparisons. A vertical strip reads like a story, which suits a day in order. A horizontal strip reads like a timeline, which suits a before-and-after or a panorama of moments.

Change the layout freely after uploading. The photos stay in place and the cells rearrange, so you can compare a grid and a strip in seconds. The layout that reads best at a glance is usually the right one.

Step 4: Arrange the Photos

Drag a photo onto another cell to swap them on desktop, or tap to select and tap again to swap on a phone. The swap is instant, and you can rearrange as many times as you like before exporting.

Put the hero photo in the most prominent cell. In a 2 by 2 grid, that is the top-left cell, which the eye sees first. In a 3 by 3 grid, the center cell draws the eye, so the hero belongs there or in the top-left.

Balance light and dark across the grid. A dark photo beside another dark photo creates a heavy corner, while a dark photo beside a light one balances the collage. Spread the bright and dark photos evenly rather than clustering them.

Try several arrangements before you commit. A photo that looks lost in one cell can anchor the collage in another, and a swap that takes a second can change the whole balance. Trust the arrangement that reads best at a glance.

Step 5: Feature a Photo

Make the hero photo larger than the rest by adjusting the splits. Drag a split line to enlarge the hero cell and shrink its neighbors, which gives the collage a focal point and a hierarchy instead of a flat grid.

Choose the feature by quality as well as by subject. The hero should be the sharpest, brightest photo of the set, because enlarging a weak photo only draws attention to its flaws. A strong photo enlarged looks deliberate; a weak one enlarged looks like a mistake.

Keep the feature to one photo. Two large cells compete with each other and the hierarchy collapses, so enlarge one cell clearly above the rest and leave the others equal. One feature is the rule that makes the collage read at a glance.

If the splits become uneven and you want to start over, return them to a uniform grid. The tool resets the splits to equal cells, so you can try a different feature arrangement without rebuilding the collage.

Step 6: Adjust Gaps and Corners

Set the gap between cells. A zero gap makes a seamless borderless collage, where the photos touch and form one continuous image. A visible gap separates the photos into distinct frames, which suits a scrapbook or a gallery look.

Set the corner radius for the cells. A small radius softens the collage and suits social posts, while square corners feel formal and suit prints and listings. A large radius turns each cell into a rounded tile, which suits playful designs.

Gap and corner radius are independent, so you can combine them freely. A wide gap with square corners feels like a gallery wall, while a narrow gap with rounded corners feels like an app. The combination sets the mood of the collage.

The background color shows through the gaps, so choose the gap and the background as a pair. A white gap on a white background looks like a clean border, while a brand color in the gaps ties the collage to a campaign.

Step 7: Set the Background

Choose the background color from the quick presets. White is the default and the safest choice: it keeps the collage clean, it matches any feed or page, and it makes the photos the only color in the image.

A brand color turns the collage into a designed graphic. The color in the gaps ties the collage to a campaign, and a set of collages in the same color forms a recognizable series. Use it when the collage is an advertisement as much as a collection.

Transparent PNG stickers placed in cells show the background color behind them. A sticker on a white background reads as a clean cutout, while a sticker on a colored background gains a tint from the color behind it. Check stickers against the background before exporting.

Keep the background simple. A background that fights the photos draws the eye to the gaps instead of the images, and a bright gap color between muted photos makes the collage feel busy. White or a soft neutral is the safe choice.

Step 8: Choose the Canvas Size

Pick a canvas size and ratio for the destination. Square suits feeds, listings, and thumbnails. Portrait suits stories and phone wallpapers. Wide suits banners and headers. The tool offers quick presets for each shape.

The export resolution goes up to 4000 pixels, which is large enough for printing. Choose a large export when the collage might be printed, and a moderate one when it will only be posted online. You can always downscale later, but you cannot add pixels back.

Check the preview at the chosen ratio. A collage that looks balanced as a square can look stretched as a portrait, because the cells change shape. Adjust the arrangement after changing the ratio, since the best layout for one shape may not suit another.

If you are not sure where the collage will be used, choose square. It is the most forgiving shape, it crops predictably everywhere, and it works as a thumbnail, a listing image, and a feed post without further edits.

Step 9: Preview the Collage

Look at the full preview before exporting. Check that every photo is in the right cell, the hero photo is prominent, the gaps are even, and the background color looks the way you intended. A preview at full size shows details a thumbnail hides.

Check each cell for bad crops. The Cover fit can cut off a face, a product label, or a line of text at the edge of a photo. Switch the fit to Contain for any cell where the crop hurts, and accept the border that comes with it.

Look at the collage on your phone if it is destined for social media. A gap color that looks subtle on a desktop monitor can look loud on a phone screen, and a collage that looks balanced large can look busy small. The phone is where the audience sees it.

Compare the collage at a small size too. Shrink your view to thumbnail size and check that the collage still reads. A collage that only works at full size fails as a feed post, where it is seen small among many others.

Step 10: Export and Share

Choose the export format. JPG suits photographs and any collage where a small file matters, such as a social post. PNG suits collages with sharp text, flat colors, or transparency that must survive the export.

Click download and the finished collage saves to your device. The filename reflects the collage settings, so you can tell versions apart when you export several. Open the file and look at it once more before sharing.

Share the collage as a single image: upload it to a social feed, attach it to a message, or add it to a listing. The collage is one file, so it posts like any other image, and the photos inside it travel together wherever the file goes.

Keep the source photos as well as the finished collage. If you need a different ratio, a different layout, or a corrected photo later, the sources let you rebuild in minutes. A flat exported collage cannot be edited back into its parts easily.

Collages for Print

A collage for print needs a large export. Choose the maximum resolution, up to 4000 pixels, so the print stays sharp at the size you want. A collage exported for social media prints poorly at a large size, because the pixels are not there.

Choose PNG for the sharpest print, or a high-quality JPG for a photographic print. PNG keeps edges and text crisp, while JPG compresses photos efficiently. Either works at a large export size; the choice depends on whether the collage is graphic or photographic.

Check the aspect ratio against the paper or frame size. A square collage fits a square frame, while a portrait collage fits a standard photo print. A mismatch means cropping the print or living with white borders, so match the ratio before you export.

Print a small test first when the collage matters, such as a gift or a framed print. A test print reveals color shifts and sharpness problems that the screen hides, and it costs far less than reprinting a large frame.

Fixing a Collage That Looks Off

A collage that looks off usually has one of four problems: a weak photo, a bad crop, an unbalanced arrangement, or a gap color that fights the photos. Fix them in that order, starting with the photos themselves.

Replace the weakest photo first. A dark, blurry, or off-topic photo draws the eye no matter where it sits, and no arrangement can hide it. Swap it for a stronger photo from the same set, or drop to a smaller grid without it.

Next, check the crops. A face cut off at the edge of a cell or a subject squeezed by the fit ruins the cell. Switch the fit to Contain for that cell, or choose a different photo for it, and the collage improves immediately.

Then rebalance the arrangement. Move the bright photos apart, move the hero to the prominent cell, and spread the dark photos evenly. A single swap often fixes a collage that felt wrong without an obvious cause.

Finally, simplify the gaps and background. A bright gap color or a busy background can make a good collage look noisy. Set the gaps to white and the corners to a small radius, and the photos will carry the collage on their own.

Quick Reference: Collage Checklist

Pick four to nine strong photos with similar light, upload them, and choose a grid or a strip. Arrange by dragging or tapping to swap, enlarge the hero photo, and set the gaps, corners, and background.

Choose a canvas ratio for the destination, export JPG for photos and PNG for graphics, and check every cell for bad crops at full size and at thumbnail size. Keep the source photos for rebuilding later.

Frequently asked questions

How do I choose which photos go in a collage?

Pick photos that share a subject, such as one trip, one event, or one product range, and choose four to nine of the strongest. Photos with similar light and color look like a collection, while a random mix looks like a pile. Leave out any photo that is dark, blurry, or off-topic.

What if I upload fewer photos than the layout has cells?

The empty cells wait for more photos, and you can add them later. You can also switch to a smaller layout, such as a 2 by 2 grid for four photos, so there are no empty cells. Do not export with blank cells unless the empty space is deliberate.

Can I move a photo to a different cell?

Yes. Drag a photo onto another cell to swap them on desktop, or tap to select and tap again to swap on a phone or tablet. You can rearrange as many times as you like before exporting, and the preview updates instantly.

Should I save my collage as JPG or PNG?

Use JPG for photo collages and for any image where a small file matters, such as a social post. Use PNG for collages with sharp text, flat colors, or transparency, because PNG keeps every edge crisp and preserves transparent areas.

How do I print my collage?

Export at the largest resolution, up to 4000 pixels, and match the canvas ratio to the paper or frame size. Choose PNG for the sharpest print or a high-quality JPG for a photographic print, and run a small test print first for an important piece.

Can I make a collage on my phone?

Yes, the collage maker runs in the browser on phones and tablets. Upload from your photo library, tap to swap photos between cells, and download the finished collage. Tap-to-swap replaces dragging on touch screens, so arranging works reliably on mobile.
