How to Remove Metadata from an Image

Step-by-step guide to removing metadata from an image: upload, strip EXIF and GPS, choose the right method, download clean files, and verify the result.

Removing metadata from an image takes less than a minute. You upload the photo, the tool strips the hidden data, and you download a clean copy.

This guide walks through each step with the free Remove Image Metadata tool. It works in your browser, needs no account, and never uploads your photos anywhere.

By the end you will know how to clean one photo, clean a whole batch, pick the right stripping method, and prove the result is clean.

What You Need Before You Start

You need the image file you plan to share, saved on your device. Work from the original file, not from a copy you already posted somewhere.

The tool accepts JPG, PNG, WebP, and HEIC files up to 20 MB each. That covers phone photos, DSLR JPEGs, PNG exports, and iPhone HEIC files.

Keep a private copy of the original if the metadata matters to you. Stripping is permanent for the cleaned copy, and camera settings or GPS data cannot be recovered from it later.

Open the tool in any modern browser. Chrome, Firefox, Safari, and Edge all work, on desktop and on mobile.

If you are unsure what your photo contains, check it first in the Image Metadata Viewer. It shows every tag before you decide what to remove.

Step 1: Open the Tool

Go to the Remove Image Metadata page. You will see an upload box and a short explanation of what the tool does.

There is no sign-in screen and no paywall. The tool is free, and you can clean unlimited photos with zero watermarks or fees.

On a phone, open the page in mobile Safari or Chrome. The layout adapts, and you can pick photos directly from your camera roll.

Bookmark the page if you clean photos often. Sellers, journalists, and parents tend to use it before every public post, so quick access helps.

Step 2: Upload Your Photos

Drag photos into the upload box, or click it to pick files from your device. You can drop one photo or a whole batch at once.

You can also paste with Ctrl+V on Windows or Cmd+V on Mac. A copied image or screenshot drops straight into the upload queue and is scrubbed automatically.

Cleaning starts immediately upon upload. There is no start button to press, and no waiting for a server, because all the work happens inside your browser memory.

Each file can be up to 20 MB. If a file is larger, compress it first with the image compressor, then clean the smaller copy.

Within a moment, a card appears for each photo. That card shows the file name, the stripping result, and the verification badge.

Step 3: Check the Verification Badges

After upload, each card re-scans automatically and shows how many metadata tags were stripped, for example Stripped X metadata tags to 0 remaining.

A green or emerald badge means the output was verified clean. The tool runs its metadata parser a second time on the output file, so the badge is a real check, not a guess.

If a photo shows Verified clean right after upload, the source already contained zero EXIF, GPS, or text chunks. Screenshots and photos downloaded from messaging apps commonly arrive this way.

Wait for the badges to turn green across all cards before downloading. A batch is only finished when every card is verified.

If a card shows tags remaining, the file needs another pass. Change the stripping method and click Re-clean All, as explained in step 5.

Step 4: Choose the Stripping Method

The default method is Lossless Binary Strip, and it is the right choice for most JPG and PNG photos. It removes metadata segments without decoding or recompressing a single pixel.

Keep this method when you want zero quality change. Your JPG stays a JPG, your PNG stays a PNG, and transparent PNG backgrounds survive byte-for-byte.

Pick Full Canvas Re-encode when you want a brand-new bitmap with zero inherited segments. It is also used automatically for WebP and HEIC inputs, and whenever you change the output format.

Re-encoding also fixes orientation. If a vertical JPEG looks sideways after a lossless strip, the orientation flag was deleted without rotating the pixels. Canvas re-encode bakes the upright orientation into the exported image.

The choice is reversible before you download. Change the method, click Re-clean All, and the batch is processed again with the new setting.

Step 5: Set Output Format and Quality

By default the tool keeps the original format, so a JPG stays a JPG and a PNG stays a PNG. That is the safest choice when you only want metadata gone.

To convert while cleaning, pick JPG, PNG, or WebP under Output Format. Each file re-encodes cleanly through Canvas with all metadata stripped and the chosen format applied.

Set the Re-encode Quality slider between 50 and 100 percent when you re-encode. Quality near 85 percent is a good default for WebP and JPG exports, balancing size and detail.

The slider does nothing for Lossless Binary Strip files, because no pixels are re-encoded. It only applies to WebP and HEIC inputs, or to Full Canvas Re-encode exports.

After changing any setting, click Re-clean All. Files scrub immediately on drop using your active settings, so the button applies your updated options across the entire batch.

For the full comparison of the two methods, read Remove Image Metadata: Complete Guide.

Step 6: Download the Clean Files

When the badges are green, download your files. Click Download Clean on an individual card to save that photo, or click Download All Clean (ZIP) for the whole batch.

The ZIP file is named clean_images_no_metadata.zip. It holds every cleaned photo from the batch, ready to upload to a marketplace, forum, or listing.

Downloads are separate files. Your originals on disk are untouched, because browsers cannot overwrite files in your local folders.

Save the clean files somewhere you can tell them apart from the originals, such as a folder named clean or ready-to-post. Mixing them up defeats the purpose.

Share the cleaned copies, not the originals. If you upload the original by mistake, the metadata goes with it.

Step 7: Verify the Cleaned File

Verification is the step most people skip, and it is the step that proves the job is done. Drop a downloaded clean file into the Image Metadata Viewer.

If scrubbing succeeded, the green banner confirms that no EXIF, GPS, or hidden camera tags were detected, and no GPS alert appears. That is an independent check of the file bytes.

If tags remain, the viewer table shows exactly which ones survived. Common survivors are PNG text chunks and JPEG comments, which some cleaners miss.

When leftovers appear, return to the removal tool, switch to Full Canvas Re-encode, click Re-clean All, and download again. Then verify the new file the same way.

Make this a two-pass habit for sensitive photos: inspect, strip, inspect again. The second inspection takes seconds and removes all doubt.

Removing Metadata on a Phone

Most photos are shared from phones, so the workflow works in mobile browsers. Open the tool in iOS Safari or Android Chrome and pick photos from your camera roll.

Files scrub on upload, the badges turn green, and you download clean copies straight to your device. Then share the cleaned files from your gallery.

Clean before you open the social or marketplace app, not after. Once a photo is posted, anyone may have already downloaded the original with its metadata intact.

If your phone camera records location by default, consider turning off location for the camera app. That stops GPS tags at the source, so future photos need no cleaning for location.

Cleaning Batches for Marketplaces

Sellers photograph products at home, and those photos can carry GPS coordinates pointing straight to the seller's address. Batch cleaning removes that risk for a whole shoot at once.

Drop the full set of listing photos together and wait for the badges to turn green across all cards. Then click Download All Clean (ZIP) and upload the cleaned files to the listing.

Use the same routine for forum posts, rental listings, and classified ads. Anywhere strangers download full-resolution images, metadata is a risk worth removing.

There is no limit on file count beyond the 20 MB cap per file. A hundred listing photos clean just as easily as one, with no watermarks, signups, or fees.

For the privacy risks behind this routine, read Photo Privacy: GPS, EXIF and Metadata Risks.

Troubleshooting: When Cleaning Does Not Work as Expected

Most problems have a simple cause. Work through the list below before assuming the tool is broken.

  • A photo looks sideways after cleaning. The lossless strip deleted the EXIF orientation flag. Switch to Full Canvas Re-encode and click Re-clean All to bake the upright orientation into the pixels.
  • A file still shows tags after cleaning. Some PNG text chunks or JPEG comments can survive a first pass. Try Full Canvas Re-encode, which rebuilds the image from scratch.
  • The quality slider seems to do nothing. It only applies to WebP and HEIC inputs, or to Full Canvas Re-encode exports. Lossless Binary Strip never re-encodes pixels, so the slider has no effect there.
  • A file will not upload. Check the size: the cap is 20 MB per file. Compress the image first, then clean the smaller copy.
  • The downloaded file looks identical to the original. That is correct. Cleaning removes hidden data, not pixels, so a successful clean looks exactly the same as the original.
  • The badge says Verified clean immediately. The source already had no metadata, which is common for screenshots and photos from messaging apps. Nothing was wrong.

If a problem persists, test with a fresh original straight from the camera roll. That tells you whether the file or the settings are the cause.

Removing Metadata from HEIC Photos

iPhone photos often arrive as HEIC files, and they need slightly different handling. The tool accepts HEIC uploads up to 20 MB, just like JPG and PNG.

HEIC files are not stripped with Lossless Binary Strip. Instead, the tool draws the pixels onto a fresh Canvas, which guarantees all metadata headers are dropped.

That means an HEIC clean is always a re-encode. Set the Re-encode Quality slider before you download: near 85 percent is a good default, and 90 to 100 percent keeps the most detail.

You can also convert an HEIC photo while cleaning it. Pick JPG, PNG, or WebP under Output Format, and the tool exports a clean copy in the format you chose.

Converting HEIC to JPG is a common move before sharing, because JPG opens everywhere. The conversion and the cleaning happen in one step, so no second pass is needed.

After downloading, verify the result the same way as any other file. Drop the cleaned copy into the Image Metadata Viewer and confirm the green banner.

What to Do After Downloading

Downloading the clean file is not the last step. A few small habits after downloading make sure the clean copy is the one that actually gets shared.

First, check the file name. The cleaned download may share a name with the original, so keep it in a separate folder to avoid mixing the two up.

Second, verify at least one file from each batch. Open the cleaned copy in the viewer and confirm the green no-tags banner before you upload it anywhere.

Third, delete the clean copies from your downloads folder once they are posted, if the originals live elsewhere. Old clean copies mixed with originals cause confusion later.

Fourth, if you share from a phone, confirm the share sheet picked the cleaned file. Gallery apps can look identical for originals and cleaned copies, so check the file name or the folder.

Quick Recap

Open the Remove Image Metadata tool, drop in your photos, and wait for the green verification badges. Choose Lossless Binary Strip for JPG and PNG, or Full Canvas Re-encode for WebP, HEIC, format changes, or sideways photos.

Download the clean files or the ZIP, then verify one in the Image Metadata Viewer. When the green banner appears, the photo is safe to share.

The whole routine takes under a minute. Make it a habit before every public post, and your photos will carry only what you chose to share.

Frequently asked questions

How do I remove metadata from a photo on my iPhone?

Open the Remove Image Metadata tool in mobile Safari, pick the photo from your camera roll, and wait for the green verification badge. Then tap Download Clean to save the cleaned copy to your device. Share the cleaned copy, not the original from your camera roll.

Can I remove metadata from a screenshot?

Screenshots usually carry no camera metadata in the first place, because the clipboard passes raw pixels rather than the original camera file. Dropping a screenshot into the tool will typically show Verified clean immediately. The tool still checks it, so you lose nothing by running it through.

What is the fastest way to clean many photos before a listing goes live?

Drop the whole set into the tool at once, wait for the green badges across all cards, and click Download All Clean (ZIP). The ZIP holds every cleaned photo in one file. Upload the cleaned files to your listing — never the originals.

Do I need to clean a photo if I am only sending it to one friend?

It depends on the channel. Messaging apps such as WhatsApp strip metadata automatically, so a photo sent that way usually arrives clean. Email attachments and direct file transfers often keep the original intact, so clean the photo first if you are sending it as a file.

Why does my cleaned photo have a smaller file size than the original?

Camera photos often carry embedded EXIF preview thumbnails, XMP blocks, and comment segments. Stripping those in Lossless Binary Strip mode frequently trims tens of kilobytes with zero change in image sharpness. The smaller size is a bonus, not a sign of quality loss.

Can I undo metadata removal after downloading the clean file?

No. The cleaned copy has no metadata left to restore. That is why you should keep your original file in a private folder before cleaning. The original keeps its camera settings and GPS data for your own records, while the cleaned copy is the one you share.
