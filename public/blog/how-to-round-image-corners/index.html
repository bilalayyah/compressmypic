How to Round Image Corners

Round image corners in minutes: upload, set the radius, use per-corner control or the circle shortcut, choose transparent or solid fill, and download.

Rounding the corners of a photo takes only a few minutes. You upload the image, drag a slider to set the curve, and download the softened result.

This how-to walks through the whole process step by step. It covers the radius slider, per-corner control, the full circle shortcut, fill choices, and the export formats that keep corners transparent.

By the end you will be able to make a rounded avatar, a card image, or a pill-shaped banner on any device. Keep the original photo until you have checked the result.

Before You Start: Pick the Shape

Decide what shape you need before you open the tool. A circle suits an avatar, a gentle rounding suits a card, and a pill suits a banner or a badge.

Check the destination as well. A profile slot may demand a circle, while a blog card may need only the top two corners rounded.

Use the original photo, not a compressed copy. Rounding from a sharp original keeps the curved edges smooth, while rounding a soft copy makes the curves look rough.

Open the Round Image Corners tool in your browser. Everything runs locally, so your photo is never uploaded to a server.

Round Image Corners in Five Steps

The process is the same for a gentle rounding and a full circle. Upload, set the radius, choose the fill, check the preview, and download.

Take your time with the radius. A small change in the value can make a big difference in how the shape feels, so watch the preview as you drag.

  1. Upload your image by dragging it into the dropzone, choosing it from your device, or pasting a screenshot with Ctrl+V.
  2. Drag the All Corners Radius slider to set the curve, watching the live pixel readout as you adjust it.
  3. Turn on per-corner mode to set different radii, or use the Full Circle / Pill shortcut for a round or pill shape.
  4. Choose Transparent under Outer Corner Fill for see-through corners, or Solid Color for a filled background.
  5. Pick the export format, PNG or WebP to keep transparency, then click Download to save the result.

Open the downloaded file once before you use it. Confirm the corners look smooth and nothing important was cut away.

Setting the Corner Radius

The radius slider controls how round the corners are. A small value gives a gentle softening, while a large value turns the corners into deep curves.

A radius of 10 to 20 pixels suits cards, thumbnails, and screenshots. The rounding is subtle, and the image still feels like a rectangle with soft edges.

A radius of 50 to 100 pixels gives a clearly rounded look. It suits avatars, social posts, and app graphics, where the shape is part of the design.

Watch the live pixel readout next to the slider. It shows the exact radius value, so you can reproduce the same rounding on another image later.

Using Per-Corner Mode

Per-corner mode lets you set a different radius for each corner. Turn it on, and four separate sliders appear, one for each corner of the image.

A blog card is the classic use case. Round the top two corners and leave the bottom two square, so the photo sits neatly at the top of the card.

Turning per-corner mode off returns to a single radius for all corners. The individual values are kept while the mode is off, so you can switch back without losing them.

Use per-corner control sparingly. A design with four different radii looks accidental, while one or two rounded corners with the rest square looks deliberate.

Using the Full Circle / Pill Shortcut

The Full Circle shortcut sets the radius to half the image size in one click. On a square photo it creates a perfect circle, and on a rectangle it creates a pill shape.

For a clean circle, crop the photo to 1:1 first. A square source with the shortcut gives a perfect round avatar that fits any profile slot.

On a rectangular photo, the shortcut creates a pill: a rectangle with fully rounded ends. Pills suit buttons, badges, and banners.

Check the result before downloading. A pill shape can look elegant, but it can also crop away important content at the sides of a wide photo.

Choosing Transparent or Solid Fill

When corners are cut away, the empty space must be filled. Transparent leaves the corners see-through, while solid fills them with a colour.

Choose transparent for web graphics, avatars, and overlays. The image blends with any background, because the corners show whatever is behind the image.

Choose solid when the destination cannot handle transparency. A solid white fill makes the image look like a rounded photo on a white card, which suits documents and prints.

If you click transparent while JPG is selected, the tool switches the format automatically. JPG has no transparency, so it moves you to PNG or WebP.

Choosing the Export Format

Choose PNG for the sharpest result with transparency. It is lossless, so the curved edges stay smooth, and it works everywhere, including old browsers.

Choose WebP when you want small files with transparency. It gives smaller files than PNG with the same see-through corners, though a few older systems reject it.

Choose JPG only when the corners have a solid fill. JPG has no transparency, so a transparent export as JPG gets a solid fill, which may not match your background.

For rounded web graphics, PNG or WebP is the right choice. For a rounded photo on a white page, JPG with a white fill works fine.

Previewing Before You Download

Always preview before downloading. Check that the corners are smooth, that the radius feels right, and that nothing important was cut away.

Zoom into a corner and look at the curve. A smooth arc means the rounding worked well, while a jagged edge means the source was too small or too soft.

Check the fill on the background where the image will live. A transparent corner looks right anywhere, while a solid fill only matches the colour it was set to.

If the radius feels wrong, adjust the slider and check again. A small change in the value can make a big difference in how the shape feels.

Rounding Corners for an Avatar

Crop the photo to a square first, using the Image Cropper. A 1:1 crop with the face centred gives the best starting point for a round avatar.

Upload the square photo and click the Full Circle shortcut. The radius jumps to half the image size, and the photo becomes a perfect circle.

Keep the face well inside the frame. The corners are cut away, so a face near the edge may lose an ear or part of the hair to the curve.

Export as PNG or WebP. The transparency keeps the corners see-through, so the avatar blends with any profile background, light or dark.

Rounding Corners for Cards and UI

Match the photo radius to the card radius. A photo with a 16 pixel radius inside a card with a 16 pixel radius looks seamless, while mismatched radii look careless.

For a blog card, round the top two corners and leave the bottom two square. Turn on per-corner mode, set the top radii, and leave the bottom ones at zero.

Keep the radius consistent across all cards in a set. A grid of cards with matching corners looks professional, while mixed radii look random.

Export card images as PNG or WebP when the card sits on a coloured background. The transparency keeps the corners clean on any colour.

Rounding Corners on a Screenshot

Paste a screenshot directly with Ctrl+V to skip the save step. The image appears in the tool immediately, ready to round.

Use a small radius of 10 to 20 pixels for screenshots. A gentle rounding makes the capture look like a modern app card without hiding any of the content.

Export rounded screenshots as PNG. The lossless format keeps the small text sharp, which matters when the screenshot shows a setting or an error message.

Keep the radius consistent across all screenshots in a guide. Matching corners help the reader follow the steps, while mixed radii distract from the instructions.

Rounding Corners on a Phone or Tablet

The tool works on touchscreens, so you can round a photo on the phone that took it. Upload from your gallery, drag the radius slider, and download the result.

Use the Full Circle shortcut for avatars on a small screen. One tap turns a square photo into a circle, which is faster than dragging the slider to the exact value.

Check the preview at full size before downloading. A radius that looks subtle on a small phone screen may be more obvious when viewed on a computer.

Downloaded files save to your usual downloads folder. From there you can set the rounded image as a profile picture or share it directly.

Troubleshooting Common Rounding Problems

If the corners show a white background instead of transparency, check the export format. JPG has no transparency, so export as PNG or WebP to keep the corners see-through.

If the shape is a pill instead of a circle, the source photo is rectangular. Crop it to a square first, then apply the Full Circle shortcut.

If the corners look jagged, the source image may be too small. Start from a higher-resolution original, and the curves will render smoothly.

If your per-corner values vanished, check whether you turned the mode off. The values are kept while the mode is off, so turning it back on restores them.

Combining Rounded Corners with a Border

Round the corners first, then add a border with the Image Border Generator. The border tool reads the shape of the image, so rounding first keeps the frame aligned.

Match the border radius to the corner radius. A border with a 16 pixel radius around a photo with a 16 pixel radius looks seamless.

For a card look, use a thin border with a gentle radius. The combination feels like a modern app card, which suits social posts and presentations.

For a Polaroid style, keep the corners square. The classic instant film look has sharp corners, and rounding them breaks the retro feel.

Quick Tips for Better Rounded Corners

Crop to the right shape before you round. A square crop for a circle, a wide crop for a pill, and a card crop for a blog image all start with the cropper.

Preview at full size before downloading. Zoom into the corners and confirm the curves are smooth and nothing important is cut away.

Save the rounded file with a clear name, such as avatar-circle.png. You may need the same shape again, and a clear name saves you from re-doing the work.

Keep the original photo without rounding. Once the corners are cut away in an export, they cannot be restored from that file.

Rounding Corners for a Social Post

A social post with rounded corners feels modern and friendly. The soft edges match the cards and buttons of the app around it, so the image blends with the interface.

Use a small to medium radius for feed posts. A gentle rounding of 10 to 20 pixels softens the image without changing its character or hiding any content.

Keep the radius consistent across your posts. A feed where every image shares the same corner shape looks curated, while mixed radii look random.

Export social images as JPG when the corners have a solid fill, or PNG and WebP when they are transparent. The right format keeps the corners looking clean on any background.

Rounding Corners for a Presentation

Photos on slides look better with rounded corners. The soft edges match the modern slide design, and the image feels like a deliberate element rather than a pasted rectangle.

Match the photo radius to the slide theme. A deck with 16 pixel card corners needs photos with a 16 pixel radius, so the images and the cards align.

Keep the radius small for slides that are projected large. A huge radius on a big screen can dominate the image, while a gentle rounding stays subtle at any size.

Export presentation images as JPG at a high quality setting when the corners have a solid fill. The photo needs to stay sharp when projected, and a low quality export will soften the curves.

Frequently asked questions

How do I round the corners of a photo on my phone?

Open the Round Image Corners tool in your phone browser, upload the photo from your gallery, and drag the radius slider with your finger. Use the Full Circle shortcut for a round avatar. Export as PNG or WebP to keep the corners transparent.

What is the best radius for a card image?

A small to medium radius of 10 to 20 pixels suits most cards, giving a gentle softening without changing the shape. Match the photo radius to the card radius for a seamless look. For a blog card, round the top two corners and leave the bottom two square.

Can I round the corners of a JPG image?

Yes, but the corners will be filled with a solid colour, since JPG has no transparency. Choose a fill colour that matches the background where the image will live, or export as PNG or WebP to keep the corners see-through.

How do I replace the image without losing my radius settings?

Load the new image through the replace option in the tool, which keeps your current radius and fill settings. You can then adjust the radius for the new photo and export it with the same setup.

Why do my rounded corners look jagged?

The source image is probably too small or too soft, so the curve renders as visible steps. Start from a higher-resolution original and the edges will look smooth. Exporting as PNG also helps, since it is lossless.

Does rounding corners change the width and height of my photo?

No. Rounding only cuts away the corner areas and leaves the width and height unchanged. The file keeps the same pixel dimensions, with the corners either transparent or filled with a solid colour.
