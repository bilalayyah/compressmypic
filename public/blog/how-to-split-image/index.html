How to Split an Image

Split any image into grid tiles or strips in minutes: upload, pick a mode, set rows and columns, check the cut lines, and download the tiles or a ZIP.

Splitting an image is a short job: upload a photo, choose how to divide it, check the cut lines, and download the pieces. This guide walks through each step in order, from the first upload to the finished tiles.

You need just one thing before you start: the image you want to split. A large, high-resolution source gives the best tiles, because each tile is cut at full original size. A small source gives small tiles, so use the biggest file you have.

The image splitter does the work in your browser. Nothing uploads to a server, there is no account, and the tiles download free. Follow the steps below and you will have a split image in a few minutes.

Step 1: Upload Your Image

Open the splitter and drop your image onto the upload zone, or click to choose a file from your device. You can also paste an image straight from your clipboard with Ctrl and V, which is handy for screenshots that you have not saved as files.

The tool accepts JPG, PNG, WebP, HEIC, GIF, BMP, and SVG files up to 20 MB. Most photos and screenshots are well under that limit. An iPhone HEIC photo uploads and splits like any other file.

As soon as the image loads, an initial 3 by 3 split generates automatically. You will see the grid and the numbered cut lines on the preview right away, which gives you a starting point to adjust from.

If you uploaded the wrong file, replace it without losing your settings. The tool lets you swap the image while keeping the split mode and grid size, so a mistake costs seconds rather than a restart.

Step 2: Choose a Split Mode

Pick one of the three modes: Grid, Horizontal Strips, or Vertical Strips. Grid divides the image into rows and columns, and it is the mode for Instagram grids, puzzles, and posters. Choose it when the tiles will sit side by side to rebuild the picture.

Horizontal Strips cut the image into stacked rows. Choose this mode for long screenshots, tall infographics, and anything that reads top to bottom. Each strip is a wide, short image that prints or posts cleanly on its own.

Vertical Strips cut the image into side-by-side columns. Choose this mode for wide panoramas and banners, where each strip continues the scene to the right. A panorama split into vertical strips works as a swipeable carousel.

If you are not sure which mode fits, start with Grid. It is the most flexible mode, and you can switch to a strip mode at any time without losing the image. The preview updates as you switch, so the difference is visible immediately.

Step 3: Set Rows and Columns

In Grid mode, set the number of rows and columns. Three rows and three columns give the classic nine-tile Instagram grid. Two by two gives a simple four-tile split, and four by four gives sixteen tiles for a larger puzzle.

Think about the tile size before you choose. A 3000 pixel photo split 3 by 3 gives 1000 pixel tiles, which are large enough to post anywhere. The same photo split 10 by 10 gives 300 pixel tiles, which are small and suited only to thumbnails or mosaics.

In strip modes, set the number of strips instead. Four horizontal strips for a long screenshot, three vertical strips for a panorama, and two strips for a simple split. The strips divide the image equally, so the count decides the size of each piece.

Change the numbers freely and watch the preview. The cut lines move as you adjust, and the tile count updates. There is no cost to experimenting, since nothing downloads until you ask for it.

Step 4: Use a Preset

Presets are one-click settings for the common splits. The 3 by 3 Instagram grid preset is the one most people need, and the tool includes others for common grid sizes and strip counts. Tap a preset and the rows and columns fill in automatically.

A preset is a starting point. After tapping it, adjust the numbers if your image or your plan needs a different shape. The preset saves the typing, and you keep full control over the final split.

Use presets when you split images often. A social media manager building a grid every week can tap the same preset each time and get the same tile sizes, which keeps the profile grid consistent from one puzzle to the next.

If no preset matches your job, set the rows and columns by hand. A 4 by 5 grid for a poster or a 1 by 6 strip for a banner are both valid splits, and the manual controls cover every combination up to 10 by 10.

Step 5: Inspect the Cut Lines

Look at the preview and find the numbered cut lines. They show exactly where each tile begins and ends, and the numbers tell you the position of each tile in the grid, reading left to right and top to bottom.

Check whether any cut line passes through a face, a line of text, or an important detail. A face split across two tiles looks like an error, and text cut in half is unreadable in both tiles. If a cut lands badly, change the grid or reposition the image.

For a non-square source, check the tile shapes. A wide photo split 3 by 3 gives wide tiles, not squares, and those tiles may not suit a square grid. Adjust the rows and columns, or crop the source to a square first, so the tiles come out the shape you need.

Zoom into the preview if the image is detailed. Cut lines that look fine at a small size can hide a problem in a busy area of the photo. A few seconds of inspection saves a download and a re-split.

Step 6: Choose the Tile Format

Pick the format for the tiles. JPG suits photos and gives small files, which is what you want for social posts and web use. PNG suits graphics, text, and anything with transparency, because it keeps every pixel exact.

The Tile Quality slider appears for JPG tiles. It controls the compression of the JPG output, so a higher setting keeps more detail and a lower setting makes smaller files. PNG tiles are lossless and have no quality slider.

For transparent PNG sources, choose PNG as the tile format. JPG has no transparency, so a transparent tile exported as JPG gains a solid background. The transparency survives only in a format that supports it.

Match the format to the destination. Social posts and print work well with JPG, while sprite sheets, icons, and design assets need PNG. When in doubt, export PNG for graphics and JPG for photographs.

Step 7: Download the Tiles

Download the tiles as a ZIP to get them all at once. The ZIP contains every tile as a numbered file, in reading order from the top-left corner. Unzip it and the set is ready to use, with the numbers showing where each tile belongs.

You can also download tiles one by one if you only need a few of them. A single tile for a profile picture or a single strip for a banner does not need the whole set, and the individual download skips the unzipping step.

Check the tile dimensions after downloading. Open one tile and confirm it is the size you expected, especially for print work or sprite sheets where exact pixels matter. A quick check now catches a wrong grid setting before you use the tiles.

Keep the ZIP as a backup until the tiles are used. If a tile gets lost or a post fails, the ZIP restores the set without another round of splitting. Delete it only after the job is done.

Step 8: Name and Organize

The downloaded tiles are numbered, but the filenames are generic. Rename them if the job needs meaningful names, such as puzzle-row-1 or poster-tile-a1. Clear names prevent mix-ups when several splits live in one folder.

Keep each split in its own folder. A folder named for the job, with the tiles inside in order, is easier to work with than a downloads folder full of numbered files from different projects. Organization matters most when a grid has nine or more tiles.

For Instagram grids, keep the tiles in posting order, not grid order. The platform shows the newest post first, so the tiles must be posted from the last grid position to the first. A folder sorted by posting order removes the guesswork.

Note the split settings somewhere if you will repeat the job. A grid of 3 by 3 from a square source is easy to remember, but a custom split of 4 by 5 with JPG quality 85 is not. A short note saves you from rediscovering the settings next time.

Step 9: Check the Tiles

Open a few tiles at full size and look at the edges. Each tile should show a clean cut with no sliver of the neighboring tile and no missing strip along the boundary. A clean split is invisible when the tiles sit together.

Check the first and last tiles especially. The edge tiles reveal problems with uneven division, where the image dimensions did not split evenly and the edge tiles came out a pixel or two different in size. A small difference is normal and rarely visible.

View the tiles together if the job is a grid or a puzzle. Lay them out in order on screen, or assemble them in a collage, and confirm the picture rebuilds correctly. A misplaced tile is obvious when the set is viewed as a whole.

If a tile looks wrong, do not fix the tile by hand. Go back to the splitter, adjust the grid or the source image, and split again. A fresh split keeps every tile consistent, while a hand-edited tile can drift from the rest.

Step 10: Reuse the Setup

Save the settings that worked. The grid size, the mode, and the tile format are the three numbers that define a split, and reusing them keeps a series of splits consistent. A weekly grid puzzle built with the same settings looks like a series, not a set of accidents.

Split a new image with the same settings by uploading it and applying the same numbers. The tool remembers nothing between visits, so the note you made in step 8 is the record. Keep it with the project files.

When the source changes shape, the settings may need adjusting. A square source and a wide source split 3 by 3 give different tile shapes, so check the preview each time even when the numbers are the same.

Build a small library of split presets in your notes: the Instagram 3 by 3, the poster 4 by 5, the screenshot 4-strip, and any other split you use often. A written list turns a five-minute job into a one-minute job.

Fixing Uneven Splits

An uneven split happens when the image dimensions do not divide evenly by the rows or columns. A 1000 pixel image split into 3 columns gives tiles of 333, 333, and 334 pixels, because the extra pixels have to go somewhere. The edge tiles end up a pixel larger.

A one-pixel difference is invisible in almost every use. Social posts, carousels, and print layouts tolerate it without any visible seam. Do not chase a perfect division when a pixel-level difference has no visible effect.

For pixel-perfect work such as sprite sheets, trim the source first. Crop the image to dimensions that divide evenly by the grid, then split. A 960 pixel sheet split 3 by 3 gives exact 320 pixel tiles with no remainder at all.

Check the tile dimensions in the download if exactness matters. Open the tiles and confirm the sizes before using them in a game engine or a print layout, where a one-pixel difference can shift alignment.

Splitting for a Specific Platform

Each platform has its own tile expectations. Instagram grids need square tiles, so the source should be square and the grid should be 3 by 3. A non-square source gives non-square tiles that break the profile grid.

Print needs tiles that match the paper size. A poster split for A4 pages needs tiles sized to A4 proportions, so set the grid from the paper dimensions rather than from a round number. The tile count follows the poster size divided by the page size.

Carousels need tiles that read on their own. A panorama split into vertical strips gives strips that each show a slice of the scene, and each slice should be interesting enough to hold a swipe. A strip that is all sky or all empty ground wastes a swipe.

When the platform changes its layout, the split may need to change too. Instagram has changed its profile grid shape before, and a grid built for the old shape can look wrong on the new one. Check the current grid before building a puzzle.

Quick Reference: Split Checklist

Upload a large source, pick Grid or a strip mode, set the rows and columns or tap a preset, and inspect the numbered cut lines for faces and text. Choose JPG for photos and PNG for transparency.

Download the ZIP, keep the tiles in order, and post grid tiles in reverse order. If a cut lands badly or the tiles come out uneven, adjust the grid or trim the source and split again.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know how many rows and columns to choose?

Match the count to the job: 3 by 3 for an Instagram grid, 2 by 2 for a simple split, and as many strips as you have pages or slides for a long screenshot. Fewer tiles mean larger tiles, so use the smallest count that does the job.

What happens if my image is not evenly divisible?

The edge tiles come out a pixel or two larger than the rest, because the leftover pixels have to go somewhere. A one-pixel difference is invisible in normal use. For pixel-perfect work, crop the source to dimensions that divide evenly first.

Can I split a screenshot into printable pages?

Yes. Use Horizontal Strips and set the strip count to the number of pages you want. Each strip prints on its own page, and the strips together contain the whole screenshot. Choose the count so each strip matches the paper size.

How do I put the tiles back together?

Lay them out in numbered order, left to right and top to bottom, starting from tile one at the top-left corner. For an Instagram grid, post them in reverse order, from the last tile to the first, so they land in the right positions.

Which format should I choose for the tiles?

Choose JPG for photographs and for any use where small files matter, such as social posts. Choose PNG for graphics, text, and transparent images, because PNG keeps every pixel exact and preserves transparency.

Can I split an image on my phone?

Yes, the splitter runs in the browser on phones and tablets as well as on desktop. Upload from your photo library, set the grid, and download the ZIP. A large source image works best, so use the original photo rather than a compressed copy.
