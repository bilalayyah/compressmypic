Photo Collage Maker: Complete Guide

Build photo collages in minutes: grids and strips, drag-to-swap, adjustable splits, gaps, rounded corners, background colours, and JPG or PNG export.

A photo collage turns several pictures into one image. It shows a set of moments together: a trip, a product range, a before and after, or a family gathering. One collage says more than a row of separate photos, and it fits anywhere a single image fits.

The image collage maker arranges your photos in 2 by 2 and 3 by 3 grids or in vertical and horizontal strips, with drag-to-swap, adjustable splits, gaps, rounded corners, and export up to 4000 pixels. It runs entirely in your browser.

This guide explains the layouts, the arrangement controls, the framing options, and the export choices, and shows which layout suits which job. Whether you make a social post, a print, or a gift, the collage is the same simple idea.

What Is a Collage Maker?

A collage maker arranges several photos into a single image on a shared canvas. Each photo sits in a cell, and the cells together form a grid or a strip. The result is one image file that contains all of the photos at once.

The tool is different from a photo editor. It does not change the photos themselves; it arranges them. You choose the layout, place the photos, adjust the spacing, and export the combined image as a JPG or PNG.

Collages are used for social posts, where one image carries several moments; for product listings, where one image shows a range; for before-and-after comparisons, where two images sit side by side; and for prints and gifts, where several memories share one frame.

The collage maker works in the browser, so your photos never leave your device. That matters for family photos and customer before-and-after shots, which are private by nature. There is no upload, no account, and no cost.

Layouts: Grids and Strips

The tool offers two grid layouts and two strip layouts. The 2 by 2 grid holds four photos in a square, and the 3 by 3 grid holds nine. The vertical strip stacks photos in a tall column, and the horizontal strip lines them up in a wide row.

The layout is chosen automatically from the number of photos you upload. Dropping five or more photos picks the 3 by 3 grid, while four or fewer picks the 2 by 2 grid. You can change the layout at any time after uploading.

Grids suit sets of photos that belong together: a trip, an event, or a product range. The square shape fits feeds, listings, and thumbnails, and the equal cells give every photo the same weight.

Strips suit sequences and comparisons. A vertical strip reads top to bottom like a story, which suits a day in order or a step-by-step process. A horizontal strip reads left to right like a timeline, which suits a before-and-after or a panorama of moments.

Uploading Photos

Drop your photos onto the upload zone, or click to choose them from your device. The tool accepts JPG, PNG, WebP, HEIC, GIF, BMP, and SVG files up to 20 MB each, so phone photos, screenshots, and graphics all work without conversion.

Uploading a mix of formats works fine. An iPhone HEIC photo, a PNG screenshot, and a JPG from a camera can sit in the same collage, because the tool converts each one for display and export. The formats only matter for the final export choice.

The number of photos decides the starting layout. Five or more photos auto-pick the 3 by 3 grid, and four or fewer auto-pick the 2 by 2 grid. If you upload fewer photos than the grid has cells, the empty cells wait for more photos or stay blank until you add them.

Choose photos with similar quality and lighting where you can. A collage of nine photos where one is dark and blurry draws the eye to the weak photo, while a set with consistent light looks like a deliberate collection.

Drag Swap and Tap Swap

Arranging photos is a drag-and-drop job on desktop: pick up a photo and drop it onto another cell, and the two photos swap places. The swap is instant, and you can rearrange as many times as you like before exporting.

On a phone or tablet, tap-to-swap does the same job. Tap a photo to select it, then tap the cell you want it to move to, and the two photos swap. Touch dragging is unreliable on small screens, so the tap method is the reliable one for mobile.

Arrangement matters because the eye reads a collage in order. The top-left cell is seen first, the center cell draws the eye in a 3 by 3 grid, and the bottom-right cell is seen last. Put the strongest photo in the most prominent cell for the layout you chose.

Try several arrangements before you commit. A photo that looks lost in one cell can anchor the collage in another, and a swap that takes a second can change the whole balance. The preview updates instantly, so experimenting costs nothing.

Adjusting Splits

The splits are the dividers between cells, and they can be adjusted. Drag a split line to make one cell larger and its neighbor smaller, which lets a feature photo take more space than the rest. The layout stays a grid, but the proportions change.

A feature photo is the reason to adjust a split. A hero shot of the main subject deserves a larger cell, while the supporting photos can be smaller. A 3 by 3 grid with an enlarged center cell reads as a showcase rather than a set of equals.

If the splits become uneven and you want to start over, return them to a uniform grid. The tool resets the splits to equal cells, so you can try a different arrangement without rebuilding the collage from scratch.

Keep the splits sensible. A cell squeezed to a thin strip crops its photo heavily, and a cell enlarged too far unbalances the grid. Small adjustments improve a collage; extreme ones break it.

Gaps and Rounded Corners

Gap is the space between cells, and it sets the feel of the collage. A zero gap makes a seamless borderless collage, where the photos touch and form one continuous image. A visible gap separates the photos into distinct frames, which suits a scrapbook or a gallery look.

Cell spacing and corner radius are independent controls, so you can set a wide gap with square corners, or a narrow gap with rounded corners. The combination decides whether the collage feels modern, classic, or playful.

Rounded corners soften the collage and suit social posts and app-style graphics. Square corners feel formal and suit prints and product listings. A small radius is the safe default, while a large radius turns each cell into a rounded tile.

The background color shows through the gaps, so the gap and the background work together. A white gap on a white background looks like a clean border, while a brand color in the gaps ties the collage to a campaign. Choose them as a pair.

Image Fit: Cover vs Contain

Image Fit decides what happens when a photo does not match its cell shape. Cover fills the cell completely by cropping the photo, so there are no empty areas but the edges may be cut off. It is the default, because a full cell looks finished.

Contain fits the whole photo inside the cell without cropping, so nothing is lost but empty space appears around the photo. It suits photos where every detail matters, such as a document or a product shot with text at the edges.

Switch to Contain when Cover crops something important. A face at the edge of a photo, a product label, or a line of text can be cut by Cover, and Contain saves it at the cost of a border. Switch back to Cover when the border looks worse than the crop.

Check each cell after choosing the fit. A collage mixes portrait and landscape photos, and the fit that suits one cell may not suit another. The setting applies per cell, so adjust the ones that need it and leave the rest.

Background Colors

The background color fills the canvas behind the photos and shows through the gaps. The tool offers quick preset colors, so you can pick a clean white, a soft neutral, or a brand color without opening a color picker.

White is the default and the safest choice. It keeps the collage clean, it matches any feed or page, and it makes the photos the only color in the image. A white background never fights the photos.

A brand color turns the collage into a designed graphic. The color in the gaps ties the collage to a campaign, and a set of collages in the same color forms a recognizable series. Use it when the collage is an advertisement as much as a collection.

Transparent PNG stickers placed in cells show the background color behind them. A sticker on a white background reads as a clean cutout, while a sticker on a colored background gains a tint from the color behind it. Check stickers against the chosen background before exporting.

Canvas Sizes and Ratios

The canvas size and ratio decide the final shape of the collage. The tool offers quick canvas dimensions and preset ratios, so you can build a square for the feed, a portrait for a story, or a wide image for a banner without calculating pixels.

Square is the most versatile shape. It works in feeds, listings, and thumbnails, and it crops predictably everywhere. A square collage with a 2 by 2 or 3 by 3 grid is the safe default when you are not sure where the image will be used.

Portrait ratios suit stories and phone wallpapers, where the image is viewed tall. A vertical strip on a portrait canvas reads like a story told top to bottom, which suits a day in order or a step-by-step process.

Wide ratios suit banners and headers, where the collage sits across the top of a page. A horizontal strip on a wide canvas reads left to right like a timeline, which suits a product range or a team photo.

Feature Photos

A feature photo is one image made larger than the rest, and it is the simplest way to give a collage a focal point. The eye lands on the large photo first, then explores the smaller ones, which gives the collage a hierarchy instead of a flat grid.

Make the strongest photo the feature. The hero shot of a trip, the best product angle, or the main portrait deserves the large cell. A weak photo enlarged only draws attention to its flaws, so choose the feature by quality as well as by subject.

Adjust the splits to enlarge the feature cell, or choose a layout that already favors one cell. A 2 by 2 grid with one enlarged cell reads as a showcase, while a 3 by 3 grid with an enlarged center reads as a gallery with a centerpiece.

Keep the feature to one photo. Two large cells compete with each other and the hierarchy collapses. One feature, clearly larger than the rest, is the rule that makes the collage read at a glance.

Export JPG vs PNG

The tool exports the finished collage as a JPG or a PNG. JPG suits photographs and any collage where a small file matters, such as a social post or a website image. It compresses well and loads fast.

PNG suits collages with sharp text, flat colors, or transparency that must survive. A PNG file is larger, but it keeps every edge crisp and preserves transparent areas exactly. Choose PNG for graphics-heavy collages and JPG for photo-heavy ones.

The export resolution goes up to 4000 pixels, which is large enough for printing. A collage exported at 4000 pixels prints cleanly at a large size, while a small export prints soft. Choose the export size for the destination: social posts need less, prints need more.

The exported filename reflects the collage settings, so you can tell versions apart when you export several. A clear filename saves you from opening five near-identical files to find the right one.

Common Mistakes

The most common mistake is too many photos. A 3 by 3 grid with nine busy photos is hard to read, because no single photo gets enough space. Fewer, stronger photos make a better collage than a grid stuffed with everything from the trip.

Another mistake is ignoring the fit. A portrait photo in a landscape cell under Cover loses its top and bottom, which can cut off a face or a subject. Check each cell after placing the photos, and switch the fit where the crop hurts.

A third mistake is a gap color that fights the photos. A bright gap color between muted photos draws the eye to the gaps instead of the images, and a dark gap color can make the collage feel heavy. White or a soft neutral is the safe gap color.

Finally, many people export at a small size and then try to print the collage. A collage exported for social media prints poorly at a large size, because the pixels are not there. Export at the largest size you might need, and downscale later if you must.

Quick Reference: Collage Checklist

Choose four to nine strong photos with similar light, upload them, and let the layout auto-pick the grid. Arrange the photos by dragging or tapping to swap, enlarge the feature photo, and set the gaps and corners.

Pick a canvas ratio for the destination, choose JPG for photos and PNG for graphics, and export at up to 4000 pixels for print. Check each cell for bad crops before you download.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a collage and a grid split?

A grid split divides one photo into tiles that rebuild the original when placed together. A collage combines several different photos into one image. Splitting cuts one picture apart; a collage brings many pictures together.

How many photos can I put in one collage?

The grids hold four photos in a 2 by 2 layout or nine in a 3 by 3 layout, and the strips can hold many more in a single row or column. Four to nine photos is the practical range; more than that makes each photo too small to enjoy.

Can I mix portrait and landscape photos in one collage?

Yes. The Image Fit setting handles the mix: Cover fills each cell by cropping, while Contain shows the whole photo with a border. Check each cell after placing the photos, since the fit that suits one orientation may not suit the other.

What export size should I choose for a collage?

Match the size to the destination. Social posts need only a moderate size, while prints need the largest export, up to 4000 pixels. Export at the largest size you might need, since you can always downscale later but cannot add pixels back.

Do collages work for printing?

Yes, as long as you export at a high resolution. A collage exported at up to 4000 pixels prints cleanly at a large size. Export as PNG for the sharpest print, or as a high-quality JPG for a photographic print.

Can I edit a collage after exporting it?

Not easily, because the export is a single flat image. Keep the collage open in the tool while you work, or save the source photos so you can rebuild it. Rebuilding from the originals is faster and cleaner than editing the exported file.
