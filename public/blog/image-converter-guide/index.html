Image Converter: Complete Guide

Convert images between JPG, PNG, WebP, HEIC, SVG, BMP, GIF and AVIF in your browser. Formats, quality, transparency and batch tips explained.

Every image on your devices lives in a format, and formats do not always get along. Phones save HEIC, cameras save JPG, designers work in PNG, and websites serve WebP. When the wrong format meets the wrong app, the file simply refuses to open.

An image converter solves that mismatch. It reads a file in one format and writes it out in another, keeping the picture itself intact. The pixels stay the same, but the container changes, so the file works wherever you need it.

This guide explains how image conversion works, which formats to choose, and how to get clean results every time. It covers quality settings, transparency, batch conversion, and the mistakes that waste the most time.

What Is an Image Converter?

An image converter is a tool that changes the file format of a picture without changing what the picture shows. You give it a JPG, and it hands back a PNG, a WebP, or any other format it supports.

The conversion rewrites how the pixels are stored. A JPG stores colours with lossy compression, while a PNG stores them without loss. The image looks the same, but the file behaves differently in apps and on websites.

People convert images for practical reasons, not for fun. A form may only accept JPG, a website may demand WebP, or a print shop may insist on PNG. The converter bridges the gap between what you have and what is asked for.

The CompressMyPic converter runs entirely in your browser. Nothing is uploaded to a server, so your photos stay on your own device while they change format.

Which Formats Does the Converter Handle?

The converter accepts JPG, PNG, WebP, HEIC, SVG, BMP, GIF, and AVIF as input. That covers phone photos, camera shots, screenshots, logos, and downloads from modern websites.

For output, you can choose JPG, PNG, or WebP. These three cover almost every real-world need: JPG for photos, PNG for graphics and transparency, and WebP for the web.

HEIC deserves a special mention because iPhones save photos in that format by default. Many Windows programs and upload forms still reject HEIC files, so converting them to JPG is one of the most common jobs here.

AVIF is the newest format on the list. It appears on modern websites and in some Android galleries, yet older software cannot open it. Converting AVIF to JPG or PNG makes those files usable everywhere.

How Does Browser Conversion Work?

When you drop a file into the converter, your browser decodes it into pixels using built-in image support. For HEIC files, a small WebAssembly decoder runs inside the tab, since browsers cannot decode HEIC natively.

Those pixels are drawn onto an HTML5 Canvas, which is the browser's drawing surface. The canvas then encodes the pixels again, this time in the format you selected, at the quality you chose.

Because everything happens locally, conversion speed depends on your device, not on your internet connection. A large photo converts in a second or two on a modern phone or laptop.

The local approach also protects privacy. Screenshots of bank statements, ID photos, and family pictures never leave your device, which matters more than most people realise.

Choosing the Right Output Format

JPG is the safest choice for photographs. It handles smooth gradients, skin tones, and busy scenes well, and every device and website on earth can open it. Use JPG when compatibility matters most.

PNG is the right choice when you need transparency or perfectly sharp edges. Logos, icons, screenshots with text, and graphics with flat colours all look best as PNG files.

WebP is the best choice for the web. It produces smaller files than JPG at similar quality, and it supports transparency like PNG. Most modern browsers and website builders accept it now.

When you are unsure, ask what the file is for. A photo for a form wants JPG, a logo for a website wants PNG or WebP, and a picture for a blog post usually wants WebP.

Quality Settings Explained

The quality slider controls how much detail the encoder keeps when it writes a JPG or WebP file. Higher numbers keep more detail and produce larger files, while lower numbers shrink the file and soften the image.

A setting around 80 to 85 percent works well for most photos. At that level, the file is much smaller than the original, yet the difference is hard to spot without zooming in closely.

The slider is disabled when PNG is selected, and that is not a bug. PNG is a lossless format, so there is no quality level to choose. Every pixel is stored exactly, and the file size is whatever it needs to be.

For WebP photos on a website, 75 to 85 percent is a good range. For a photo you plan to print, push the quality to 90 percent or higher, since print shows flaws that screens hide.

Transparency and the Matte Fill Colour

JPG cannot store transparency, so something must fill the see-through parts when a PNG becomes a JPG. That something is called the matte fill colour, and you can choose it before converting.

White is the usual choice, since most documents and web pages sit on white backgrounds. A logo converted onto white looks natural on a white page or in a Word document.

Black works better for dark designs, and a brand colour can match a specific website background. The converter lets you pick any hex colour, so you can match a page exactly.

When converting to PNG or WebP, transparency is preserved instead. The see-through areas stay see-through, which is why those formats suit logos and stickers.

Converting Batches and ZIP Downloads

You do not need to convert one file at a time. Drop a whole folder of mixed formats into the queue, pick one output format, and convert them all together.

Each file converts independently, so one bad file cannot stop the rest. A corrupted image shows an error badge, while the valid files still finish and download normally.

When two or more files finish, the Download All button bundles them into a single ZIP archive. That is far easier than downloading twenty files one by one.

Output filenames keep your original name and swap only the extension. A file called beach-day.heic becomes beach-day.jpg, so you always know which file is which.

HEIC Photos from iPhones

iPhones save photos as HEIC because the format stores the same quality in roughly half the space of JPG. The trade-off is compatibility, since many programs outside the Apple world cannot open HEIC at all.

The converter decodes HEIC files with a WebAssembly decoder that runs inside your browser tab. It reads the HEIC container locally, renders the photo, and exports a standard JPG, PNG, or WebP.

Converted JPGs often look larger in megabytes than the original HEIC files. That is normal, because HEIC is a more efficient format. The JPG is not worse, it is simply stored less efficiently.

If your iPhone keeps producing HEIC files and you would rather have JPG, you can change the camera setting on the phone itself. Until then, converting each batch takes only a few seconds.

SVG and Vector Files

SVG files are different from the other formats because they describe shapes with code, not pixels. A logo saved as SVG can scale to any size without ever going blurry.

Converting an SVG to PNG or WebP turns those shapes into pixels at a fixed size. The result is a normal raster image that any app can open, which is useful when a website or form does not accept SVG.

Linked images inside an SVG will not survive the trip, since the browser cannot fetch them during conversion. Embed images as data URIs inside the SVG if they must appear in the PNG.

Web fonts inside an SVG may also fall back to a default font during conversion. For a logo, convert text to outlines in your design tool before converting, so the lettering stays exact.

Checking Quality Before Saving

Every converted card has a Compare button that opens a side-by-side view of the original and the converted file. Drag the split handle across the image to inspect edges, text, and fine detail.

The comparison also shows the before-and-after file sizes. That number tells you whether the conversion actually saved space, which is the whole point for web images.

Zoom into the areas that matter most. Faces, small text, and sharp diagonal lines show compression damage first, while smooth skies and blurred backgrounds hide it well.

If the result looks soft, raise the quality slider and convert again. The original file is still in the queue, so you never need to re-upload it to try a different setting.

Privacy: What Happens to Your Files?

Conversion happens on your device, so your files are never uploaded to a server. That is the single biggest privacy advantage of a browser converter over a cloud service.

Drawing an image onto Canvas also strips hidden metadata. EXIF camera data, GPS location tags, and camera serial numbers do not survive the conversion, which protects you when sharing files online.

There is no account, no upload queue on a server, and no copy of your photos left behind. Closing the tab ends the session completely.

For sensitive files such as ID scans and medical documents, this local approach is the safest option. Never upload identity documents to a converter you do not trust.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

The most common mistake is converting a lossy JPG into PNG and expecting the quality to improve. It will not. PNG stores the damaged pixels perfectly, so the file gets bigger while the image stays exactly as flawed as before.

Another mistake is ignoring the matte fill colour. A transparent logo converted to JPG with a black fill looks broken on a white page, and the fix is a ten-second colour change before converting.

People also forget that animated GIFs become still images here. The canvas draws only the first frame, so the motion is lost. Use the GIF Compressor if you need to keep the animation.

Finally, do not convert the same file back and forth between lossy formats. Each round of JPG encoding adds fresh damage, so always convert from the best original you have.

Converting for Different Destinations

Every destination has its own favourite format, and the converter should match the destination rather than your habit. A website wants WebP, a form wants JPG, and a design tool wants PNG, so the same source image may need three different conversions.

For websites, WebP is the smart target. It keeps pages fast and supports transparency, which suits logos and photos alike. Convert the image to WebP at 80 percent quality and upload that version to your site.

For email, JPG is the safe target. Email clients display JPG reliably, while WebP support is inconsistent and PNG files are often too large for attachment limits. A JPG at 85 percent quality is the practical choice.

For print, JPG at 90 percent or PNG is the right target, depending on the content. Photos print well as high-quality JPG, while documents with text print better as PNG, since the letters stay sharp on paper.

For social media, check the platform first. Most platforms convert uploads to their own formats anyway, so a high-quality JPG is usually the simplest input. A PNG works too, though the larger file uploads more slowly.

File Naming and Organisation After Conversion

Converted files deserve clear names, since a folder of IMG_4821.jpg files is useless a month later. Keep the original name, change the extension, and add a short tag describing the purpose, such as hero-web.webp or passport-photo.jpg.

Store converted files separately from originals. A folder named converted or web-ready keeps the two sets apart, so you never overwrite a master copy by accident or send the wrong version.

When a batch produces several formats, use subfolders per format. A folder for WebP files and another for JPG files is easier to navigate than one folder holding both, especially when the names are similar.

Delete failed or duplicate conversions promptly. A folder full of half-finished attempts slows you down every time you search it, while a tidy folder makes the next job faster.

Back up the originals before any large conversion job. A converted file can never become a perfect original again, so the masters deserve a safe copy on a separate drive or in cloud storage.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between converting and compressing an image?

Converting changes the file format, such as JPG to PNG, while compressing reduces the file size, usually within the same format. A conversion can also shrink a file, for example JPG to WebP, but its main job is compatibility. Compression keeps the format and targets a smaller size.

Will converting an image change its pixel dimensions?

No, conversion keeps the same width and height by default. A 4000 by 3000 photo stays 4000 by 3000 after conversion, only the format and file size change. If you also need smaller dimensions, use the Image Resizer tool after converting.

Can I convert an image without losing any quality?

Yes, when the target format is lossless. Converting JPG to PNG loses nothing beyond the damage already in the JPG. In this tool, PNG is the lossless option: WebP uses the quality slider, so a PNG to WebP conversion is only as faithful as the quality setting allows. Converting to JPG always involves some loss, so keep the quality slider high for important photos.

Why does my converted file have a different name?

The converter keeps your original filename and only swaps the extension, so beach-day.heic becomes beach-day.jpg. This keeps your files organised and makes it obvious which file is the converted copy. If two files would collide, the browser download adds a number.

Do I need to install anything to convert images in the browser?

No. The converter runs entirely in a modern browser on Windows, Mac, Linux, ChromeOS, Android, and iOS. There is no app to install and no account to create. Just open the page, drop in a file, and download the result.

What should I do if my converted image looks worse than the original?

Raise the quality slider and convert again from the original file, since the original is still in the queue. Check whether you converted to the wrong format, for example a detailed photo to low-quality JPG. If the source itself was low quality, no setting can restore detail that was never there.
