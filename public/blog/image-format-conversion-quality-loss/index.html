Image Format Conversion: Quality Loss

Does converting an image lose quality? Lossy vs lossless formats, repeated conversions, and the settings that keep your photos sharp.

Every format conversion changes how your image is stored, and some of those changes are permanent. Understanding which conversions lose quality, and which do not, saves you from slowly damaging your photos.

The rule is simple to state and easy to forget: lossy formats throw away detail, lossless formats keep everything. Every conversion decision follows from that one fact.

This article explains quality loss in plain terms. You will learn what happens inside each format, why repeated conversions hurt, and when converting is completely safe. What Quality Loss Actually Means

Quality loss means detail that existed in the original file is gone from the converted file, and no tool can bring it back. The pixels are still there, but the fine information inside them has been simplified.

The damage is not always visible. A photo converted at 90 percent quality looks identical to the original at normal size, yet a pixel-level comparison shows tiny differences in textured areas.

Loss becomes visible when it accumulates. One conversion at high quality is harmless, but five conversions at medium quality turn a sharp photo into a soft, blotchy one.

Think of it like photocopying a photocopy. Each generation looks slightly worse than the last, and the damage is worst in the fine details.

Lossy vs Lossless: The Core Difference

Lossy formats, such as JPG, WebP in lossy mode, and AVIF, discard data to shrink the file. They keep the parts of the image your eyes notice most and simplify the rest.

Lossless formats, such as PNG, store every pixel exactly. The file is bigger, but the image is a perfect copy of the source, with zero degradation. (The WebP format also has a lossless mode, but this site's Image Converter outputs WebP through its quality slider, so its WebP files are lossy — pick PNG here for a truly lossless result.)

HEIC sits in the lossy camp too, though it is far more efficient than JPG. It discards data as well, just more cleverly, which is why iPhone photos look good at half the size.

When you convert, the loss depends on the target format, not the source. Converting a JPG to PNG loses nothing new, while converting a PNG to JPG always loses something.

JPG Quality, Explained Honestly

JPG compression works by grouping nearby pixels into blocks and simplifying the colour inside each block. Smooth areas compress well, while busy textures and sharp edges suffer most.

At 90 percent quality, the blocks are nearly invisible. At 70 percent, you can spot them in skies and skin if you zoom in. At 50 percent, the blocky artefacts are obvious even at normal size.

The damage concentrates where the image is complex. A flat blue sky survives 60 percent quality easily, while a detailed forest scene falls apart at the same setting.

For photos you care about, stay at 80 percent or higher. Below that, you are trading visible quality for file size, and the trade is rarely worth it.

PNG: Lossless, But Not Magic

PNG stores every pixel exactly, so converting to PNG never adds damage. A PNG made from a JPG is a perfect copy of that JPG, flaws included.

That perfection has a cost. PNG files are much larger than JPG files for photos, because lossless storage cannot throw away the noise and texture that JPG discards.

PNG shines for graphics, not photos. Sharp text, flat colours, and thin lines stay crisp in PNG, while JPG would smear them with compression artefacts.

Use PNG when the image must survive many rounds of editing. Each edit and re-save of a JPG adds damage, while a PNG can be edited and saved forever without loss.

WebP: The Best of Both Worlds?

WebP supports both lossy and lossless encoding in one format. Lossy WebP usually beats JPG by 25 to 35 percent in file size at the same visual quality, which is why websites love it.

Lossless WebP beats PNG too, often by a similar margin, while keeping transparency. For web graphics, lossless WebP is frequently the smallest perfect copy available.

The catch is compatibility. Older software, some printers, and a few upload forms still reject WebP, so it is not yet a universal format like JPG.

When compatibility does not matter, WebP is the smartest conversion target. When it does matter, JPG remains the safe choice.

HEIC Efficiency and Its Limits

HEIC stores photos at roughly half the size of JPG with similar quality. That efficiency is why iPhones adopted it, and why your camera roll holds twice as many photos as it used to.

The format achieves this with modern compression that understands image structure better than the ageing JPG standard. It also stores depth data and live-photo video in the same file.

Converting HEIC to JPG always makes the file bigger. That is not a failure of the converter, it is the price of moving from a modern format to an older one.

Keep the HEIC originals if you can. They are the best-quality copies you own, and you can always convert them again later.

The Lossy-to-Lossless Myth

Converting a JPG to PNG does not restore any detail. The JPG damage is baked into the pixels, and PNG simply stores those damaged pixels perfectly.

The file gets much bigger, because PNG must describe every compression artefact and blocky edge exactly. You pay a size penalty for zero quality gain.

This conversion is still useful in one situation: editing. If you plan to annotate, crop, and re-save an image many times, PNG stops the damage from accumulating further.

Do not convert JPG to PNG hoping for a sharper photo. The sharpness was lost when the JPG was created, and no format change can bring it back.

Why Repeated Conversions Hurt

Each lossy conversion re-compresses an already compressed image. The encoder treats the previous artefacts as real detail and tries to preserve them, while adding fresh damage of its own.

After three or four rounds, the image looks visibly worse. Edges develop halos, skies develop bands, and fine textures turn to mush.

The damage is worst when the quality drops each time. A file converted at 90, then 80, then 70 percent ends up far worse than a file converted once at 70 percent.

The fix is simple: always convert from the original. Keep your best copy untouched, and generate every delivery format from that one source.

Quality Settings That Actually Work

For web photos, 80 to 85 percent JPG or WebP quality is the practical sweet spot. The files are small, the images look clean, and the savings are large.

For photos you will print, use 90 to 95 percent. Printers reveal flaws that screens hide, and the extra file size is cheap compared to a ruined print.

For screenshots with text, use WebP at 85 percent or PNG. JPG smears small letters, while WebP keeps them readable at a fraction of the PNG size.

For archival copies, use PNG or lossless WebP. The file is big, but it is the only way to guarantee zero loss across years of storage.

How to Spot Visible Damage

Zoom to 100 percent and look at diagonal edges. Stair-step patterns and halos along high-contrast edges are the first signs of aggressive compression.

Check smooth gradients next. Skies, walls, and skin should blend smoothly, not in visible bands. Banding means the quality setting was too low.

Look at small text in screenshots. Blurry or bleeding letters mean the format or the quality setting was wrong for that image.

Use the Compare viewer on the converter to check before downloading. A ten-second inspection beats discovering the damage after you have already sent the file.

When Conversion Is Completely Safe

Converting from a lossless source to a lossless target is always safe. PNG to lossless WebP, or PNG to PNG, changes nothing about the image itself.

Converting a lossy source to a lossless target is also safe in the sense that nothing new is lost. The file gets bigger, but the image does not get worse.

A single high-quality lossy conversion is safe for practical purposes. At 90 percent, the loss is measurable by software but invisible to human eyes.

Converting between lossless formats for compatibility is the safest move of all. The pixels never change, only the container does.

When to Keep the Original Instead

Keep the original when the photo matters and you may need it again. A wedding photo, a portfolio piece, or a product shot deserves an untouched master copy.

Keep the original when you plan to edit. Cropping, colour correction, and resizing all work better from a high-quality source than from a compressed copy.

Keep the original when the format is already correct. If a file is already a JPG and the destination wants a JPG, converting it again only adds damage for no benefit.

Store originals in a separate folder from converted copies. Clear naming, such as originals and web-ready, prevents the two from getting mixed up later.

The Generation Problem in Plain Terms

Every lossy conversion creates a new generation of the image, and each generation is slightly worse than the last. The first generation is nearly perfect, the fifth is visibly damaged, and the tenth may be unusable.

The damage happens because the encoder treats the previous artefacts as real detail. It tries to preserve the blocky patches and halos from the last conversion, while adding fresh ones of its own.

Photocopying a photocopy is the classic analogy. Each copy loses a little sharpness, and after several copies the text is blurry and the images are muddy, even though the first copy looked fine.

The solution is to keep the number of generations at one. Convert from the original master, once, at high quality, and never convert a converted file again unless you have no choice.

If you must convert a converted file, use the highest quality setting available. A high-quality second generation does far less damage than a medium-quality one, and it preserves more of what remains.

Lossy Formats Compared by Efficiency

Not all lossy formats discard data equally. JPG is the oldest and least efficient, while AVIF is the newest and most efficient, with WebP and HEIC sitting between them.

At the same file size, AVIF looks the best, followed by HEIC, then WebP, then JPG. The ranking reflects the age of each format and the sophistication of its compression.

At the same visual quality, the file sizes reverse. An AVIF file is the smallest, a JPG file is the largest, and the others fall in between, which is why websites prefer the newer formats.

Efficiency is not the only factor, though. A format that no one can open is useless, no matter how efficient it is, which is why JPG remains the safe choice for sharing.

For archiving, efficiency matters less than longevity. A JPG master will open in fifty years, while a newer format may not, so the oldest format is sometimes the safest archive.

Why Screenshots Suffer More Than Photos

Screenshots suffer more from lossy conversion than photos do, because they contain sharp edges and flat colours that lossy compression handles badly. Text, icons, and interface elements all have high-contrast boundaries.

A photo hides compression well, since its gradients and textures are naturally soft. The encoder can simplify a sky or a skin tone without anyone noticing the difference.

A screenshot cannot hide anything. Every letter edge, every button border, and every thin line is a sharp transition, and the encoder smears each one slightly.

For screenshots, use lossless formats or high-quality lossy settings. PNG is perfect for screenshots, while WebP at 85 percent is a good compromise when file size matters.

When you must convert a screenshot to JPG, keep the quality at 88 percent or higher. Below that, the text damage becomes visible, and the screenshot looks unprofessional.

Archiving Masters for the Future

A master copy is the best version of an image you own, and it deserves lossless storage. A PNG or a lossless WebP master preserves every pixel exactly, forever, without any degradation.

Store masters separately from delivery copies. A folder named masters holds the originals, while a folder named web-ready holds the converted versions, and the two never mix.

Back up the masters in two places. A local drive and a cloud backup protect against hardware failure, theft, and accidental deletion, which are the real risks to an archive.

Do not convert the masters themselves. Generate every delivery format from the master, so each conversion is a first generation with minimal damage.

Review the archive every year or two. Formats change, and a master stored in an obsolete format may need migrating to a current one before the old format becomes unreadable.

The short version: convert freely, but convert from the original, once, at high quality. Frequently asked questions

Does converting JPG to WebP lose quality?

Very little, at the right settings. WebP at 80 to 85 percent quality usually looks identical to the original JPG while producing a smaller file. The loss is only visible if you zoom in closely or set the quality very low. For web use, the trade is almost always worth it.

Is it better to convert to PNG or WebP for a website?

WebP is usually better for websites because it produces smaller files at the same visual quality, which makes pages load faster. PNG wins only when you need maximum compatibility with old software or when the image has very sharp text that WebP might soften. For most sites, WebP is the right call.

Can I convert a low-quality JPG into a high-quality PNG?

No. Converting to PNG preserves the JPG exactly as it is, including its blur, blockiness, and artefacts. The PNG will be much larger, but the image will not look any better. The only way to get a sharper image is to start from a better original.

How many times can I convert an image before it looks bad?

One high-quality conversion is harmless. Two or three are usually fine if the quality stays high. Beyond that, especially if the quality drops each time, the damage becomes visible. Always convert from the original file, not from a previously converted copy.

Does converting an image affect its colours?

It can, slightly. Lossy compression simplifies subtle colour variations, and some converters drop the colour profile. The shift is usually invisible, but for colour-critical work such as product photos, keep the quality at 90 percent or higher and check the result against the original.

Should I convert my photos to HEIC to save space?

HEIC does save space, but it creates compatibility problems on Windows and older software. Unless you work entirely inside the Apple ecosystem, JPG or WebP are safer choices. Store your masters as JPG or PNG and convert copies for specific needs instead.
