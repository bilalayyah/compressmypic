Image Metadata Viewer: Complete Guide

Learn what an image metadata viewer shows, from EXIF camera settings and GPS coordinates to PNG and WebP chunks, and how to inspect any photo privately in your browser.

Every photo you take carries a hidden layer of information. Camera settings, capture time, device model, and sometimes your exact location travel inside the file, invisible in the image itself.

An image metadata viewer reads that hidden layer and shows it to you in plain language. It turns raw binary headers into a readable report you can actually use.

This guide explains what image metadata is, what the viewer reveals, and how to use it before you share a photo. It also covers privacy risks, JSON export, and how to verify a cleaned file.

The Image Metadata Viewer runs entirely in your browser. Your photos never leave your device, which matters because the whole point is privacy.

What Is Image Metadata?

Image metadata is data stored inside an image file alongside the pixels. It describes the photo rather than showing it, like a label stuck to the back of a printed picture.

The most common kind is EXIF, short for Exchangeable Image File Format. Cameras and phones write EXIF data automatically every time you press the shutter button.

EXIF can include the camera make and model, lens details, aperture, shutter speed, ISO, focal length, flash state, and the exact date and time the photo was taken.

Other metadata lives in different places. PNG files carry text chunks for author, software, and creation time. WebP files can hold EXIF blocks and animation flags. JPEG files can also carry XMP packets and comments.

None of this is visible when you look at the picture. You need a viewer to see it, and most people never check. That is why photos shared online often leak more than their owners realise.

What the Viewer Shows You

Drop an image into the viewer and it parses the raw file bytes on your device. Within a moment you get a summary, a privacy risk banner, and a grouped table of every field it found.

The summary shows the detected container format, the pixel dimensions, and the file size. It also tells you whether any sensitive embedded metadata tag types were found.

The table groups entries so you can scan them quickly. Camera fields sit together, EXIF exposure fields sit together, GPS fields sit together, and PNG or WebP chunks get their own group.

Sensitive fields carry an amber Privacy Tag label. Typical examples include GPS coordinates, exact capture timestamps, photographer names, camera serial numbers, and device model strings.

If GPS data exists, a warning box highlights the exact decoded latitude and longitude. It also offers a one-click jump to the Remove Image Metadata tool, so you can clean the file right away.

EXIF Fields Explained

EXIF is organised into directories called IFDs. The viewer reads TIFF IFD0, ExifIFD, and GPSIFD tables from JPEG files, plus PNG eXIf blocks and WebP EXIF chunks.

IFD0 holds basic facts about the image and the device. Camera make and model live here, along with the software that last edited the file and any copyright note.

ExifIFD holds the exposure telemetry recorded when the photo was taken. Shutter speed, aperture, ISO, focal length, metering mode, and flash state all live in this directory.

GPSIFD holds location data as rational numbers. The viewer converts degrees, minutes, and seconds into decimal latitude and longitude automatically, so you can read the coordinates directly.

Not every field appears in every photo. A screenshot has no camera data, and a photo downloaded from a messaging app usually has no EXIF at all, because the app stripped it during upload.

When a field is missing, that is useful information too. It tells you the file has already been processed, or that it was never a camera original in the first place.

GPS Data and Privacy

GPS coordinates are the most sensitive thing a photo can carry. A single pair of numbers can point to your home, your workplace, your child's school, or the exact spot where a photo was taken.

Phones record GPS data by default in most camera apps. The coordinates are stored silently, and nothing in the photo itself hints that they are there.

Sharing an unstripped photo online can therefore reveal your location to anyone who downloads it and opens it in a metadata viewer. That includes strangers on marketplaces, forums, and social platforms.

The viewer makes this risk visible. When GPSIFD tags exist, the amber GPS Location Coordinates Detected banner appears right above the properties table, showing the decoded coordinates.

Checking before you post takes seconds. If coordinates appear, strip them with the Remove Image Metadata tool before the photo goes anywhere public.

PNG and WebP Technical Headers

JPEG gets most of the attention, but PNG and WebP files carry their own metadata. The viewer reads these technical chunks so nothing stays hidden.

For PNG files it reads the IHDR chunk, which holds bit depth and colour type. It also reads pHYs, the chunk that stores physical pixel density for print or layout scaling.

PNG text chunks matter for privacy too. tEXt and iTXt chunks can hold software names, author names, comments, and creation times. The tIME chunk records when the file was last changed.

For WebP files the viewer reads VP8X flags, which show whether the image has an alpha channel or animation. WebP EXIF chunks are decoded alongside them, so camera data in a WebP file is not missed.

The viewer also reports the true container format by inspecting binary magic bytes, not the filename. A PNG renamed with a .jpg extension is still reported as a PNG.

How to Use the Viewer

Using the viewer takes five steps, and none of them needs an account or an upload. Everything happens on your own device.

  1. Drop or select an image to inspect. JPG, PNG, WebP, HEIC, GIF, BMP, and SVG files up to 20 MB are accepted, or paste with Ctrl+V.
  2. Check the summary and privacy risk banner at the top. It shows the format, dimensions, file size, and whether sensitive tags were found.
  3. Review any GPS location alert. If coordinates exist, the warning box shows the exact latitude and longitude.
  4. Browse the extracted metadata table. Entries are grouped by Camera, EXIF, GPS, PNG or WebP chunks, and File info, with amber Privacy Tag labels on sensitive fields.
  5. Copy or export JSON, or strip the tags. Click Copy JSON for the full report, Export JSON to save a .json file, or Strip Metadata to clean the file.

The viewer is strictly read-only. It reads headers from browser memory to show you what is inside, without changing, compressing, or deleting anything in your original file.

You can inspect files back to back without refreshing. Click the red trash icon in the summary bar to reset to the upload zone, then drop the next image.

Copying and Exporting JSON Reports

Sometimes you need the metadata in a form you can keep, share, or paste into a bug report. The viewer exports the full parsed report as structured JSON.

Click Copy JSON in the summary bar to place the whole ImageMetadataReport on your clipboard. It is ready to paste into a document, a ticket, or a chat message.

Click Export JSON to download a file named after your image with a _metadata suffix. Developers use these reports for debugging image pipelines and documenting file behaviour.

The JSON includes every field the viewer decoded, grouped the same way as the on-screen table. That makes it easy to compare two files, or to keep a record of what a file contained before cleaning.

Verifying a Cleaned File

One of the most useful jobs for a metadata viewer is checking someone else's work. After you strip a photo, drop the cleaned output back into the viewer to confirm the tags are really gone.

If scrubbing succeeded, the green status banner confirms that no EXIF, GPS, or hidden camera tags were detected, and no GPS alert appears. That is a real check of the file bytes, not a guess.

This matters because some tools claim to strip metadata but leave chunks behind. A PNG tEXt block or a JPEG comment can survive a careless cleaner. The viewer catches those leftovers.

Make verification a habit for sensitive photos. Inspect, strip, then inspect again. The second inspection takes seconds and proves the first step worked.

If the cleaned file still shows tags, try the Full Canvas Re-encode option in the Remove Image Metadata tool. It rebuilds the pixels from scratch, which drops every inherited binary segment.

When to Check Image Metadata

Check metadata any time a photo leaves your private storage. The risk is not the photo itself, but who can download it and what they can read from it.

  • Before posting to a marketplace listing, where buyers download full-resolution images.
  • Before sharing family photos on social media, especially pictures taken at home or school.
  • Before sending photos to a journalist, client, or stranger who may forward them further.
  • Before publishing photos on a blog or website, where files stay downloadable for years.
  • After receiving a photo, to learn when and where it was taken and with what camera.
  • When debugging an image pipeline, to see which fields survive each processing step.

Photographers check metadata for a different reason. Exposure settings from a great shot are a recipe worth keeping, and the viewer reads ISO, aperture, and shutter speed in seconds.

Developers check it when images behave oddly. A wrong orientation flag, an unexpected DPI value, or a hidden colour profile often explains a rendering bug.

What the Viewer Cannot Do

The viewer is honest about its limits. Its deep binary chunk parser targets JPEG, PNG, and WebP containers natively in JavaScript, and those three formats get the fullest reports.

For HEIC, GIF, BMP, and SVG files, the viewer reports reliable container info, file size, and pixel dimensions. Full binary EXIF chunks are not extracted for those formats, so a HEIC file may show less detail than the same photo as a JPEG.

Pasted screenshots usually show no camera tags. Operating system clipboards pass raw raster bitmaps rather than original camera files, so clipboard images lack camera headers entirely.

Photos downloaded from WhatsApp or Instagram show zero EXIF tags for a similar reason. Social networks and messaging apps strip EXIF, camera, and GPS headers automatically when you upload through their servers.

The 20 MB per-file limit covers full-resolution DSLR JPEGs, detailed PNG exports, and uncompressed BMP files. Larger files need to be compressed first, for example with the image compressor.

Checking Metadata on Mobile

Most photos are taken on phones, so the viewer works in mobile browsers too. Open the page in mobile Safari or Chrome and pick a photo directly from your camera roll.

You can check hidden GPS coordinates and camera details on your phone before posting pictures online. That is the moment the check matters most, because posting usually happens from the phone.

The interface is the same as on desktop. The summary banner, the GPS alert, and the grouped table all appear, and the Copy JSON and Strip Metadata actions work the same way.

If you find GPS data on a phone photo, strip it before sharing. The Remove Image Metadata tool also works on mobile, so the whole inspect-and-clean workflow fits in your pocket.

The Short Version

Image metadata is the hidden data inside every photo: camera settings, timestamps, software notes, and sometimes GPS coordinates. A viewer makes it visible.

Check any photo before it goes public, especially pictures taken at home, at school, or at work. Look for the GPS alert first, then scan the amber Privacy Tag labels.

Export JSON when you need a record, and verify cleaned files by inspecting them again. The viewer is free, needs no signup, and never uploads your photos anywhere.

Start with the Image Metadata Viewer, and when a file needs cleaning, move straight to the Remove Image Metadata tool. Your photos should show the world what you chose to share, and nothing else.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between EXIF, XMP, and IPTC metadata?

EXIF is camera-written data such as exposure settings, timestamps, and GPS coordinates. XMP is an Adobe XML packet that can hold editing history, ratings, and descriptions. IPTC is a press and publishing standard for captions, credits, and keywords. A photo can carry all three at once, and the viewer shows EXIF and XMP-style fields plus PNG text chunks in one grouped table.

Can a metadata viewer tell me where a photo was taken?

Yes, if the photo contains GPS coordinates. The viewer decodes the GPSIFD tags into decimal latitude and longitude and shows them in a warning banner. Without GPS tags, the viewer cannot guess a location — it can only show the capture time and camera details, which may hint at a place but never prove one.

Does the viewer work with photos taken on a DSLR or mirrorless camera?

Yes. DSLR and mirrorless cameras write rich EXIF data, including lens model, focal length, aperture, shutter speed, ISO, and metering mode. The viewer decodes all of these from the ExifIFD directory. Full-resolution JPEGs up to 20 MB are supported, which covers most camera exports.

Why do some of my photos show metadata and others show none?

Original files from your camera roll keep full metadata. Photos that passed through WhatsApp, Instagram, Facebook, or email usually arrive stripped, because those services remove EXIF and GPS headers on upload. Screenshots also show no camera tags, since the clipboard passes raw pixels rather than the original camera file.

Is it safe to inspect a private photo in an online metadata viewer?

It depends on the tool. This viewer parses file bytes locally in your browser, so zero bytes leave your device — nothing is uploaded to a server. Avoid viewers that require an upload, because sending an unstripped photo to a server hands your GPS data and camera details to a third party.

Can I use the viewer to check a photo someone else sent me?

Yes. Save the photo, drop it into the viewer, and read the grouped table. You can see when it was taken, with what camera, and whether it carries GPS coordinates. Keep in mind that a stripped file shows nothing — that only proves the metadata was removed, not that the photo is original.
