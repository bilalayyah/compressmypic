Image Splitter: Complete Guide

Split any image into grid tiles or strips: how grid, horizontal, and vertical modes work, presets, full-resolution exports, and the best uses for each split.

An image splitter cuts one picture into several smaller pieces. The pieces can form a grid for a social profile, strips for a carousel, or slices for printing a large image across many pages. The original image stays intact; the splitter simply gives you its parts.

The image splitter divides photos into N by M grid tiles, horizontal rows, or vertical strips, and downloads the pieces individually or as one ZIP. It runs entirely in your browser, so your photos never leave your device.

This guide explains the three split modes, the presets, the export options, and the jobs each mode suits best. Whether you plan an Instagram grid, slice a panorama, or cut a long screenshot for printing, the split is the same simple idea.

What Is an Image Splitter?

An image splitter divides a single image into a set of smaller images along straight cut lines. A 3 by 3 grid split turns one square photo into nine square tiles. A horizontal strip split turns a tall image into stacked rows, and a vertical strip split turns a wide image into side-by-side columns.

Each tile is a real image file, not a crop preview. You can download the tiles one by one or all at once in a ZIP, then use them wherever separate images are needed: social posts, print pages, game tiles, or design mockups.

The split is non-destructive. The original file is never changed, and you can split the same image again with different settings as many times as you like. A 3 by 3 grid today and a 2 by 4 grid tomorrow both come from the same untouched source.

Splitting is different from cropping. Cropping throws part of the image away to keep one piece, while splitting keeps every part and gives you all of them. The tiles together contain the whole original, just in separate files.

Grid Mode: N by M Tiles

Grid mode is the main mode. You set a number of rows and columns, and the tool divides the image into that many equal tiles. A 3 by 3 grid gives nine tiles, a 2 by 4 grid gives eight, and the maximum grid is 10 by 10, which gives one hundred tiles from one image.

The grid is the mode for Instagram profile grids, where nine square tiles form one large picture across the profile page. It is also the mode for puzzles, mosaics, and any layout where the tiles sit side by side to rebuild the original.

Each tile is cut at full original pixel dimensions, so a 3000 pixel photo split 3 by 3 gives nine 1000 pixel tiles. Nothing is scaled down or resampled, which means the tiles stay sharp enough to print or post at full quality.

Numbered cut lines appear on the preview, so you can see exactly where each tile begins and ends before you download. If a cut line passes through a face or an important detail, change the grid or reposition the image rather than accepting a bad cut.

Horizontal Strips

Horizontal strips cut the image into stacked rows. A tall image split into four horizontal strips gives four wide, short images that read top to bottom, like the pages of a long document.

This mode suits long screenshots that need printing. A full-page screenshot is too tall for one sheet of paper, but four horizontal strips print cleanly across four pages. The same idea works for tall infographics that need to be shared as separate slides.

Horizontal strips also suit social carousels where each slide continues the image downward. The viewer swipes through the strips and the picture reveals itself in order, which creates a simple storytelling effect.

Choose the number of strips to match the destination. Four strips for four printed pages, three for a three-slide carousel, and two for a simple top-and-bottom split. The strips are equal height, so the content divides evenly.

Vertical Strips

Vertical strips cut the image into side-by-side columns. A wide panorama split into three vertical strips gives three tall images that read left to right, like the panels of a triptych.

This mode suits wide panoramas and banners that are too wide for one post or one page. A panorama split into vertical strips can be posted as a carousel where each swipe reveals the next part of the scene, which suits travel and landscape photography.

Vertical strips also suit website banners and headers that need to be rebuilt across columns. A designer can place each strip in its own column and reassemble the panorama in a layout where a single wide image would not fit.

As with horizontal strips, the strips are equal width, so the content divides evenly. A panorama with its subject in the center may need an odd number of strips so the subject lands in the middle strip rather than on a cut line.

Split Presets

Presets are one-click split settings for the common jobs. The 3 by 3 Instagram grid is the best-known preset, but the tool includes others for common grid sizes and strip counts, so you can skip the manual row and column settings.

A preset is a starting point, not a rule. Tap the 3 by 3 preset for an Instagram grid, then adjust the rows or columns if your image or your plan needs a different shape. The preset fills in the numbers, and you stay free to change them.

Presets help when you split images often. A social media manager who builds a grid every week can tap the same preset each time and get the same tile sizes, which keeps the profile grid consistent from one puzzle to the next.

If no preset matches your job, set the rows and columns by hand. A 4 by 5 grid for a poster, a 1 by 6 strip for a banner, or a 5 by 5 grid for a mosaic are all valid splits, and the manual controls cover every combination up to 10 by 10.

Full-Resolution Tiles

Tiles are cut at full original pixel dimensions, which means a split never reduces quality. A 3000 by 3000 pixel photo split into nine tiles gives nine 1000 by 1000 pixel images, each as sharp as the corresponding part of the original.

This matters for printing and for large displays. A tile that was scaled down would look soft when printed or viewed at full size, while a full-resolution tile holds the same detail as the source. The splitter is a cutting tool, not a resizing tool.

The trade-off is file size. Nine full-resolution tiles together are roughly the same total size as the original image, and a 10 by 10 split gives one hundred files to manage. Download the ZIP to keep them together rather than saving each tile by hand.

Start from the highest resolution source you have. A 600 pixel photo split 3 by 3 gives nine 200 pixel tiles, which look fine as thumbnails but poor as posts. A large source gives large tiles, and large tiles survive every use.

Numbered Tiles and ZIP Download

Every tile is numbered so you know where it belongs. The numbering follows the reading order: left to right, top to bottom, starting at the top-left corner. Tile one is the top-left piece, and the last tile is the bottom-right piece.

Numbering matters most for Instagram grids, where the tiles must be posted in reverse order so they land in the right places. The numbers tell you the grid position of each tile, so the posting order is never a guess.

The ZIP download contains all the tiles in one file, named in order. Downloading the ZIP is faster than saving each tile individually, and it keeps the set together on your device. Unzip it and the numbered files are ready to use.

You can also download tiles individually if you only need one or two of them. A single tile for a profile picture or a single strip for a banner does not need the whole set, and the individual download saves the trouble of unzipping.

Input Formats

The splitter accepts JPG, PNG, WebP, HEIC, GIF, BMP, and SVG files up to 20 MB. Photos from phones, screenshots, and graphics from design tools all work without conversion. An iPhone HEIC photo uploads and splits like any other file.

The tile format follows your choice, not the input. A JPG source can produce PNG tiles, and a PNG source can produce JPG tiles. Choose the format for the destination: JPG for photos and small files, PNG for transparency and sharp graphics.

Note that the Tile Quality slider appears for JPG tiles only. PNG tiles are lossless, so there is no quality setting to adjust. If you need the smallest files, choose JPG and set the quality; if you need exact pixels, choose PNG.

Animated GIFs do not split as animations. Uploading an animated GIF gives you the tiles of one frame, not a set of animated tiles. Split a still image for grids and strips, and keep animations whole.

Transparency and Sprite Sheets

Splitting a transparent PNG preserves the transparency in each tile. A sprite sheet with transparent cells splits into transparent tiles, which is exactly what game developers need when they cut an icon sheet or a tileset into individual sprites.

Choose PNG as the tile format for transparent work. JPG has no transparency, so a transparent tile exported as JPG gains a solid background, usually white or black. The transparency survives only when the format supports it.

Sprite sheets need exact cuts. A sheet of 32 by 32 pixel icons split into a grid gives tiles of exactly 32 pixels each, as long as the sheet dimensions divide evenly. Check the tile dimensions after the split before using them in a game engine.

When the sheet does not divide evenly, the edge tiles may differ slightly in size. That is normal for a grid split, and it means the sheet has a margin or a gap the grid did not account for. Trim the source to an exact multiple first for pixel-perfect tiles.

Splitting for Print

A large image printed across several pages is the classic print use for a splitter. A poster split into a grid of tiles prints on many sheets of paper, which you trim and join into a large poster. The tile numbers tell you the order.

Horizontal strips suit long documents and banners that need to print across pages. A tall infographic split into four strips prints on four pages, each one a clean section of the original. The strips are equal height, so the pages match.

Check the tile dimensions against the paper size before printing. A tile that is wider than the printable area gets cropped by the printer, while a tile that is much smaller wastes paper. Set the grid so each tile matches a sheet.

Print at full resolution and do not let the printer scale the tiles. A scaled tile loses the sharpness that the full-resolution split preserved. Set the printer to actual size and the tiles will join cleanly.

Splitting Screenshots

Long screenshots are the most common thing people split. A full-page screenshot of a website or a chat is too tall to read comfortably and too tall to print, but horizontal strips turn it into readable sections.

Paste a screenshot straight from the clipboard with Ctrl and V. The splitter accepts pasted images, so a screenshot never needs to be saved as a file first. Paste, choose the strip count, and download the sections.

Choose the strip count to match the content. A screenshot with four natural sections splits into four strips, while a very long page may need six or eight. More strips mean shorter, more readable sections, but also more files to manage.

Watch for cut lines through text. A strip boundary that passes through a line of text makes both strips hard to read. Adjust the strip count until the boundaries fall in the gaps between paragraphs or sections.

Common Mistakes

The most common mistake is splitting a low-resolution image into too many tiles. A 600 pixel photo split 3 by 3 gives nine 200 pixel tiles, which look poor as posts. Start from a large source, or use fewer tiles.

Another mistake is a cut line through a face or a key detail. The preview shows the cut lines before you download, so check them and adjust the grid or the image position. A face split across two tiles looks like an error, not a design.

A third mistake is choosing the wrong tile format. Transparent tiles exported as JPG lose their transparency and gain a solid background. Choose PNG whenever transparency matters, and JPG when file size matters.

Finally, many people forget the posting order for grids. Instagram displays the most recent post first, so grid tiles must be posted in reverse order to land correctly. The tile numbers give the grid position; post from the last tile to the first.

Quick Reference: Splitting Checklist

Start from a large source, choose Grid, Horizontal, or Vertical mode, and set the rows and columns or tap a preset. Check the numbered cut lines in the preview, especially around faces and text.

Choose JPG for photos and PNG for transparency, download the ZIP, and post grid tiles in reverse order. If a cut lands badly, change the grid and split again rather than accepting it.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between splitting and cropping an image?

Cropping keeps one piece of an image and throws the rest away. Splitting keeps every piece and gives you all of them as separate files. The tiles together contain the whole original, just divided along straight cut lines.

Will splitting reduce the quality of my photo?

No. Tiles are cut at full original pixel dimensions, so each tile is as sharp as the corresponding part of the source. Quality only drops if you start from a low-resolution source, because the tiles are then small as well.

What is the largest grid I can split an image into?

The maximum grid is 10 by 10, which gives 100 tiles from one image. Most jobs need far fewer: 3 by 3 for an Instagram grid, 2 by 2 for a simple split, or a handful of strips for a long screenshot.

Can I split an image into uneven pieces?

No, the splitter divides the image into equal tiles or strips. If the image dimensions do not divide evenly, the edge tiles may differ by a pixel or two. For exact uneven pieces, crop each section separately with an image cropper instead.

Do split tiles keep the original file format?

You choose the tile format yourself. A JPG source can produce PNG tiles and a PNG source can produce JPG tiles. Choose JPG for photos and small files, and PNG when you need transparency or exact pixels.

Why would I split an image instead of just posting the whole photo?

Splitting serves jobs a single image cannot: a grid puzzle across a profile page, a carousel that reveals a panorama swipe by swipe, a poster printed across many pages, or a long screenshot divided into readable sections.
