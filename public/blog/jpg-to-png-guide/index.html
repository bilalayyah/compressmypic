JPG to PNG: Complete Guide

Convert JPG photos to PNG the right way. When it helps, why files get bigger, and how to use PNG for editing, text and graphics.

Converting a JPG to PNG feels like an upgrade. PNG sounds better, lossless sounds better, and the file looks more professional. The reality is more complicated than that.

A JPG to PNG conversion never improves the image. It preserves the JPG exactly as it is, flaws included, while making the file much bigger.

This guide explains when that trade is worth making, and when it is a waste of disk space.

What Happens When JPG Becomes PNG?

The converter decodes the JPG into pixels, then encodes those pixels as a PNG. The pixels are the same ones the JPG held, including every compression artefact and soft edge.

PNG stores those pixels losslessly, so nothing further is lost. The result is a perfect copy of the JPG, not a better version of the original scene.

The file gets bigger because PNG must describe every artefact exactly. A 300KB JPG often becomes a 1.5MB to 3MB PNG, with no visible improvement at all.

Understanding this is the key to the whole tool. JPG to PNG is a format change, not a quality improvement.

Why Do PNG Files Get So Much Bigger?

JPG compression discards data aggressively, which is why JPG files are small. PNG compression is lossless, so it must keep everything the JPG left behind, including its mistakes.

A JPG artefact, such as a blocky patch in a sky, is random-looking data. Random data compresses poorly, so PNG needs many bytes to describe it exactly.

Detailed photos suffer most. A busy landscape has endless texture, and PNG must store every pixel of it, while JPG could simplify the texture away.

Simple images suffer least. A mostly white scanned form converts to a modest PNG, because large flat areas compress well even losslessly.

When Is JPG to PNG Genuinely Useful?

Editing is the best reason. If you plan to annotate, crop, and re-save an image many times, PNG stops the damage from accumulating, while repeated JPG saves keep adding damage.

Text overlays are another good reason. Adding sharp text to a JPG and saving again smears the letters, while PNG keeps them crisp through many edits.

Some software demands PNG input. A design tool, a print workflow, or an app that only accepts PNG may force the conversion, regardless of file size.

Archiving an edited result is a third reason. Once you finish editing a JPG, saving the final version as PNG locks in the result without further loss.

When Is JPG to PNG Pointless?

Converting a JPG to PNG to make it sharper is pointless. The sharpness was lost when the JPG was created, and no format change restores it.

Converting for a website is usually pointless too. A PNG photo is several times larger than the JPG, so the page loads slower for zero visible benefit.

Converting to save space is backwards. PNG is almost always larger than the JPG it came from, so the conversion grows the file instead of shrinking it.

If the goal is a smaller file, convert the JPG to WebP instead. The JPG to WebP converter shrinks photos while keeping them looking clean.

Why There Is No Quality Slider

The quality slider disappears on the JPG to PNG converter, and that is correct. PNG is lossless, so there is no quality level to choose.

Every pixel is stored exactly, and the file size is whatever it needs to be. No setting can make the PNG smaller without losing data, and PNG does not lose data.

If the PNG comes out too large, the problem is the choice of format, not a missing setting. Switch to WebP or JPG for a smaller file.

This is a good reminder that PNG is a tool for specific jobs, not a general-purpose photo format. Use it where lossless matters, and avoid it elsewhere.

The Transparency Myth

Converting a JPG with a white background to PNG does not make the background transparent. The white pixels are real pixels, and PNG stores them as white.

Transparency must exist in the source. A JPG has no transparent areas at all, so the PNG version has none either, no matter what you do.

To get a transparent background, remove it first with the background remover, then save as PNG. The converter alone cannot invent transparency.

This myth wastes a lot of time. If your goal is a transparent logo, start from a PNG with transparency or remove the background properly.

Renaming Is Not Converting

Changing a file's extension from .jpg to .png does not convert it. The file is still a JPG inside, and programs that check the contents will reject it or misread it.

Some apps tolerate the lie, but many do not. Upload forms, design tools, and printers check the real format, and a renamed file fails their checks.

Always convert properly with a real tool. The converter rewrites the file structure, not just the name, so the result is a genuine PNG.

If a file you received has the wrong extension, convert it properly rather than renaming it back. The converter reads the actual contents, not the name.

Batch Converting JPGs to PNG

You can convert up to twenty JPGs at once and download them as one ZIP. Each file converts independently, so one bad file cannot stop the batch.

Files up to 25MB each are accepted, which covers high-resolution camera exports, DSLR photographs, and wide digital artwork.

Watch the total size. Twenty JPGs at 300KB each become a ZIP of several megabytes as PNGs, so check that the destination can handle the larger files.

Use the Compare viewer on one or two files before downloading the batch. It confirms zero colour shifting between the JPG and the PNG, so you know the conversion is faithful.

CMYK JPGs and Colour Shifts

Some JPGs from print workflows use CMYK colour instead of RGB. Those files can look wrong when converted, since browsers and screens expect RGB.

The converter handles the colour space, but a CMYK JPG converted to PNG may look slightly different from the print version. Check the result before using it.

For screen use, RGB is the correct colour space. If your JPG came from a print shop, ask for an RGB export rather than converting the CMYK file yourself.

Colour profiles are another subtle point. The converter preserves the visible colours, but exotic profiles may not survive the trip exactly.

A Sensible Editing Workflow

Start from the best original you have. If the original is a JPG, accept its quality and convert to PNG once, at the start of editing.

Do all your edits on the PNG version. Crop, annotate, add text, and adjust colours, saving as PNG each time without any further loss.

Export the final result in the format the destination needs. A web page wants WebP or JPG, a print shop wants PNG or TIFF, and a form wants JPG.

Keep the PNG master after exporting. If you need a different export later, you can make it from the PNG without reopening the original JPG.

EXIF Data and Privacy

Converting JPG to PNG strips EXIF metadata, including GPS location tags and camera details. The canvas redraws the pixels, and the metadata does not survive.

That is good for privacy. A photo shared as PNG carries no record of where or when it was taken, which is safer than sharing the original JPG.

It is bad for archiving, though. If you need the camera settings or the date, note them before converting, since the PNG will not carry them.

For scanned IDs and personal portraits, the stripping is a benefit. The fewer hidden details a shared file carries, the better.

Where PNG Files Work Best

PNG opens in Photoshop, Illustrator, Figma, Canva, PowerPoint, and every modern browser. It is a universal format for graphics and editing.

Websites accept PNG uploads everywhere, though the large file size makes it a poor choice for photos. Use PNG on the web only for logos, icons, and graphics.

Print shops accept PNG, and the lossless quality suits print well. A PNG with sharp text prints cleanly at any size.

Office apps handle PNG natively. A PNG pasted into Word or PowerPoint keeps its sharp edges, which is why screenshots belong in PNG.

PNG for Presentations

Presentations need sharp images, and PNG delivers them. A screenshot pasted into PowerPoint as PNG keeps its text crisp, while the same screenshot as JPG looks soft on a big screen.

For slides with diagrams and charts, PNG is the right format. The thin lines and small labels stay readable, even when the slide is projected onto a large screen.

For slides with photographs, JPG is usually better. The photos are smaller, the presentation file stays manageable, and the images look identical at presentation sizes.

Mix the two formats in one deck. Photos as JPG, screenshots and diagrams as PNG, and the presentation is both sharp and reasonably sized.

Check the total presentation size before sharing it. A deck full of PNG photos can exceed email limits, so convert the photos to JPG if the file is too large.

PNG for Social Media Graphics

Social media graphics often contain text, and PNG keeps that text sharp. An announcement graphic saved as PNG reads cleanly, while the JPG version softens the letters.

Platforms compress uploads anyway, so the PNG advantage is partial. A PNG uploaded to Instagram becomes a JPG on the platform, though starting from a sharp PNG gives a better result.

For a profile picture or a cover image, PNG is a good choice. The image is small, the file size hardly matters, and the sharpness is visible.

For a photo post, JPG is better. The photo is large, the PNG version would be enormous, and the platform compresses it regardless of the source format.

Design the graphic at twice the display size in PNG, then let the platform scale it down. A larger source produces a sharper result after the platform's compression.

PNG for Print Work

Print shops accept PNG, and the lossless quality suits print well. A PNG with sharp text prints cleanly at any size, without the softening that JPG compression causes.

For printed documents, use PNG at 300 DPI or higher. A 300 DPI PNG prints crisply, while a low-resolution PNG looks soft on paper regardless of the format.

For printed photos, JPG at high quality is usually fine and far smaller. The photo prints identically, and the smaller file is easier to send to the print shop.

Ask the print shop what they prefer before sending files. Many accept both formats, and their workflow may favour one over the other for practical reasons.

For a logo that must print sharply, PNG with transparency is the standard. The logo sits cleanly on any background colour in the printed design.

Storing PNG Masters

A PNG master is the lossless working copy of an image, and it deserves safe storage. Keep it in a folder named masters, separate from the delivery copies.

Back up the masters in two places. A local drive and a cloud backup protect against hardware failure, theft, and accidental deletion, which are the real risks to an archive.

Do not convert the masters themselves. Generate every delivery format from the master, so each conversion is a first generation with minimal damage.

Name the masters clearly, with the purpose and the date. A name such as logo-master-2026.png is useful years later, while a name such as final-v2.png is not.

Review the masters every year or two. Formats change, and a master stored in an obsolete format may need migrating to a current one before the old format becomes unreadable.

PNG for App Store Screenshots

App store screenshots must look sharp, since they sell the app. PNG keeps the interface text crisp, while JPG softens the small labels that describe features.

Take the screenshots at the highest resolution the device offers. A retina screenshot has twice the pixels, and the text stays sharp even after the store scales it down.

Convert the JPG screenshots to PNG before uploading them to the store. The lossless format preserves the interface exactly, and the store's compression has a good starting point.

Check the store's size and dimension rules before uploading. Apple and Google each list exact sizes, and a screenshot in the wrong shape gets rejected.

Keep the PNG masters in the project folder after uploading. If the app updates, the screenshots can be retaken from the same masters without starting over.

The rule is simple: convert JPG to PNG for editing, not for quality. Open the JPG to PNG tool when you need a lossless working copy. Frequently asked questions

Will converting JPG to PNG make my photo look sharper?

No. The conversion preserves the JPG exactly as it is, including its blur and compression artefacts. PNG cannot restore detail that the JPG already discarded. The file gets bigger, but the image looks identical. Only start from a better original if you need a sharper photo.

Why is my PNG file five times bigger than the JPG?

PNG is lossless, so it stores every pixel exactly, including the random-looking artefacts left by JPG compression. Those artefacts compress poorly, which inflates the file. Detailed photos suffer most, while simple scans stay modest. The size jump is normal, not an error.

Can converting JPG to PNG add transparency to a photo?

No. A JPG has no transparent areas, so the PNG version has none either. The white background stays white, because those are real pixels. To get transparency, remove the background first with a background removal tool, then save the result as PNG.

Is JPG to PNG conversion safe for scanned documents?

Yes, and it is often the right choice. Scanned documents benefit from PNG because text stays sharp through editing and re-saving. The file is larger, but for a document that must stay readable, the trade is worth it. For photos, though, JPG or WebP is better.

Can I convert JPG to PNG on my phone?

Yes. Open the converter in your phone browser, pick the JPG from your gallery, and download the PNG. The conversion runs on the phone, and the file downloads to your device. No app install is needed.

When should I choose JPG to PNG over JPG to WebP?

Choose PNG when you need lossless editing, maximum compatibility with design tools, or sharp text that must survive many saves. Choose WebP when you need smaller files for the web. PNG is the editing format, WebP is the delivery format.
