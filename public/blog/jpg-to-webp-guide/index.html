JPG to WebP for Faster Websites

Convert JPG photos to WebP for faster websites. How much smaller, which quality to use, browser support, and when to keep JPG instead.

WebP is the format Google built to make the web faster, and it works. A JPG photo converted to WebP is typically 25 to 35 percent smaller, with no visible difference in quality.

That saving matters. Smaller images mean faster pages, happier visitors, and better search rankings, which is why every major website now serves WebP.

This guide explains how JPG to WebP conversion works, which settings to use, and when to keep the JPG instead.

What Is WebP?

WebP is an image format developed by Google and released in 2010. It was designed from the start for the web, with modern compression that beats JPG at the same quality.

The format supports both lossy and lossless compression, plus transparency. That combination makes it a replacement for JPG, PNG, and GIF in many web uses.

WebP files use the .webp extension. They look like ordinary images, and modern browsers display them natively without any plugin or extra software.

Google recommends WebP for faster websites and better Core Web Vitals, which are the speed measurements that affect search rankings.

How Much Smaller Is WebP Than JPG?

For photographs, WebP is typically 25 to 35 percent smaller than JPG at the same visual quality. A 1MB JPG photo becomes a 650KB to 750KB WebP.

The saving varies by image. Detailed landscapes save less, since there is more texture to preserve, while smooth portraits save more.

Some images save even more. A simple graphic or a screenshot with large flat areas can shrink by half or more as WebP.

A few images save nothing at all. A JPG that is already heavily compressed has little left for WebP to improve, and the WebP version may even be slightly larger.

How Does JPG to WebP Conversion Work?

The converter decodes the JPG into pixels, then encodes those pixels as WebP at the quality you chose. The pixels are the same, but the compression is modern.

The conversion runs entirely in your browser. Your photos are never uploaded to a server, and the work happens at local processor speed.

Files up to 25MB each are accepted, which covers high-resolution camera photos and large screenshots without any preparation.

Each file converts independently in a batch, so one bad file cannot stop the rest. A corrupted JPG shows an error badge, while the valid files finish normally.

Quality Settings That Work

For website photos, 75 to 85 percent WebP quality is the sweet spot. The images look clean, the files are small, and the pages load fast.

At 80 percent, most photos are indistinguishable from the original JPG. The saving over JPG is large, yet the visible quality is the same.

For product photos on an online store, use 80 to 85 percent. Customers zoom into products, so the extra quality protects the details they inspect.

For photos you plan to print, do not use WebP at all. Printers and print shops expect JPG or PNG, and WebP adds compatibility risk for no benefit on paper.

Which Browsers Support WebP?

Chrome, Edge, Firefox, Safari, and Opera all support WebP natively now. That covers the overwhelming majority of web traffic.

Safari added support in version 14, released in 2020. Older iPhones and Macs running earlier versions cannot display WebP, though those devices are increasingly rare.

Internet Explorer never supported WebP, but it is discontinued and its usage is negligible. No modern website needs to support it.

For the small number of visitors on very old browsers, serve a JPG fallback. Modern websites do this automatically with the picture element.

Dimensions and Metadata

Converting JPG to WebP does not change the pixel dimensions. A 4000 by 3000 photo stays 4000 by 3000, only the format and file size change.

EXIF camera data and GPS tags are stripped during conversion. Drawing onto a canvas removes the metadata, which protects your privacy when publishing photos online.

Colour profiles are handled by the browser encoder. Most photos keep their colours accurately, though very saturated images may shift slightly.

The output filename keeps your original name with a .webp extension. A file called hero.jpg becomes hero.webp, so it is easy to find.

Using WebP in WordPress, Shopify, and Wix

WordPress supports WebP uploads since version 5.8. You can upload converted WebP files directly to the media library, and they work like any other image.

Shopify converts uploaded images to WebP automatically in many cases, but uploading your own WebP files gives you control over the quality and size.

Wix also supports WebP. Upload the converted files to your media manager, and the platform serves them to visitors in the right format.

For any platform, test one image first. Upload a converted WebP, check that it displays correctly, and then convert the rest of the batch.

WebP for Product Photos

Product photos benefit enormously from WebP. An online store with hundreds of product images loads far faster when each one is 30 percent smaller.

Customers zoom into products, so keep the quality at 80 to 85 percent. Lower settings soften the fine detail that buyers inspect, such as fabric texture and stitching.

Convert the whole catalogue in one batch. The converter handles mixed folders, and the ZIP holds every WebP ready to upload.

Keep the JPG originals as well. If a marketplace demands JPG, you can upload those instead, without converting again.

Screenshots with Text

WebP preserves text better than JPG at the same file size. A screenshot converted to WebP at 85 percent keeps its letters readable, while the JPG version softens them.

That makes WebP the better choice for blog screenshots, documentation images, and tutorial graphics, where text is the content.

For screenshots where every letter must stay perfect, such as legal evidence, consider lossless WebP or PNG instead. Lossy WebP is excellent, but not perfect.

Check the result with the Compare viewer before downloading. Zoom into the smallest text and confirm it reads cleanly.

When WebP Comes Out Larger

Occasionally, a converted WebP is larger than the original JPG. That happens with simple or already-compressed JPGs, where WebP has little left to save.

It also happens at very high quality settings. A WebP at 95 percent quality can exceed the JPG it came from, since the encoder is preserving nearly everything.

When the WebP is larger, keep the JPG. There is no rule that says WebP must always win, and the goal is smaller files, not a specific format.

Check the before-and-after sizes on each card. The converter shows both numbers, so the decision is obvious at a glance.

When to Keep JPG Instead

Keep JPG when the destination demands it. Some upload forms, printers, and older programs list JPG specifically and reject WebP.

Keep JPG for email attachments. Email clients display JPG reliably, while WebP support in email is still inconsistent across apps.

Keep JPG for photos you will edit further. Most photo editors handle JPG natively, while WebP support in desktop editors is still catching up.

Keep JPG when the audience uses very old devices. A JPG opens on every device ever made, while WebP needs a browser from the last few years.

Checking Quality Before Downloading

Click Compare on any card to see the original JPG and the converted WebP side by side. Drag the split handle across the image to inspect it closely.

Check faces, text, and fine detail first. Those are the areas where low quality shows, and a ten-second inspection catches problems early.

You can change the quality after the first conversion without re-uploading. The original stays in the queue, so try a different setting and compare again.

For a batch, check three or four representative files rather than every one. If those look good, the rest will too, since the settings are the same.

WebP for Blogs

Blog images are the classic WebP use case. A blog post with ten photos loads far faster as WebP, and readers stay longer on fast pages.

Convert the blog's JPG photos to WebP at 80 percent quality. The images look identical, the files shrink by a third, and the pages feel noticeably quicker.

For blog screenshots with text, WebP at 85 percent keeps the letters readable. The text stays sharper than JPG at the same size, which matters for tutorial images.

Update the image references after converting, and test the post. A broken image reference is worse than a slow one, so check every image on the page.

Keep the JPG originals in the media library as a fallback. If a reader's browser cannot display WebP, the JPG version loads instead, and nobody sees a broken image.

WebP for Online Shops

An online shop lives or dies by its images. Hundreds of product photos, each a third smaller as WebP, make the difference between a fast shop and a slow one.

Convert product photos to WebP at 80 to 85 percent quality. Customers zoom into products, so the extra quality protects the fine detail they inspect.

For product thumbnails, 75 percent quality is enough. The thumbnails are small, the softening is invisible, and the category pages load instantly.

Test the shop on a phone after converting. Mobile shoppers are the majority now, and a fast-loading shop on a slow connection converts far better than a slow one.

Measure the shop speed before and after the switch. A concrete improvement in loading time justifies the conversion work to the whole team.

WebP for Portfolios

A portfolio must look perfect, and WebP delivers that at a smaller size. A photographer's portfolio with twenty images loads in seconds as WebP, instead of minutes as JPG.

Use 85 percent quality for portfolio images. The photos are the product, and the extra quality protects the detail that clients judge you by.

For a portfolio hero image, 80 percent is enough. The image fills the screen, yet the file stays small, and the page loads fast for every visitor.

Keep the JPG originals as well, since some clients ask for them. A client who wants a JPG for a presentation should get one without a fresh conversion.

Update the portfolio regularly, converting new work as you add it. A portfolio that grows with JPGs mixed among WebPs becomes inconsistent, while a uniform WebP set stays fast.

WebP for News Sites

News sites publish dozens of images a day, and the volume makes WebP savings enormous. A single article with five images saves megabytes, and a homepage with fifty saves far more.

Convert news photos to WebP at 80 percent quality. The images look clean, the pages load fast, and readers on mobile connections stay instead of leaving.

For breaking news, speed matters most. A WebP image uploads and loads faster than a JPG, so the story reaches readers sooner, which is the point of news.

Automate the conversion in the publishing workflow if possible. A newsroom that converts automatically never forgets, while a manual step gets skipped under deadline pressure.

Keep a JPG fallback for older readers. The picture element serves WebP to modern browsers and JPG to the rest, so every reader sees the image.

Measuring the Speed Improvement

Measure the page speed before converting, so the improvement is concrete. A tool such as PageSpeed Insights gives a score and a loading time for the page.

Convert the images, then measure again. A page that loaded in four seconds may load in three, which visitors feel immediately.

Check the total page weight as well. A page that weighed 10MB as JPG might weigh 7MB as WebP, which is a visible improvement in the numbers.

Test on a slow mobile connection, not just on fast office wifi. The improvement is largest where it matters most, on the connections that real visitors actually use.

Share the numbers with the team. A concrete speed gain justifies the conversion work and encourages everyone to use WebP for new images too.

WebP for Documentation Sites

Documentation sites are full of screenshots, and WebP keeps them readable at a fraction of the PNG size. A docs site with hundreds of screenshots loads far faster as WebP.

Convert the documentation screenshots to WebP at 85 percent quality. The text stays readable, the files shrink dramatically, and the pages feel noticeably quicker.

For screenshots where every letter must stay perfect, such as API references, consider lossless WebP or PNG instead. Lossy WebP is excellent, but not perfect.

Update the image references after converting, and test the docs site. A broken image reference is worse than a slow one, so check every page after updating it.

Keep the PNG originals in the docs repository as a fallback. If a reader's browser cannot display WebP, the PNG version loads instead, and nobody sees a broken image.

Frequently asked questions

How much smaller is WebP than JPG for the same photo?

Typically 25 to 35 percent smaller at the same visual quality. A 1MB JPG becomes a 650KB to 750KB WebP at 80 percent quality. The saving varies by image: smooth portraits save more, detailed landscapes save less, and already-compressed JPGs may save nothing at all.

Will converting JPG to WebP change my photo dimensions?

No. Conversion keeps the exact pixel width and height of the original JPG. Only the format and file size change. If you also need smaller dimensions, resize the image first with the Image Resizer, then convert the resized copy to WebP.

Can I use WebP images in WordPress?

Yes. WordPress has supported WebP uploads since version 5.8, and converted WebP files work in the media library like any other image. Upload one test image first to confirm it displays correctly, then convert and upload the rest of your batch.

What quality setting should I use for website photos?

80 percent is a good default for most website photos, balancing size and quality well. Use 75 percent for large hero images where loading speed matters most, and 85 percent for product photos that visitors zoom into. Avoid going below 70 percent, where softness becomes visible.

Does converting JPG to WebP work on a phone?

Yes. Open the converter in your phone browser, pick JPGs from your gallery, and download the WebP files. The conversion runs on the phone, and the files download to your device. Keep phone batches modest, since large photos take time to encode on mobile.

Why is my converted WebP larger than the original JPG?

This happens with simple or already-compressed JPGs, where WebP has little left to save, and at very high quality settings such as 95 percent. When the WebP is larger, keep the JPG instead. Check the before-and-after sizes on each card and let the numbers decide.
