JPG vs PNG: When Transparency Matters

JPG vs PNG compared: transparency, file size, text sharpness and photos. Learn when each format wins and why converting cannot add transparency.

JPG and PNG are the two oldest image formats on the web, and they solve opposite problems. JPG makes photos small, while PNG keeps graphics perfect and transparent.

The most misunderstood difference between them is transparency. JPG cannot store it at all, and PNG can, yet converting a JPG to PNG does not magically create it.

This article compares the two formats honestly, so you can pick the right one for each image you handle.

JPG at a Glance

JPG is a lossy format designed for photographs. It discards detail your eyes barely notice, which makes photo files dramatically smaller than any lossless format.

A typical phone photo is 3MB as a JPG and 15MB as a PNG. That gap is why every camera, phone, and website uses JPG for photos.

JPG supports only 8-bit colour and no transparency. Every pixel has a colour, and there is no such thing as a see-through JPG pixel.

The format is universal. Every device, browser, printer, and upload form built in the last thirty years can open a JPG without any extra software.

PNG at a Glance

PNG is a lossless format designed for graphics. It stores every pixel exactly, which keeps sharp edges, flat colours, and text perfectly crisp.

PNG supports transparency through an alpha channel. Each pixel can be fully transparent, fully opaque, or partly transparent, which allows soft edges and shadows.

The price is file size. A detailed photograph stored as PNG is several times larger than the same photo as JPG, because lossless storage cannot discard texture.

PNG is universal too, though less efficient for photos. Every browser, editor, and office app opens PNG files without trouble.

What Transparency Actually Is

Transparency means parts of the image let the background show through. A logo with a transparent background sits cleanly on any colour, because there is no white box around it.

Inside the file, transparency is stored as an alpha channel, which is an extra layer of data saying how see-through each pixel is. A value of zero means invisible, and 255 means fully solid.

Partial transparency allows soft edges. A drop shadow fades gradually from solid to invisible, which looks natural on any background.

JPG has no alpha channel at all. That is a structural fact, not a setting, and no JPG file in the world carries transparency.

When Transparency Actually Matters

Logos are the classic case. A company logo must sit on white pages, coloured websites, and dark slides, and only transparency lets one file work everywhere.

Icons and stickers need it too. An app icon with a white box around it looks broken, while a transparent icon blends into any interface.

Product cutouts for online shops depend on it. A product photo with the background removed sits cleanly on the shop's background, whatever colour that is.

Overlays and watermarks use partial transparency. A semi-transparent logo over a photo lets the photo show through while the brand stays visible.

When Transparency Does Not Matter

Photographs never need transparency. A photo is a rectangle of pixels with no see-through parts, so JPG handles it perfectly.

Documents and scans do not need it either. A scanned page is a full rectangle, and a white background is correct, not a problem to solve.

Social media posts rarely need it. A photo posted to Instagram or Facebook fills its frame, and transparency would be invisible anyway.

If the image has no see-through parts, transparency is irrelevant. Choose the format on file size and sharpness instead.

Why Converting Cannot Add Transparency

A JPG has no transparent pixels, so converting it to PNG produces a PNG with no transparent pixels either. The white background is stored as white pixels, not as see-through ones.

The only way to get transparency is to remove the background properly. Use the background remover tool, which uses AI to cut the subject out and save it as a transparent PNG.

Some people try to delete white pixels in an editor and call that transparency. That works only for simple images, and it leaves ragged edges on anything detailed.

For a clean result, start from a PNG that already has transparency, or remove the background with a proper tool. Conversion alone never creates it.

File Size Compared Honestly

For photos, JPG wins by a large margin. A 3MB JPG photo becomes a 12MB to 18MB PNG, with no visible improvement to justify the extra bytes.

For simple graphics, PNG wins. A logo with flat colours and large empty areas compresses well as PNG, sometimes smaller than a JPG of the same image.

For screenshots with text, the two are close. PNG is larger, but the text stays sharp, while JPG smears the letters, so the extra size buys real quality.

WebP beats both for the web, offering PNG-like transparency at JPG-like sizes. It is the format to choose when the destination supports it.

Text Sharpness and Line Art

PNG keeps text razor sharp because it stores every pixel exactly. A screenshot, a diagram, or a chart looks clean in PNG at any zoom level.

JPG smears text because its compression groups pixels into blocks. Small letters develop halos and soft edges, which makes them harder to read.

Line art, such as graphs and technical drawings, suffers the same way in JPG. Thin lines blur, while PNG keeps them crisp.

For any image where text or lines are the content, PNG is the right format. For any image where the photo is the content, JPG is right.

Photos: JPG Wins, PNG Loses

A photo has millions of colours blending smoothly. JPG handles those gradients efficiently, while PNG must store every subtle variation exactly.

The result is a PNG photo that is several times larger yet looks identical. The extra bytes buy nothing visible, and the slower loading is a real cost.

Editing is the one exception. If you plan to edit a photo many times, PNG stops the damage from accumulating, while repeated JPG saves keep adding damage.

For delivery, though, JPG or WebP is always the better choice for photos. PNG is a working format for photos, not a delivery format.

Choosing for the Web

For website photos, use JPG or WebP. The small file size makes pages load fast, which matters for visitors and for search rankings.

For website logos and icons, use PNG or WebP. Transparency matters here, and both formats preserve it, with WebP usually producing the smaller file.

For website screenshots, use WebP or PNG. The text must stay readable, and JPG smears it, so the lossless option is worth the extra bytes.

Modern websites often serve WebP with a JPG or PNG fallback. That combination gives fast loading to modern browsers and compatibility to older ones.

Choosing for Print

For printed photos, JPG at high quality is fine. Printers handle JPG well, and the file size is manageable for large print jobs.

For printed documents with text, PNG is better. The sharp text prints cleanly, while JPG text can look soft on paper.

For printed logos, PNG with transparency is the standard. The logo sits cleanly on any background colour in the printed design.

Ask the print shop what they prefer. Many accept both formats, and their workflow may favour one over the other for practical reasons.

A Practical Rule to Remember

Ask two questions about every image. Does it have transparency or sharp text? And is it a photograph or a graphic?

Transparency or sharp text means PNG. A photograph without those needs means JPG. A graphic for the web often means WebP, which handles both.

When in doubt, look at the image. If it is a photo of a person or a place, use JPG. If it is a logo, an icon, or a screenshot, use PNG.

That simple rule covers nearly every image you will handle. The exceptions are rare, and they usually involve editing workflows rather than delivery.

Logos on Dark Backgrounds

A logo on a dark background needs transparency, or it sits in a visible white box. A PNG logo with a transparent background blends into the dark design, while a JPG logo cannot.

Design the logo as PNG from the start when it will appear on dark backgrounds. The transparent version works on dark, light, and coloured backgrounds alike, with a single file.

For a website with a dark theme, test the logo on the actual background colour. A logo that looks perfect on white may disappear on black if its colours are too dark.

For slides with dark templates, the same rule applies. A transparent PNG logo sits cleanly on a dark slide, while a JPG logo needs a white box that breaks the design.

Keep a light version and a dark version of the logo if the brand uses both. A white logo for dark backgrounds and a dark logo for light backgrounds cover every situation.

Icons for Apps and Websites

App icons need transparency, since they sit on home screens with wallpapers behind them. A PNG icon with a transparent background blends into any wallpaper, while a JPG icon shows a box.

Website favicons are usually PNG or SVG. A PNG favicon at 32 by 32 pixels is sharp in the browser tab, and the small file size hardly matters.

Icons in an interface need consistent sizing. A set of PNG icons at the same pixel dimensions looks uniform, while mixed sizes look careless.

For a modern website, WebP icons are smaller than PNG icons with the same transparency. Convert the PNG icons to WebP for the live site, and keep the PNG masters for editing.

Test icons on light and dark backgrounds before shipping them. An icon that relies on a white background will vanish on a dark theme, and the fix is a transparent version.

Stickers and Social Graphics

Stickers are transparent PNGs by design. A sticker with a white box around it looks broken in a chat, while a transparent sticker blends into the conversation.

Social graphics with text need PNG sharpness. An announcement graphic saved as PNG reads cleanly, while the JPG version softens the letters.

For a graphic that will be shared widely, provide both formats. A PNG for quality and a JPG for compatibility cover every platform and every recipient.

Design stickers at high resolution, at least 512 by 512 pixels. A small sticker scaled up looks blurry, while a large one scaled down stays sharp.

Check the file size before uploading stickers. Some platforms cap sticker files at a few hundred kilobytes, and a large PNG may need compressing first.

Watermarks and Overlays

A watermark is a semi-transparent logo placed over a photo, and it needs partial transparency to work. PNG stores the alpha values that make the watermark see-through, while JPG cannot.

Place the watermark in a corner where it does not hide the subject. A watermark over a face annoys viewers, while one in a quiet corner protects the image discreetly.

Keep the watermark subtle, at 30 to 50 percent opacity. A faint watermark deters theft without ruining the photo, while a bold one distracts from the image itself.

For a batch of photos, apply the watermark to PNG masters, then export JPG copies for sharing. The PNG masters keep the watermark editable, while the JPGs are the delivery format.

Remove the watermark from images you sell or license. A buyer expects a clean file, and the watermark belongs only on previews and portfolio images.

Choosing a Format for a New Project

Starting a new project is the best time to choose formats deliberately. A clear rule from day one prevents a folder of mixed formats later.

For a website, choose WebP for photos and PNG for logos. That split gives fast loading and sharp graphics, with transparency where it matters.

For a print project, choose PNG for graphics and high-quality JPG for photos. The print shop accepts both, and the combination balances size and sharpness.

For a social media campaign, choose JPG for photos and PNG for graphics with text. The platforms compress everything anyway, so starting from the right format gives the best result.

Write the format rule down and share it with the team. A one-line rule in the project notes prevents the slow drift into mixed formats that plagues every project.

Frequently asked questions

Can a JPG have a transparent background?

No. JPG has no alpha channel, so every pixel has a solid colour and there is no such thing as a see-through JPG pixel. If you need transparency, use PNG or WebP. A JPG logo will always have a solid background, usually white or black.

Does converting JPG to PNG make the white background transparent?

No. The white pixels are real pixels, and PNG stores them as white. Transparency must be created by removing the background with a proper tool, not by converting formats. Conversion preserves the image exactly as it is, including its solid background.

Is PNG or JPG better for a logo on a website?

PNG is better when the logo needs transparency, since it lets the logo sit on any background colour. WebP is even better when the site supports it, because it keeps transparency at a smaller size. JPG is the wrong choice for a logo that needs a transparent background.

Why do screenshots look better as PNG than JPG?

Screenshots are full of sharp text and thin lines, which JPG compression smears. PNG stores every pixel exactly, so the text stays crisp. The PNG file is larger, but for a screenshot the readability is worth the extra bytes.

Can I use PNG for all my website images?

You can, but your pages will load slowly. PNG photos are several times larger than JPG photos with no visible benefit. Use PNG only for logos, icons, and screenshots, and use JPG or WebP for photographs. That split keeps pages fast and images sharp.

What format should I save a transparent logo in for print?

PNG with transparency is the standard for printed logos, since it keeps the edges sharp and the background see-through. Make sure the PNG is high resolution, at least 300 DPI at the printed size, so the logo does not look soft on paper.
