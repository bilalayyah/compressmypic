JPG vs WebP: Size, Quality and Support

JPG vs WebP compared head to head: file size savings, image quality, transparency, browser support, and which format to use where.

JPG has ruled the web for thirty years. WebP is the challenger Google built to replace it, and on pure efficiency, WebP wins. On compatibility, JPG still holds the crown.

The choice between them is not about which format is better in theory. It is about where the image will live, who will view it, and what matters most for that image.

This comparison covers file size, quality, transparency, and support, so you can decide with facts instead of fashion.

JPG at a Glance

JPG is a lossy format from 1992, designed for photographs. It discards detail to shrink files, and it does that job well enough to have survived three decades.

Every device, browser, printer, and upload form on earth can open a JPG. There is no mainstream system that rejects it, and there never has been.

JPG supports only 8-bit colour and no transparency. A JPG is a flat rectangle of pixels, which is simple, reliable, and limiting at the same time.

The format's age shows in its efficiency. Modern formats pack the same quality into fewer bytes, but JPG's universality keeps it relevant.

WebP at a Glance

WebP is a modern format from Google, released in 2010. It supports lossy and lossless compression, transparency, and even animation, all in one format.

Lossy WebP typically beats JPG by 25 to 35 percent in file size at the same visual quality. Lossless WebP beats PNG by a similar margin.

WebP also handles transparency, which JPG cannot do at all. A WebP logo can be see-through, while a JPG logo always has a solid background.

The format is now supported by all major browsers. Chrome, Edge, Firefox, Safari, and Opera all display WebP natively, which covers nearly all web traffic.

File Size: WebP Wins Clearly

A 1MB JPG photo becomes a 650KB to 750KB WebP at the same visual quality. That 25 to 35 percent saving is consistent across most photographs.

The saving is larger for simple images. A screenshot with flat areas can shrink by half as WebP, since the modern encoder handles flat colours efficiently.

The saving is smaller for detailed images. A busy landscape with endless texture leaves less room for improvement, though WebP still wins by a clear margin.

Across a whole website, the savings add up. A page with twenty images might drop from 10MB to 7MB, which is a visible improvement in loading time.

Image Quality: WebP Wins Narrowly

At the same file size, WebP looks better than JPG. Its modern compression preserves edges and textures more faithfully than the ageing JPG standard.

At the same visual quality, WebP is smaller. Either way you measure it, WebP is the more efficient format for photographs.

The difference is subtle at high quality. A JPG at 90 percent and a WebP at 85 percent look identical to most eyes, though the WebP file is smaller.

The difference grows at low quality. A JPG at 60 percent shows blocky artefacts, while a WebP at the same size stays cleaner and more natural.

Transparency: WebP Wins Completely

JPG cannot store transparency at all. Any transparent area must be filled with a solid colour, which puts a visible box around logos on coloured backgrounds.

WebP supports full transparency, including partial transparency for soft edges and shadows. A WebP logo sits cleanly on any background, just like a PNG.

This makes WebP a genuine replacement for PNG on the web, not just for JPG. Logos, icons, and stickers all work as WebP with smaller files than PNG.

For any image that needs transparency, JPG is not an option at all. The comparison is between WebP and PNG, and WebP usually wins on size.

Browser Support: JPG Wins, WebP Catches Up

JPG works in every browser ever made, including Internet Explorer. There is no visitor, however old their software, who cannot see a JPG.

WebP works in all modern browsers. Chrome, Edge, Firefox, Safari 14 and later, and Opera all support it, which covers the overwhelming majority of traffic.

The gap is now tiny. Safari added support in 2020, and the remaining unsupported browsers are old versions with negligible usage.

For the few visitors on ancient browsers, serve a JPG fallback. The picture element in HTML lets you offer WebP first and JPG second, so everyone sees the image.

Support Beyond Browsers

Email clients are split. Some display WebP, others do not, so JPG remains the safe choice for images sent by email.

Printers and photo labs mostly expect JPG or PNG. A WebP file sent to a print shop may be rejected, so convert to JPG for print work.

Desktop photo editors are catching up. Photoshop supports WebP now, but many older editors do not, so JPG is still the safer choice for editing workflows.

Social media platforms convert uploads to their own formats anyway, so the source format matters less there. Upload JPG or WebP, and the platform handles the rest.

When to Use JPG

Use JPG when the destination is unknown. A form, a client, a printer, or an email recipient will always accept JPG, while WebP might be rejected.

Use JPG for email attachments. Email clients display JPG reliably, and WebP support in email is still inconsistent across apps.

Use JPG for print work. Print shops expect JPG or PNG, and a WebP file adds compatibility risk for no benefit on paper.

Use JPG as the fallback format on websites. Serve WebP to modern browsers and JPG to older ones, and every visitor sees the image.

When to Use WebP

Use WebP for website images. The smaller files make pages load faster, which improves the visitor experience and the search ranking.

Use WebP for product photos on an online store. A catalogue with hundreds of images loads far faster when each one is 30 percent smaller.

Use WebP for blog images and screenshots. The text stays sharper than JPG at the same size, and the pages feel noticeably quicker.

Use WebP for logos and icons on the web. Transparency works, the files are smaller than PNG, and every modern browser displays them.

Converting Between JPG and WebP

Converting JPG to WebP is easy and safe at 80 percent quality. The photo keeps its dimensions and looks the same, while the file shrinks by a third.

Converting WebP to JPG is equally easy, using the WebP to JPG converter. The JPG is larger, but it opens everywhere, which is the point of the conversion.

Always convert from the best original you have. A JPG converted to WebP and back to JPG loses a little quality at each step, so avoid round trips.

Keep the JPG originals after converting to WebP. If a destination rejects WebP, the JPGs are ready to upload without converting again.

WebP and Core Web Vitals

Google measures page speed with Core Web Vitals, and image size is a major factor. Large images slow down the Largest Contentful Paint, which is the time until the main content appears.

Switching a site's images from JPG to WebP often improves that measurement noticeably. A page that loaded in four seconds may load in three, which visitors feel.

Smaller images also use less mobile data. Visitors on slow connections notice the difference immediately, and they stay longer on fast pages.

Search rankings reflect this. Google has said page speed affects rankings, so a faster site with WebP images has a small but real advantage.

The Verdict: Which Format Wins?

WebP wins on efficiency, quality, and features. It is smaller, sharper, and more capable, and it is the better format in purely technical terms.

JPG wins on compatibility and simplicity. It opens everywhere, it is understood by every system, and it never surprises you with a rejection.

For the web, use WebP with a JPG fallback. For everything else, use JPG. That combination gives you the best of both formats without the worst of either.

The future belongs to WebP and its successors, but the present still needs JPG. Use each where it shines, and convert between them freely.

JPG and WebP for Email

Email is one place where JPG still wins clearly. Email clients display JPG reliably, while WebP support is inconsistent, and a broken image in an email looks unprofessional.

A newsletter with WebP images may show broken icons to some subscribers. The same newsletter with JPG images displays correctly for everyone, which is the point of the email.

For email attachments, JPG is the safe choice too. The files are small enough to send, and every client on every device can open them without special software.

WebP has no role in email yet. Until clients adopt it consistently, JPG remains the format for newsletters, attachments, and signatures sent by email.

Test every newsletter in several email clients before sending it. A quick test in Gmail, Outlook, and Apple Mail catches the broken images before your subscribers do.

JPG and WebP for Social Media

Social media platforms convert uploads to their own formats, so the source format matters less than the quality. A high-quality input gives a better result regardless of the format.

For a photo post, upload a high-quality JPG. The platform compresses it anyway, and a JPG input is the most compatible starting point across all platforms.

For a graphic with text, upload a PNG or a high-quality WebP. The text stays sharper through the platform's compression, which matters for announcement graphics.

Check the platform's preferred dimensions before uploading. Instagram, Facebook, and X each have ideal sizes, and the right size matters more than the format.

Download the platform's version after uploading and check it. A quick look at the published post confirms the image survived the platform's processing intact.

JPG and WebP for Print

Print shops expect JPG or PNG, and WebP has no role in print work. A WebP file sent to a printer may be rejected outright, or printed with wrong colours.

For printed photos, JPG at 90 percent quality or higher is the standard. The photo prints cleanly, the file is manageable, and the shop's workflow handles it natively.

For printed documents with text, PNG is better than either JPG or WebP. The lossless format keeps every letter edge crisp on paper.

Ask the print shop what they prefer before sending files. Most will say JPG for photos and PNG for graphics, and following their preference avoids a wasted trip.

Order a test print for an important job. A single small print confirms the colours and sharpness before you commit to a large order.

JPG and WebP for Archiving

Archiving needs a format that will open in fifty years, and JPG is the safer bet. It is thirty years old and supported by every system ever built, while WebP is still young.

WebP may become the archival standard one day, but that day has not arrived. A WebP archive depends on future software supporting the format, which is likely but not certain.

For masters, neither format is ideal. A lossless PNG or TIFF master preserves every pixel exactly, while JPG and lossy WebP both discard detail permanently.

Store masters as PNG and delivery copies as WebP or JPG. That split gives you a perfect archive and fast delivery, without compromising either goal.

Review the archive every few years. Formats change, and a master stored in an obsolete format may need migrating before the old format becomes unreadable.

The Format Landscape in Five Years

In five years, WebP will likely be as universal as JPG is today. Browsers already support it, operating systems are catching up, and the remaining gaps are closing.

AVIF may overtake WebP in the same period, since it is even more efficient. Websites that care about speed will adopt AVIF, while WebP becomes the fallback.

JPG will not disappear. It is too deeply embedded in every system, and it will remain the universal fallback for decades, just as it is today.

For website owners, the strategy is clear: serve the newest format with a JPG fallback. That approach works today and will keep working as formats change.

For everyone else, the advice is simpler: when a file will not open, convert it to JPG. That advice has worked for thirty years, and it will work for thirty more.

JPG and WebP for Video Thumbnails

Video thumbnails are small images that must grab attention instantly. A JPG thumbnail at 85 percent quality is sharp, small, and compatible with every platform.

WebP thumbnails are smaller still, which matters when a page shows dozens of them. A video listing page with fifty WebP thumbnails loads far faster than the same page with JPGs.

For a thumbnail with text, such as a title overlay, WebP at 85 percent keeps the letters sharper than JPG. The text is the content of the thumbnail, so its sharpness matters.

Check the platform's thumbnail rules before uploading. YouTube, Vimeo, and other platforms each list preferred sizes, and the right size matters more than the format.

Test the thumbnail at its real display size, not zoomed in. A flaw visible at full size is irrelevant if the thumbnail displays at 200 pixels wide.

Frequently asked questions

Is WebP really better than JPG for websites?

Yes, for most website images. WebP is 25 to 35 percent smaller at the same visual quality, which makes pages load faster and improves Core Web Vitals. The only catch is compatibility with very old browsers, which a JPG fallback solves. For modern websites, WebP is the right choice.

Can I use WebP images in emails?

Not reliably. Some email clients display WebP, but many do not, and a broken image in an email looks unprofessional. Use JPG for email attachments and newsletters, since every client displays it. Save WebP for web pages, where browser support is consistent.

Does WebP support transparency like PNG?

Yes. WebP supports full transparency, including partial transparency for soft edges and shadows, just like PNG. That makes it a genuine replacement for PNG on the web, usually at a much smaller file size. JPG cannot store transparency at all.

Will converting JPG to WebP hurt my photo quality?

Very little, at the right settings. WebP at 80 percent quality usually looks identical to the original JPG while producing a smaller file. The loss is only visible if you zoom in closely or set the quality very low. For web use, the trade is almost always worth it.

Do I need a JPG fallback if I use WebP on my website?

It is good practice, though less necessary than it once was. All modern browsers support WebP, but a JPG fallback costs little and covers the rare visitor on an old browser. The picture element in HTML makes serving both formats straightforward.

Is WebP better than JPG for printing photos?

No. Printers and print shops expect JPG or PNG, and WebP adds compatibility risk for no benefit on paper. For print work, use JPG at 90 percent quality or higher. WebP is a web format, and its advantages disappear the moment the image leaves the screen.
