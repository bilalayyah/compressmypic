PNG to JPG: File Size and Quality Trade-offs

How much smaller does PNG to JPG really get? Real savings by image type, quality settings that protect text, and when the trade is not worth it.

PNG to JPG conversion is famous for shrinking files, but the savings are not the same for every image. A screenshot may drop by 90 percent, while a simple icon may grow instead.

Understanding what drives the savings helps you predict the result before you convert, and pick the quality setting that protects what matters.

This article looks at the numbers honestly, by image type, and explains the trade-offs in plain terms. To see the savings on your own files, try the free PNG to JPG converter, which converts in your browser and shows the size difference before you download.

Why PNG Files Are So Big

PNG is lossless, which means it stores every pixel exactly. That precision costs bytes, especially for detailed images with endless texture and colour variation.

A photograph has millions of subtly different colours. PNG must describe each one, while JPG can simplify them into smooth gradients that the eye barely distinguishes.

Noise is the hidden cost. Camera grain, screen textures, and anti-aliased edges are all random-looking data, and random data compresses poorly in any lossless format.

Screenshots are the worst case. A full-screen capture mixes photos, text, gradients, and interface elements, and PNG stores all of it at full fidelity.

Why JPG Files Are So Much Smaller

JPG is lossy, which means it discards detail to save space. It groups nearby pixels into blocks and simplifies the colour inside each block.

Smooth areas compress extremely well. A blue sky, a white wall, or a blurred background needs very few bytes in JPG, while PNG must still describe every pixel.

The quality setting controls how aggressive the simplification is. At 85 percent, the simplifications are invisible; at 50 percent, they become obvious.

For photographs and screenshots, the trade is excellent. The eye cannot see the discarded detail at normal viewing sizes, yet the file shrinks to a tenth of its size.

Savings by Image Type

Screenshots save the most, typically 80 to 90 percent. A 5MB Retina screenshot becomes a 500KB JPG at 85 percent quality, with the text still readable.

Photographs saved as PNG save 70 to 85 percent. A 12MB PNG photo becomes a 1.5MB to 2MB JPG, which is far easier to email or upload.

Simple graphics save the least, often 30 to 50 percent. A logo with flat colours already compresses well as PNG, so JPG has less left to save.

Tiny monochrome icons can grow instead of shrinking. A 20KB icon may become a 25KB JPG, because JPG adds header overhead that PNG does not need for simple images.

The Quality Slider, in Practical Terms

The slider runs from 20 to 100 percent, and each step changes both size and quality. The relationship is not linear, which surprises many people.

From 100 to 90 percent, the file often halves with no visible change. That is the best trade in the whole range, and it is why 90 is a good starting point.

From 90 to 80 percent, the file halves again, with only a slight softening visible on close inspection. This is the sweet spot for most screenshots and photos.

From 80 to 60 percent, the savings shrink while the damage grows. Text blurs, edges halo, and gradients band, so the trade stops being worth it.

Text Sharpness at Each Quality Level

At 90 percent, screenshot text looks essentially perfect. Letters are crisp, and only a pixel-level comparison shows any difference from the PNG.

At 85 percent, text is still clean at normal reading sizes. Zooming in reveals slight softening around letter edges, but nobody reads a screenshot zoomed in.

At 75 percent, small text starts to suffer. Menu labels and fine print develop halos, and the screenshot feels slightly out of focus.

At 60 percent, text is visibly damaged. Letters bleed into their backgrounds, and the screenshot looks unprofessional, so avoid this level for anything with words.

Photos at Each Quality Level

Photos tolerate lower quality better than screenshots do. A portrait at 80 percent looks clean, since skin and hair hide compression well.

Landscapes are more demanding. Fine detail such as leaves, water, and distant buildings softens visibly below 80 percent, though the overall image still looks fine.

Product photos need care around edges. A sharp product outline against a white background can develop a halo at low quality, which looks cheap on a shop page.

For photos, 80 to 85 percent is the practical floor. Below that, the savings are small and the damage is visible, so the trade is poor.

When the Savings Disappear

Simple graphics with few colours save little. A flat icon or a two-colour diagram already compresses well as PNG, so JPG has nothing left to simplify.

Very small images save little too. A 30KB image has little data to remove, and the JPG headers eat into whatever is saved.

Images with heavy noise save less than expected. Noise looks random to the encoder, and random data resists compression in both formats.

If a converted file is barely smaller than the original, the PNG was already efficient. Keep the PNG and skip the conversion for that image.

Choosing the Right Setting for Your Job

For screenshots going into a document, use 88 to 90 percent. The text stays sharp, the file shrinks by 80 percent, and the document looks professional.

For photos going to a website, use 80 to 85 percent. The images look clean, the pages load fast, and the savings are large.

For images going to a form with a size limit, start at 85 and step down by five points until the file fits. Check the text after each step.

For images going to print, use 92 to 95 percent. Print reveals flaws that screens hide, and the extra size is cheap for a single important image.

Comparing Before and After Properly

Do not judge quality by file size alone. A tiny file is useless if the text is unreadable, and a large file is wasteful if the image looks identical to a smaller one.

Use the Compare viewer to inspect the converted JPG against the original PNG. Zoom into the areas that matter: text, faces, and sharp edges.

Check the image at its real display size, not zoomed in. A flaw visible at 300 percent zoom is irrelevant if the image displays at 600 pixels wide.

Convert at two quality levels and compare them directly. The difference between 85 and 90 percent is often invisible, and the smaller file is the right choice.

Batch Savings Add Up Fast

A single screenshot saving 4MB feels minor. Fifty screenshots saving 4MB each is 200MB, which is the difference between an email that sends and one that bounces.

Websites benefit most from batch savings. A page with twenty PNG screenshots might weigh 60MB, while the JPG versions weigh 8MB, and the page loads in seconds instead of minutes.

Storage benefits too. A photo archive converted from PNG to JPG at 85 percent can shrink by 80 percent, freeing space for years of new photos.

Track the batch summary after each conversion. It totals the savings across all files, and a good batch shows a dramatic number that justifies the effort.

When Not to Convert at All

Do not convert logos that need transparency. A JPG cannot carry transparency, and the matte fill will put a visible box around the logo on coloured backgrounds.

Do not convert images you plan to edit many times. Each JPG save adds damage, while PNG survives repeated editing without loss.

Do not convert tiny icons. The savings are negligible, and the JPG version may be larger than the PNG it replaced.

Do not convert archival masters. Keep the original PNG as the master copy, and convert copies for delivery, so you can always go back to the perfect original.

The WebP Alternative Worth Knowing

WebP often beats JPG for PNG conversions, especially for screenshots. It preserves text better, keeps transparency, and produces smaller files at the same quality.

A PNG screenshot converted to WebP at 85 percent is usually smaller than the JPG version and sharper too. For websites, WebP is the better target.

The catch is compatibility. Some forms, printers, and older programs reject WebP, while JPG works everywhere without question.

When the destination is a website, choose WebP. When the destination is unknown or old, choose JPG. The PNG to WebP converter handles the web case.

Email Attachment Limits

Most email services cap attachments at 20MB to 25MB, and PNG screenshots can exceed that quickly. Five 5MB screenshots total 25MB, which bounces the email back.

Converting those screenshots to JPG at 85 percent quality brings the total to about 4MB. The email sends immediately, and the recipient sees the same images.

For a single large screenshot, the conversion alone usually solves the problem. A 5MB PNG becomes a 500KB JPG, which is far below any attachment limit.

For a very large set, zip the JPGs into a single archive after converting. One attachment is easier to manage than ten, and the recipient downloads everything at once.

Check the total size before sending, not after. A quick glance at the attachment sizes prevents the frustrating bounce message that arrives after you thought the email was sent.

Website Loading Speed

Website speed depends heavily on image size, and PNG photos are among the worst offenders. A page with ten PNG photos might weigh 30MB, while the JPG versions weigh 4MB.

Visitors notice the difference immediately. A page that loads in ten seconds loses readers, while the same page loading in two seconds keeps them.

Search engines notice too. Google measures page speed as part of its ranking, so a faster page with JPG images ranks better than a slow page with PNG photos.

Convert website PNGs to JPG at 80 to 85 percent quality, or to WebP for even smaller files. The images look identical, and the pages feel dramatically faster.

Measure the page speed before and after converting. A tool such as PageSpeed Insights shows the improvement in concrete numbers, which justifies the effort.

Storage on a Full Phone

A phone full of PNG screenshots wastes storage that photos and apps need. Converting the screenshots you must keep, and deleting the rest, frees real space.

Check how much space screenshots use in your phone settings. The number is often surprising, with hundreds of megabytes tied up in images you forgot existed.

Convert the keepers to JPG at 85 percent quality. The text stays readable, the files shrink by 80 percent, and the phone has room to breathe again.

Delete the screenshots you no longer need. Most are temporary, such as a one-time code or a chat message, and keeping them all wastes space for no benefit.

Set a monthly reminder to clean up screenshots. A five-minute cleanup each month prevents the slow accumulation that eventually fills the phone.

Upload Limits on Forms

Upload forms often cap images at 2MB or 5MB, and a PNG screenshot can exceed that easily. A 5MB PNG bounces back, while the 500KB JPG version uploads without trouble.

Convert the image before opening the form. A last-minute conversion under deadline pressure is how mistakes happen, while an early conversion leaves time to check the result.

Check the form's accepted formats as well as its size limit. Some forms list JPG and PNG but reject WebP, so the format matters as much as the size.

If the JPG is still too large, lower the quality to 80 percent and try again. That step often halves the file size with little visible change.

For a form that demands an exact size, use the target file size compressor after converting. Convert first, then compress to the exact limit, in that order.

Balancing Quality Across a Batch

A batch with mixed image types needs a quality setting that suits the weakest file. Screenshots with text need 85 percent or higher, while photos tolerate 80 percent.

When a batch mixes screenshots and photos, choose 85 percent. The screenshots stay readable, the photos look clean, and the files are still far smaller than the PNGs.

For a batch of photos only, 80 percent is the practical choice. The images look identical to the PNGs, and the files are smaller than at 85 percent.

Check the weakest file after converting, not the strongest. A photo that looks fine at 80 percent does not prove that a screenshot with small text will too.

When in doubt, split the batch by type. Two batches with tailored settings beat one batch with a compromise setting, and the extra minute is worth the better result.

The numbers tell a simple story: screenshots and photos shrink dramatically, simple graphics barely shrink, and quality above 85 percent protects text. Frequently asked questions

What quality setting gives the best size-to-quality balance for screenshots?

85 percent is the practical sweet spot for screenshots. Text stays readable, the file shrinks by about 80 percent, and the image looks clean at normal sizes. Dropping to 75 percent saves little extra space but visibly softens small text, so the trade is poor.

Why did my tiny icon get bigger after converting to JPG?

JPG adds header overhead that PNG does not need for simple images, and a flat icon has little detail for JPG to simplify. A 20KB monochrome icon can grow by a few kilobytes as a JPG. For simple graphics, keep the PNG, since the conversion saves nothing.

Is 90 percent quality noticeably better than 80 percent for photos?

Barely, at normal viewing sizes. The file at 90 percent is often twice as large as at 80 percent, yet the two look identical unless you zoom in closely. For web photos, 80 percent is the smarter choice. For print, use 90 percent or higher.

How much can I shrink a folder of PNG screenshots in one batch?

A folder of fifty 5MB screenshots, about 250MB total, typically shrinks to 25MB to 40MB as JPGs at 85 percent quality. That is an 85 percent saving, and it is the difference between a folder that emails easily and one that does not. The batch summary shows the exact total.

Does lowering quality below 70 percent ever make sense?

Rarely, and only when a strict size limit forces it, such as a form that caps uploads at 100KB. Below 70 percent, text blurs and photos look visibly damaged. If a file must be that small, consider reducing the dimensions first, which preserves quality better than crushing the quality slider.

Should I convert PNG to JPG or WebP for my website?

WebP is usually better for websites, since it preserves text better, keeps transparency, and produces smaller files than JPG at the same quality. JPG wins only when the destination is unknown or old. For modern websites, WebP is the right target.
