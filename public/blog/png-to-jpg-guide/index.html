PNG to JPG: Complete Guide

Convert PNG screenshots and photos to JPG and shrink them dramatically. Matte colours, quality settings, batches and best uses explained.

Mac and Windows screenshots save as PNG by default, and those PNGs are enormous. A single screenshot can weigh 5MB, while the same image as a JPG weighs 500KB.

Converting PNG to JPG is the fastest way to shrink screenshots, photos, and graphics that do not need transparency. The quality loss is small, and the size saving is huge.

This guide explains how the conversion works, when to use it, and how to handle the transparency question that trips most people up.

What Is PNG to JPG Conversion?

PNG to JPG conversion reads a PNG file and rewrites it as a JPG. The pixels stay the same, but the storage changes from lossless to lossy compression.

The conversion discards some detail to shrink the file. At sensible quality settings, the loss is invisible, while the size saving is dramatic, often 80 to 90 percent.

The trade is transparency. JPG cannot store see-through pixels, so any transparent areas must be filled with a solid colour, called the matte.

For screenshots, photos, and graphics without transparency, the trade is clearly worth it. For logos with transparency, it needs more thought.

Why Are PNG Screenshots So Huge?

Screenshots save as PNG because the operating system wants perfect quality. Every pixel of the screen is stored exactly, including noise, gradients, and anti-aliased text.

Retina and high-resolution screens make it worse. A screenshot at double resolution has four times the pixels, and PNG must store every one of them.

Photos inside screenshots are the biggest cost. A desktop wallpaper or a photo in a browser window is a detailed image, and PNG stores it losslessly at full size.

JPG handles all of that far more efficiently. It simplifies the texture and gradients, which the eye barely notices, and the file shrinks to a tenth of the size.

Quality Settings That Work

The quality slider runs from 20 to 100 percent. For screenshots with text, 85 to 90 percent keeps the letters crisp while still shrinking the file substantially.

For photographs saved as PNG, 80 to 85 percent is the sweet spot. The photo looks clean, and the file is far smaller than the PNG.

Below 70 percent, text starts to suffer. Small letters develop halos, and thin lines blur, so avoid low settings for screenshots that must stay readable.

For images going to a form with a size limit, lower the quality step by step. Check the size after each step, and stop as soon as the file fits.

The Matte Colour: Filling Transparency

When a PNG has transparent areas, the converter must fill them with a solid colour before writing the JPG. That colour is the matte, and you choose it.

White is the default and the safest choice. Most documents, web pages, and forms sit on white, so a white-filled logo looks natural there.

Black suits dark designs, and a brand colour matches a specific website. The converter lets you pick any hex colour, so you can match a page exactly.

Check the result before downloading. A logo converted with the wrong matte looks broken, with a visible box around it, and the fix is a ten-second colour change.

Screenshots and Text Sharpness

Screenshots are the best use case for PNG to JPG conversion. The size saving is enormous, and the text stays readable at 85 percent quality or higher.

Check small text after converting. The Compare viewer lets you zoom into menus, buttons, and body text to confirm nothing blurred beyond recognition.

For screenshots where every letter must stay perfect, such as legal evidence or technical documentation, consider WebP instead. It preserves text better than JPG at small sizes.

For everyday screenshots shared in chats or emails, JPG at 85 percent is more than good enough, and the smaller file sends faster.

Product Photos, Cutouts, and Logos

Product photos with transparent backgrounds are common in online shops. Converting them to JPG with a white matte makes them ready for marketplaces that demand JPG.

A white matte is the standard for product listings. Amazon, eBay, and most shops expect product photos on white, so the conversion matches their rules.

Logos need more care. A transparent logo converted to JPG with a white matte looks fine on a white page, but wrong on a coloured one. Match the matte to the destination.

For a logo that must work on many backgrounds, do not convert to JPG at all. Keep it as PNG or WebP, where the transparency survives.

Batch Converting Screenshots

You can convert a whole batch of 25 Retina screenshots at once. Each file converts independently, and the ZIP holds them all.

Files up to 25MB each are accepted, which covers even the largest Retina captures without any preparation.

Use the same quality and matte settings for the whole batch. Consistency matters when the screenshots belong to the same document or presentation.

Check the batch summary after converting. It totals the savings across all files, and a good batch often shrinks by 80 percent or more.

How Much Size Can You Save?

A typical 5MB PNG screenshot becomes a 400KB to 800KB JPG at 85 percent quality. That is an 85 percent saving, with the image still looking clean.

A detailed photo saved as PNG might drop from 12MB to 1.5MB. The saving is smaller in percentage terms, but still dramatic in absolute bytes.

Simple graphics save the least. A flat icon with few colours may only shrink by half, since PNG already compresses simple images well.

Tiny monochrome icons can even grow slightly. A 20KB flat icon converted to JPG at 88 percent may gain a few kilobytes, because JPG adds overhead that PNG does not need.

PNG to JPG or PNG to WebP?

Choose JPG when the destination demands it. Forms, older software, and print shops often list JPG specifically, and WebP will be rejected.

Choose WebP when the destination is a website. WebP keeps transparency, preserves text better, and produces smaller files than JPG at the same quality.

Choose JPG when the image has no transparency and must work everywhere. It is the universal format, and no system built in the last thirty years rejects it.

For screenshots going to the web, WebP is usually the better choice. For screenshots going to a form or an email, JPG is the safer one.

Metadata Stripped During Conversion

Converting PNG to JPG strips embedded PNG text chunks, such as creation software notes and comments. The canvas redraws the pixels, and the metadata does not survive.

That is good for privacy. A screenshot shared as JPG carries no record of the software that created it or the machine it came from.

It is worth knowing for archival work, though. If the PNG carried important notes in its text chunks, copy them before converting, since the JPG will not carry them.

EXIF data is stripped too, though PNGs rarely carry as much of it as JPGs do. Either way, the converted file is clean of hidden details.

Checking Quality Before Downloading

Use the Compare viewer to inspect the converted JPG against the original PNG. Drag the split handle across the image and look at edges and text.

Soft drop shadows around window captures deserve a close look. They flatten during conversion, and a low quality setting can turn a soft shadow into a blotchy one.

Check the file size as well. A JPG that is barely smaller than the PNG suggests the quality is set too high, or the image is a simple graphic that PNG already handled well.

If the result looks soft, raise the quality by five points and convert again. The original PNG stays in the queue, so you never need to re-upload.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

The most common mistake is converting a transparent logo to JPG without setting the matte colour. The default white may be wrong for a dark page, and the logo ends up in a white box.

Another mistake is using too low a quality for screenshots. Text suffers first, so keep the quality at 85 percent or higher whenever letters must stay readable.

People also convert PNGs that are already small. A 30KB icon does not need converting, and the JPG version may be larger, not smaller.

Finally, do not convert a PNG to JPG and then back to PNG expecting to save space. The round trip adds JPG damage and produces a larger PNG than the original.

PNG Photos from Phones

Some phones save photos as PNG, either by setting or by app choice. Those photos are far larger than JPG photos, and converting them saves real storage space.

A phone photo saved as PNG can weigh 10MB or more, while the same photo as JPG weighs 2MB. The difference is invisible on the phone screen, yet the storage saving is dramatic.

Convert phone PNGs to JPG at 85 percent quality for sharing. The photos look identical, the files send faster, and the recipients can open them anywhere.

For photos you plan to edit, keep a PNG copy as the master. The lossless format survives repeated edits, while each JPG save adds a little damage.

Check your camera settings if your phone keeps producing PNGs. Switching the camera to JPG saves storage automatically, without any conversion step at all.

Screenshots for Bug Reports

A bug report needs a screenshot that shows the problem clearly. A PNG screenshot is perfect for evidence, while a JPG screenshot may blur the very detail that proves the bug.

Take the screenshot at full resolution, and convert it to JPG at 90 percent quality if the bug tracker caps file sizes. The text stays readable, and the file uploads without trouble.

Annotate the screenshot before converting, circling the problem area. An annotation on the PNG version stays crisp, while the same annotation on a JPG softens with each save.

Include the steps to reproduce the bug alongside the screenshot. A clear image plus clear steps helps the developer fix the problem far faster than either alone.

Keep the original PNG screenshot after converting. If the developer asks for a sharper version, the PNG is ready, and you do not need to reproduce the bug again.

Sharing Screenshots in Chats

Screenshots shared in chats are often PNGs, and they arrive slowly on weak connections. Converting them to JPG first makes them send faster and load quicker for everyone.

For a quick share, paste the screenshot into the converter and download the JPG in seconds. The extra step is worth it when the chat group is on slow mobile data.

Keep the quality at 85 percent for chat screenshots. The text stays readable, the file is a fraction of the PNG size, and the message sends immediately.

For a screenshot that must stay pixel-perfect, such as a payment confirmation, send the PNG instead. The larger file is worth it when every digit must be legible.

Messaging apps compress images anyway, so a JPG input gives a better result than a PNG input. The app's compression has less work to do, and the final image looks cleaner.

Converting Images for Email

Email attachments have size limits, usually 20MB to 25MB, and PNG screenshots can exceed them quickly. Converting to JPG first keeps the email under the limit.

For a single screenshot, convert it to JPG at 85 percent quality and attach the result. The file is a fraction of the PNG size, and the recipient sees the same image.

For several screenshots, convert the batch and attach the JPGs together. A set of five PNG screenshots might be 25MB, while the JPG versions total 4MB.

For a very large set, zip the JPGs into a single archive. One attachment is easier to manage than ten, and the recipient downloads everything at once.

Check the total size before sending. A quick glance at the attachment sizes prevents the frustrating bounce message that arrives after you thought the email was sent.

Archiving Converted JPGs

Converted JPGs deserve the same care as any archive. Store them in folders organised by date and purpose, so a file from two years ago is still findable.

Keep the original PNGs in a separate archive folder. They are the masters, and a clear separation prevents the two sets from getting mixed up.

Name the converted files by purpose, not by date. A name such as bug-report-login-error.jpg is useful years later, while a name such as screenshot-2026-11-17.jpg is not.

Back up the archive in two places. A local drive and a cloud backup protect against hardware failure, theft, and accidental deletion.

Review the archive every year or two. Old screenshots and photos that no longer matter can be deleted, freeing space for the files that do.

Frequently asked questions

How much smaller will my PNG become as a JPG?

Most screenshots shrink by 80 to 90 percent, for example from 5MB to 500KB at 85 percent quality. Detailed photos save less in percentage terms but still shrink dramatically. Simple icons may barely shrink at all, and tiny monochrome icons can even grow slightly.

Will converting PNG to JPG blur the text in my screenshot?

Slightly, at low quality, but not at 85 percent or higher. Small text is the first thing to suffer from JPG compression, so keep the quality high for screenshots that must stay readable. Use the Compare viewer to zoom into the text before downloading.

What matte colour should I use for a transparent logo?

Match the background where the logo will appear. White suits most documents and web pages, black suits dark designs, and a brand hex colour matches a specific site exactly. Check the converted logo on its destination background before using it.

Can I convert PNG to JPG on my phone?

Yes. Open the tool in your phone browser, pick PNG screenshots from your gallery, and download the JPGs. The conversion runs on the phone, and the files download to your device. Keep phone batches modest, since large PNGs take time to encode on mobile.

Does PNG to JPG conversion work for scanned documents?

Yes, and it is a good way to shrink large scans for emailing. A scanned page saved as PNG can be several megabytes, while the JPG version is a fraction of that. Keep the quality at 85 percent or higher so the text stays readable.

Should I convert all my PNG files to JPG to save space?

No. Convert screenshots, photos, and graphics without transparency, but keep logos, icons, and images that need transparency as PNG or WebP. Converting everything to JPG destroys transparency and can make simple graphics larger, not smaller.
