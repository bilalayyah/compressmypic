PNG to WebP: Complete Guide

Convert PNG images to WebP for smaller files with transparency kept. Logos, icons, screenshots and hero images — settings and best uses.

PNG is the format of logos, icons, and screenshots, but it is also a heavy format. A PNG hero image can weigh several megabytes, while the same image as WebP weighs a fraction of that.

Converting PNG to WebP keeps the transparency that makes PNG useful, while shrinking the file dramatically. It is the best of both formats in one conversion.

This guide explains how the conversion works, which settings to use, and when WebP is the right target for your PNG files.

What Is PNG to WebP Conversion?

PNG to WebP conversion reads a PNG file and rewrites it as a WebP. The pixels stay the same, including any transparent areas, but the storage becomes far more efficient.

WebP supports both lossy and lossless compression. The converter uses lossy WebP by default, which shrinks files dramatically while keeping them looking clean.

Transparency survives the conversion. A transparent logo stays transparent, and soft shadows keep their gradual fade, which is the key advantage over converting to JPG.

The result is a file that works on modern websites, loads faster, and looks identical to the PNG it came from.

How Much Smaller Is WebP Than PNG?

For photographs saved as PNG, WebP is typically 60 to 80 percent smaller. A 5MB PNG photo becomes a 1MB to 2MB WebP at 85 percent quality.

For logos and icons, the saving is smaller but still real, often 25 to 50 percent. Simple graphics already compress well as PNG, so WebP has less left to save.

For screenshots with text, WebP saves 50 to 70 percent while keeping the text readable. That combination makes it ideal for blog images and documentation.

Lossless WebP saves less than lossy WebP, but still beats PNG by 20 to 30 percent. When every pixel must be perfect, lossless WebP is the smallest exact copy available.

Transparency Is Preserved

WebP stores an alpha channel just like PNG does. Transparent areas stay transparent, and partially transparent pixels keep their exact level of see-through.

Soft shadows survive the conversion. A drop shadow that fades gradually from solid to invisible keeps its smooth gradient in WebP, which JPG could never do.

Check the result with the Compare viewer before downloading. Zoom into the edges of a logo and confirm the transparency looks clean, with no halos or fringes.

For logos that must work on many backgrounds, WebP is the best web format. It keeps transparency at a smaller size than PNG, and every modern browser displays it.

Quality Settings That Work

For transparent PNG logos, 85 to 90 percent quality is the sweet spot. The logo stays crisp, the edges stay clean, and the file is far smaller than the PNG.

For PNG screenshots with text, 85 percent keeps the letters readable while shrinking the file substantially. Lower settings soften small text, so avoid them for documentation.

For PNG photographs, 80 to 85 percent is the right range. The photo looks clean, and the file is a fraction of the PNG size.

For hero images on a website, 80 percent balances size and quality well. The image fills the screen, yet the file stays small enough for fast loading.

Converting Whole Icon Sets

An icon set of fifty PNG files converts to WebP in one batch. Each file converts independently, and the ZIP holds them all ready to upload.

Use the same quality setting for the whole set. Consistency matters when icons appear together, since mixed quality looks uneven.

Check two or three icons after converting, rather than all fifty. If those look good, the rest will too, since the settings are the same.

Files up to 25MB each are accepted, which covers even the largest icon files and high-resolution product renders without any preparation.

Screenshots with Text

WebP preserves text better than JPG at the same file size. A screenshot converted to WebP at 85 percent keeps its letters readable, while the JPG version softens them.

That makes WebP the better choice for blog screenshots, documentation images, and tutorial graphics, where text is the content.

For screenshots where every letter must stay perfect, such as legal evidence, consider lossless WebP or PNG instead. Lossy WebP is excellent, but not perfect.

Check the smallest text with the Compare viewer before downloading. Zoom into menus and body text and confirm they read cleanly.

Hero Images for Websites

A hero image is the large banner at the top of a web page, and it is often the heaviest image on the site. Converting it from PNG to WebP can cut the page weight dramatically.

WebP is better than PNG for hero images because it is smaller at the same quality. A 4MB PNG hero becomes a 1MB WebP, and the page loads seconds faster.

WebP is better than JPG for hero images when transparency matters. A hero with a transparent overlay or a cutout product needs the alpha channel that JPG cannot provide.

For a hero image with no transparency, either WebP or JPG works. WebP is smaller, while JPG is more compatible, so choose based on your audience.

Metadata and Colour Profiles

Converting PNG to WebP strips PNG text chunks, such as creation software notes and comments. The canvas redraws the pixels, and the metadata does not survive.

That is good for privacy. A PNG shared as WebP carries no record of the software that created it or the machine it came from.

Colour profiles are a subtle point. The converter may not preserve the PNG colour profile exactly, so check saturated colours against the original before using the result.

For most web images, the colour shift is invisible. For colour-critical work such as product photos, compare the WebP against the PNG carefully.

When WebP Comes Out Larger

Occasionally, a converted WebP is larger than the original PNG. That happens with very simple images, where PNG already compresses the flat colours efficiently.

A tiny icon with two colours may grow slightly as WebP, since the format adds header overhead that PNG does not need for simple images.

When the WebP is larger, keep the PNG. There is no rule that says WebP must always win, and the goal is smaller files, not a specific format.

Check the before-and-after sizes on each card. The converter shows both numbers, so the decision is obvious at a glance.

PNG to WebP or PNG to JPG?

Choose WebP when the image has transparency or will live on a website. WebP keeps the alpha channel and produces smaller files than JPG at the same quality.

Choose JPG when the destination demands it. Forms, printers, and older programs often list JPG specifically and reject WebP.

Choose WebP for screenshots going to the web. The text stays sharper than JPG, and the files are smaller, which makes pages load faster.

Choose JPG for screenshots going to a form or an email. Compatibility matters more than size there, and JPG opens everywhere.

Checking Quality Before Downloading

Click Compare on any card to see the PNG and the WebP side by side. Drag the split handle across the image to inspect transparency, text, and edges.

Check the transparent areas first. A logo edge with a halo or a fringe means the quality was too low, and raising it by five points usually fixes it.

You can change the quality after the first conversion without re-uploading. The original PNG stays in the queue, so try a different setting and compare again.

For a batch, check three or four representative files rather than every one. If those look good, the rest will too, since the settings are the same.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

The most common mistake is using too low a quality for logos. Transparent edges suffer first, so keep the quality at 85 percent or higher for any logo.

Another mistake is converting tiny icons that are already small. A 10KB PNG icon gains nothing from conversion, and the WebP version may be larger.

People also forget to check the colour profile. A saturated product photo may shift slightly during conversion, and a quick comparison catches it before the image goes live.

Finally, do not convert a PNG to WebP and then back to PNG expecting to save space. The round trip adds lossy damage and produces a worse file than the original.

WebP for Website Logos

A website logo as WebP loads faster than the same logo as PNG, while keeping its transparency. The saving is modest per logo, but a site with many icons and logos adds up quickly.

Convert the logo to WebP at 90 percent quality. The edges stay crisp, the transparency survives, and the file is a quarter to a half smaller than the PNG.

Check the logo on light and dark backgrounds after converting. A logo that looks perfect on white may show a faint fringe on black if the quality was too low.

Keep the PNG original as a fallback for older browsers. The picture element serves WebP to modern browsers and PNG to the rest, so every visitor sees the logo.

For a logo that appears on every page, the speed saving matters most. A header logo loads on each page view, so a smaller file improves every visit, not just the first.

WebP for App Icons

App icons appear at many sizes, from a tiny favicon to a large store listing. WebP keeps them sharp at every size while using less storage than PNG.

Convert the icon set to WebP at 90 percent quality. The transparency survives, the edges stay clean, and the files are smaller than the PNG originals.

Test the icons on light and dark backgrounds before shipping them. An icon that relies on a white background will vanish on a dark theme, and the fix is a transparent version.

For an app store listing, check the platform's format rules first. Some stores accept WebP, while others demand PNG, and the format must match the store's list.

Keep the PNG masters in the project folder after converting. If a store or a platform rejects WebP, the PNGs are ready without a fresh conversion.

WebP for Blog Images

Blog images are the classic WebP use case. A blog post with ten photos loads far faster as WebP, and readers stay longer on fast pages.

Convert the blog's PNG photos to WebP at 80 percent quality. The images look identical, the files shrink by two thirds, and the pages feel noticeably quicker.

For blog screenshots with text, WebP at 85 percent keeps the letters readable. The text stays sharper than JPG at the same size, which matters for tutorial images.

Update the image references after converting, and test the post. A broken image reference is worse than a slow one, so check every image on the page.

Keep the PNG originals in the media library as a fallback. If a reader's browser cannot display WebP, the PNG version loads instead, and nobody sees a broken image.

WebP for E-commerce

An online shop lives or dies by its images. Hundreds of product photos, each far smaller as WebP, make the difference between a fast shop and a slow one.

Convert product photos to WebP at 80 to 85 percent quality. Customers zoom into products, so the extra quality protects the fine detail they inspect.

For product thumbnails, 75 percent quality is enough. The thumbnails are small, the softening is invisible, and the category pages load instantly.

Test the shop on a phone after converting. Mobile shoppers are the majority now, and a fast-loading shop on a slow connection converts far better than a slow one.

Measure the shop speed before and after the switch. A concrete improvement in loading time justifies the conversion work to the whole team.

WebP for Social Media Graphics

Social media graphics often contain text, and WebP keeps that text sharper than JPG at the same file size. An announcement graphic as WebP reads cleanly after the platform's compression.

Convert the graphic to WebP at 85 percent quality before uploading. The platform compresses it anyway, and a high-quality input gives a better result than a low-quality one.

Check the platform's preferred dimensions before converting. Instagram, Facebook, and X each have ideal sizes, and the right size matters more than the format.

For a graphic with transparency, such as a sticker or an overlay, WebP is the only format that keeps it. JPG cannot store transparency, so the graphic would gain a solid background.

Download the platform's version after uploading and check it. A quick look at the published post confirms the image survived the platform's processing intact.
