Round Image Corners: Complete Guide

Learn how to round image corners online: corner radius, per-corner control, circle and pill shapes, transparent vs solid fills, and formats.

Rounded corners soften a photo. They turn a sharp rectangle into a friendly shape that fits modern screens, cards, and avatars.

Every app and website uses rounded corners now. Profile pictures are circles, cards have soft edges, and buttons are pill-shaped, so photos with rounded corners feel at home.

This guide explains how corner rounding works, which shapes to choose, and how to export the result correctly. It covers radius, per-corner control, transparency, and formats. The free Round Image Corners tool does the rounding for you: set a radius, preview the curve, and export with transparent corners.

What Rounding Corners Actually Does

Rounding cuts away the corners of an image along a curve. The straight edges stay, but the sharp 90 degree corners become smooth arcs.

The curve is set by a radius value. A small radius gives a gentle softening, while a large radius turns the corners into deep curves that change the whole shape.

Rounding does not change the pixels inside the image. It only removes the corner areas, so the photo itself stays exactly as sharp as the original.

What changes is the shape of the file. A rounded image is no longer a rectangle, and the corners must be either transparent or filled with a solid colour.

Corner Radius Explained

The radius is measured in pixels, and the tool shows a live readout of the value as you adjust it. A larger radius means a rounder corner.

A small radius of 10 to 20 pixels softens the corners just enough to notice. It suits cards, thumbnails, and screenshots, where the rounding should be subtle.

A medium radius of 50 to 100 pixels gives a clearly rounded look. It suits avatars, social posts, and app graphics, where the shape is part of the design.

A very large radius turns the image into a circle or a pill. That is a deliberate shape change, not a subtle softening, and it suits profile pictures and badges.

Per-Corner Control: Rounding One Corner at a Time

Most images need all four corners rounded equally, but some designs need only one or two. A blog card, for example, may have rounded top corners and square bottom ones.

Per-corner mode lets you set a different radius for each corner. You can round the top two and leave the bottom two square, or round a single corner for a special effect.

Turning per-corner mode off returns to a single radius for all corners. The individual values are kept while the mode is off, so you can switch back without losing your settings.

Use per-corner control sparingly. A design with four different radii looks accidental, while one or two rounded corners with the rest square looks deliberate.

Full Circle and Pill Shapes

The Full Circle shortcut turns a square image into a perfect circle. It sets the radius to half the image size, so the corners curve all the way around.

On a rectangular image, the same shortcut creates a pill shape: a rectangle with fully rounded ends. Pills suit buttons, badges, and banners.

For a clean circle, start with a square photo. Crop the image to 1:1 first, then apply the full circle shortcut, and the result is a perfect round avatar.

Check the result on a rectangular photo before you commit. A pill shape can look elegant, but it can also crop away important content at the sides.

Transparent vs Solid Corner Fill

When corners are cut away, the empty space must be filled with something. The tool offers two choices: transparent, which leaves the corners see-through, or a solid colour.

Transparent corners are the right choice for web graphics, avatars, and overlays. The image blends with any background, because the corners show whatever is behind the image.

Solid fill is the right choice when the destination cannot handle transparency. A solid colour, such as white, fills the corners so the image still looks like a rectangle with rounded photo edges.

Choosing transparent while JPG is selected switches the format automatically. JPG has no transparency, so the tool moves you to PNG or WebP to keep the corners see-through.

Formats: PNG, WebP, and JPG

PNG and WebP support transparency, so they keep rounded corners see-through. They are the right formats for avatars, logos, and any graphic that sits on a coloured background.

JPG has no transparency, so rounded corners exported as JPG get a solid fill. The corners become a solid colour, usually white, which looks fine on a white page but wrong on a coloured one.

For web graphics, WebP is often the best choice. It gives smaller files than PNG with the same transparency, though a few older systems still reject the format.

For the sharpest result, choose PNG. It is lossless, so the curved edges stay smooth, and it works everywhere, including old browsers and messaging apps.

Rounding for Avatars and Profile Pictures

Avatars are almost always round or rounded. A square photo with rounded corners feels modern, while a sharp-cornered square feels like a raw file.

Crop the photo to a square first, then round the corners. A 1:1 crop with a large radius gives a clean circle, which is the standard avatar shape.

Keep the face well inside the frame. The corners are cut away, so a face near the edge may lose an ear or part of the hair to the curve.

Export avatars as PNG or WebP. The transparency keeps the corners see-through, so the avatar blends with any profile background, light or dark.

Rounding for Cards and UI Graphics

Cards are the building blocks of modern interfaces, and they almost always have rounded corners. A photo with matching rounded corners fits neatly inside a card.

Match the photo radius to the card radius. A photo with a 16 pixel radius inside a card with a 16 pixel radius looks seamless, while mismatched radii look careless.

For a blog card, round the top two corners and leave the bottom two square. The photo sits at the top of the card, and the square bottom aligns with the text below.

Keep the radius consistent across all cards in a set. A grid of cards with matching corners looks professional, while mixed radii look random.

Why Curved Edges Look Smooth, Not Jagged

A curved edge on a screen is made of square pixels, so it should look jagged. In practice it looks smooth, because the tool blends the edge pixels with the background colour.

This blending is called anti-aliasing. The pixels along the curve are partially transparent, which fools the eye into seeing a smooth line instead of a staircase.

Anti-aliasing works best on a transparent background. The blended pixels fade into whatever sits behind the image, so the curve looks clean on any colour.

On a solid fill, the blend is less flexible. The edge pixels mix with the fill colour, so the curve looks smooth on that colour but rough on any other.

Rounding Corners for Print

Printed photos rarely have rounded corners, because cutting a print to a curve is extra work. A digital rounded image printed on rectangular paper will show the corners as a solid fill.

If you print a rounded image, choose a solid fill that matches the paper. A white fill on white paper looks like a rounded photo, while a transparent export printed as JPG gets a white fill anyway.

For stickers and labels, rounded corners are the point. A sticker with rounded corners peels cleanly and looks finished, while a sharp-cornered sticker can catch and lift.

Check the print size before you round. A small radius on a large print may be invisible, while a large radius on a small print can dominate the shape.

Rounding vs Cropping: Different Jobs

Cropping removes pixels from the edges to change the framing, while rounding removes only the corners to change the shape. They solve different problems.

Crop when the framing is wrong: too much sky, a stranger at the edge, or a subject that is too small. Rounding cannot fix framing.

Round when the shape is wrong: a sharp rectangle that feels harsh in a modern interface. Cropping cannot soften a corner.

Use both together when a photo needs framing and shaping. Crop first to fix the composition, then round the corners of the cropped result to fit its destination.

Rounding Corners and Adding Borders

Rounded corners and borders work well together. A thin border with a matching radius creates a card look that suits social posts, presentations, and product images.

Match the border radius to the corner radius. A border with a 16 pixel radius around a photo with a 16 pixel radius looks seamless, while mismatched radii look careless.

Round the corners first, then add the border. The border tool reads the shape of the image, so rounding first keeps the frame aligned with the curves.

For a Polaroid style, keep the corners square. The classic instant film look has sharp corners, and rounding them breaks the retro feel.

Rounding Corners on a Phone or Tablet

The tool works on touchscreens, so you can round a photo on the phone that took it. Upload from your gallery, drag the radius slider, and download the result.

Use the Full Circle shortcut for avatars on a small screen. One tap turns a square photo into a circle, which is faster than dragging the slider to the exact value.

Check the preview at full size before downloading. A radius that looks subtle on a small phone screen may be more obvious when viewed on a computer.

Downloaded files save to your usual downloads folder. From there you can set the rounded image as a profile picture or share it directly.

Privacy: Rounding Without Uploading

All corner rounding happens in your browser. Your photos are never uploaded to a server, which matters when the images are personal portraits or client work.

This is a real advantage over cloud editors. A profile photo or a family portrait should not travel to a server you cannot see, just to soften its corners.

The tool also strips EXIF GPS data on export. A rounded photo downloaded from the tool carries no record of where it was taken.

Keep the original photo without rounding. Once the corners are cut away in an export, they cannot be restored from that file, so the original is your only full copy.

Common Mistakes When Rounding Corners

The most common mistake is exporting as JPG and expecting transparency. JPG has no transparency, so the corners become a solid fill, which looks wrong on a coloured background.

Another mistake is a radius that is too large for the image. A small photo with a huge radius becomes a circle when a gentle rounding was intended.

People also forget to crop to a square before making a circle. A rectangular photo with the full circle shortcut becomes a pill, not a circle, which may not fit the avatar slot.

Finally, do not round the same JPG repeatedly. Each save adds damage, so always return to the original file when you want a different radius.

A Workflow That Keeps Rounding Clean

Start with the original file and decide the destination first. An avatar, a card, and a sticker need different shapes, so the radius comes before the rounding.

Crop to the right shape before you round. A square crop for a circle, a wide crop for a pill, and a card crop for a blog image all start with the cropper.

Preview the rounded result at full size before downloading. Zoom into the corners and confirm the curves are smooth and nothing important is cut away.

Save the rounded file with a clear name, such as avatar-circle.png. You may need the same shape again, and a clear name saves you from re-doing the work.

Rounding Corners for Stickers and Labels

Stickers and labels look best with rounded corners. A round-cornered sticker peels cleanly and feels finished, while a sharp-cornered one can catch and lift at the edges.

Use a medium radius for stickers, large enough to see but small enough to keep the design intact. A radius that is too large can cut into the text or the artwork.

Export stickers as PNG or WebP to keep the corners transparent. The sticker can then sit on any background, from a laptop lid to a notebook cover, without a white box.

Test the sticker at its real size before printing. A radius that looks fine on screen may be invisible on a small sticker, or may dominate a large one.

Frequently asked questions

Why do my rounded corners show a white background instead of transparency?

You probably exported as JPG, which has no transparency. JPG fills the cut-away corners with a solid colour, usually white. Export as PNG or WebP instead, which both keep the corners see-through on any background.

How do I make a perfect circle from my photo?

Crop the photo to a square first, then use the Full Circle shortcut, which sets the radius to half the image size. Export as PNG or WebP to keep the corners transparent. A rectangular photo becomes a pill shape, not a circle.

Can I round only the top two corners of an image?

Yes. Turn on per-corner mode and set a radius for the top two corners while leaving the bottom two at zero. This suits blog cards, where the photo sits at the top and the square bottom aligns with the text below.

What radius should I use for a profile picture?

Use the Full Circle shortcut for a round avatar, or a large radius of 50 to 100 pixels for a softly rounded square. Crop to 1:1 first and keep the face well inside the frame, since the corners are cut away.

Does rounding corners reduce image quality?

No, rounding only removes the corner areas and leaves the rest of the pixels untouched. The quality of the kept area stays the same as the original. Export as PNG for a lossless result, or JPG at a high quality setting for photos.

Can I round the corners of a screenshot?

Yes. Paste the screenshot with Ctrl+V, set a small radius of 10 to 20 pixels for a subtle softening, and export as PNG. The rounded screenshot looks like a modern app card and blends well into guides and presentations.
