How to Reduce Image Size to 100KB for Websites and Email

By Bilal

A 4 MB photo slows down your website and clogs up an email. Shrinking it to about 100KB makes pages load fast and emails send instantly — and if you do it right, nobody can tell the difference.

Why 100KB Is the Magic Number

100KB is the size most people aim for because it sits in the sweet spot: small enough to load instantly on a website or slip into any email, but large enough to still look sharp on a screen. A typical phone photo starts at 3–5 MB, so reaching 100KB means cutting away roughly 97% of the file — and modern compression does that mostly by removing data your eyes cannot see.

One honest note before you start: CompressMyPic does not have a box where you type in "100KB" and get exactly that size. No honest tool can promise an exact number, because every photo compresses differently. Instead, the tool shows you the live compressed size after every change, and you steer it to 100KB yourself with the quality slider. This guide shows you exactly how.

Step-by-Step: Reduce an Image to 100KB

  1. 1
    Open CompressMyPic

    Go to compressmypic.online in your browser. There is nothing to install and no account to create — the tool is free, with no signup and no watermark.

  2. 2
    Upload your image

    Drag and drop your image onto the page, or tap "Browse Files" to pick it from your device. JPG, PNG and WebP are supported, up to 20 MB per image. You can upload several images at once.

  3. 3
    Start with the Balanced preset

    The Balanced preset sets the quality to around 70%. For most photos this already cuts the file to a fraction of its original size — check the live size shown next to your image to see where you stand.

  4. 4
    Lower the quality slider step by step

    There is no box where you type "100KB" — instead, drag the quality slider down slowly and watch the live before-and-after size update after every change. Move in small steps (for example 70% to 60% to 50%) so you do not overshoot and lose more quality than needed.

  5. 5
    Switch to WebP if you are still over 100KB

    If the file is still above 100KB at a reasonable quality, change the output format to WebP. WebP is typically 25–35% smaller than JPG at the same visible quality, and it often closes the gap to 100KB without dropping the quality further.

  6. 6
    Compare before and after

    Open the before-and-after comparison and zoom in on faces, text and fine detail. If the picture still looks good, you are done. If it looks blocky or washed out, raise the quality a little and accept a file slightly above 100KB — a sharp 120 KB photo is better than a blurry 100 KB one.

  7. 7
    Download your image

    Tap Download to save the smaller file, or use "Download All (ZIP)" if you reduced several images at once. Your original image is never changed — you always get a new, smaller copy.

Common Targets at a Glance

100KB is not the only target people need. The same method works for any size — here is what to expect for each one:

20KB25–40% + WebPIcons, tiny previews

These are starting points, not promises. A simple photo reaches 100KB easily; a busy, detailed photo needs a lower quality setting. Trust the live size number, not the percentage.

Tips for Websites and Email

  • For websites, use WebP output — it is the fastest way to reach 100KB, and every modern browser supports it.
  • For email, JPG is the safest format because every email app opens it. A 100KB JPG attaches instantly and never bounces for size.
  • Compress all the photos for one page or one email together, then download them all with the Download All (ZIP) button.
  • Do not chase an exact 100KB. A sharp photo at 115KB is better than a blurry one at exactly 100KB — the difference in loading time is nothing.
  • Always check the before-and-after comparison at full zoom before you publish or send the photo.

When 100KB Is Not Possible

Some images simply cannot reach 100KB without looking bad: a very large, very detailed photo, or a PNG with transparency that must stay PNG. In that case, do not force it. A sharp 180KB photo beats a ruined 100KB one every time — for a website it still loads fast, and for email it is still far below any attachment limit. The goal is a small file that still looks good, not a magic number.
